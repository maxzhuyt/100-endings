# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## I.

The form required the applicant's species designation in triplicate, a census figure current within one standard galactic rotation, and a brief statement (not to exceed 140,000 characters, Auditor Standard Notation) explaining why the species in question merited continued allocation of existential resources. Miriam Fontaine-Park had gotten very good at the brief statement. Her six thousand two hundred and eleventh was, by her private estimation, her finest: concise, grammatically flawless in three of the four required syntactic registers, and filed a full two cycles before the deadline.

She aligned the edges of the printout against the lip of her desk. She ran a thumbnail along the fold. She placed it in the outgoing docket, which was a repurposed mail sorter she'd salvaged from B-Deck storage three years ago when the original docket had been requisitioned for scrap metal, and she noted the time—0914 shipboard, which corresponded to nothing in particular, since there was no sun to orient by and hadn't been for seventeen years—and she entered the filing timestamp into her log.

Around her, the Humanities Liaison Office hummed with the particular quiet of a room where people are working very hard on things they cannot fully explain. Two desks over, Gerald Wen was cross-referencing Auditor Statute 1,448,002(b) against an amendment that might or might not have been superseded by a meta-amendment that existed only as a rumor in a footnote. Behind him, Dana Olowu was translating a compliance checklist from a language that used smell as a grammatical case. The air near her workstation was, as a result, unusual.

Miriam's station occupied the corner nearest the portside bulkhead, where a vent cover had been replaced with a sheet of aluminum riveted slightly off-true so that a faint whistle accompanied changes in air pressure. She had requisitioned a proper replacement twice. Both requisitions were pending. She would file a third next week, because that was the correct escalation interval, and because if you stopped filing requisitions the moment a system failed to process them, you would very quickly stop filing anything at all, and then where would you be.

On the floor beside her station, sitting cross-legged on a square of packing foam that had once cushioned navigational components, Pei drew.

Pei was six, or near enough. Her mother, Lian He, worked the hydroponic shift that overlapped with Miriam's filing hours, and the arrangement had calcified into routine the way arrangements do on a ship where three thousand people occupy a space designed for nine hundred: someone needed a thing, someone else could provide it, and within a week it was policy. Pei arrived each morning with a ration-bar in one pocket and a collection of writing implements in the other—actual pencils, when they could be found, or styluses, or grease markers taken from engine maintenance, or, once, a stick of compressed nutrient paste that left wobbly tan lines on everything it touched.

She drew constantly. She drew with the concentration of a safecracker.

Today she was working on a shape. It was not a shape Miriam recognized, though Miriam had not tried very hard to recognize it. It occupied most of a sheet of scrap paper—the back of a decommissioned shift schedule—and it was drawn in a dark blue grease marker that Pei gripped in her full fist, bearing down. The shape had curves that seemed to change direction mid-stroke without Pei lifting the marker. It had angles that a geometer would have found uncomfortable. It was also, at certain points, rather pretty, the way a knot can be pretty if you stop trying to untie it.

Pei finished a line. Held the paper up. Examined it with an expression of serious, private assessment. Then she set it aside, took a fresh sheet, and began another.

When Miriam glanced down on her way to the filing cabinet, she saw the drawing and felt a small, unplaceable tug somewhere behind her sternum—the kind of sensation you get when you almost remember a word in a language you studied in school and abandoned. She looked away. She had a filing cabinet to reach and a cabinet's worth of forms to organize, because the Auditors' system recognized a Species Continuity Extension only if submitted with all prior extensions in reverse chronological order, and the last time Miriam had miscollated a sequence, the rejection notice had taken four months to arrive and contained a seven-page appendix on proper document ordering that was, itself, misnumbered.

The drawing joined the others on the wall. There were dozens now, pinned with tape and magnets and, in one case, a bent paper clip that Miriam had shaped into a hook with the competence of someone who had once, in another life, maintained a cubicle corkboard at the California Department of Motor Vehicles, Branch 1138, Reseda. That corkboard had held family photos, a Sierra Club calendar, and a bumper sticker that read I'D RATHER BE FILING (a gift from a coworker who thought this was ironic; Miriam had thought it was, if anything, a simple statement of preference). The corkboard was gone now. The DMV was gone. Reseda was gone. California was, in the most technical sense, still there—the tectonic plates had not been reclassified—but the concept of California as a place where a person might maintain a corkboard had been processed, stamped, and archived along with everything else.

It had not been fire. People expected fire, when they imagined the end, or flood, or some comprehensible violence. It had been a Tuesday. Miriam had been at Branch 1138, processing a registration renewal for a woman who was upset about a late fee, when the sky, for a period of approximately eleven minutes, displayed a notice. The notice was not in English. It was not in any human language. It was, however, clearly a notice—it had the unmistakable visual grammar of an official communication, the way a stop sign is a stop sign in any country: red, octagonal, absolute.

Eleven minutes. Then the sky returned to its normal appearance, which that day was hazy and 74 degrees, and the woman resumed arguing about the late fee, and Miriam processed the renewal, because what else would you do. And then, over the following six hours, the Auditors arrived—not invading, not descending, simply *present* the way an inspector is present when you've failed a code compliance check—and Earth's status was updated in a central filing system that, as near as anyone could later determine, predated the formation of the planet's sun.

Active Civilization (Tier 2), the old designation had read.

Archived Holdings (Recoverable—Pending Review), it read now.

The planet was still there. You could see it on the colony ship's long-range sensors if you knew which frequency to check, a blue-green dot with the right amount of cloud cover, spinning at the correct rate, orbiting its star with the punctuality of a civil servant who has never once been late. The oceans moved. The trees, presumably, continued. The buildings stood. It was simply that the planet had been *filed*—placed in a category that did not include active habitation or the generation of new cultural, biological, or administrative output. The Auditors had not killed anyone. The word "kill" did not appear in any of their statutes. What they had done was revoke the species' Resource Allocation Permit, which meant that the atmospheric processors, the gravitational calibrators, the quantum-environmental stabilizers—systems so fundamental that no human had known they existed, systems that had been running since before multicellular life, maintained by the Auditors as part of their Basic Existential Infrastructure package—were scheduled for decommission. Not immediately. There was a grace period. The grace period was, by cosmic standards, generous.

By human standards, it was the length of time it took to build three ships and fill them with whoever you could reach.

Two of those ships had failed to file their Transitional Existence Permits on time. Miriam did not know what had happened to them. The Auditors' records classified them as Lapsed, which was a word that, in the Auditors' usage, could mean anything from "dissolved" to "reclassified to a non-observable existence state." Miriam had tried to file an inquiry. The inquiry form required a Kinship Verification Code that could be obtained only from the subjects of the inquiry, which, if they had been dissolved, presented obvious difficulties.

The third ship—this ship, the *Alouette*—had survived because Miriam Fontaine-Park had, on the second day of the voyage, noticed that the Transitional Existence Permit contained a subsection requiring ongoing Species Continuity Extensions filed at intervals determined by a formula she could not solve but could, through trial and error over the course of months, approximate. Her first filing had been rejected. Her second had been rejected. Her fourteenth had been accepted, and she had reverse-engineered from its formatting everything the previous thirteen had gotten wrong, and she had filed the fifteenth, and it had been accepted too, and she had kept going, entering a rhythm that was not hope exactly but something more durable: procedure.

Six thousand two hundred and eleven. One for each cycle. Each one a small, correct thing in a universe that processed small, correct things and ignored everything else.

On the wall, Pei's drawings fluttered in the recycled air. Nobody looked at them. They were a child's drawings, pinned up in an office the way children's drawings are always pinned up in offices, which is to say: decoratively, fondly, and without scrutiny. One of them—done in red grease marker on the back of a nutritional info sheet—contained a pattern that, if subjected to the Auditors' Foundational Authentication Protocol, would have triggered a system response that had not been triggered in eleven thousand years. But no one subjected it to anything. It hung on the wall between a drawing of what appeared to be a cat (Pei had never seen a cat; the ship's biological manifest did not include cats; the drawing was nonetheless unmistakably a cat) and a drawing of nothing anyone could name.

Miriam aligned the edges of her next form. She checked the timestamp. She filed.

## II.

The summons arrived as a vibration in Captain Elena Vasquez's left molar, which was where the Auditors' communications relay had installed itself during her first visit to a Waiting Room three years ago. She had not consented to the installation. She had filed a complaint. The complaint was under review.

The Waiting Room materialized around her between one step and the next—she had been walking the corridor between the bridge and the water-reclamation bay, and then she was not. The corridor was gone, or rather, the corridor was still there and she was simply also somewhere else, both locations true simultaneously in a way that the Auditors' literature described as Concurrent Spatial Filing and that Vasquez privately described as horseshit.

The room was beige. Not a specific beige—not eggshell, not cream, not sand. A *categorical* beige, the beige that exists prior to all particular beiges, the platonic ideal of a color chosen to be as inoffensive as possible and thereby becoming the most offensive color in the universe. The walls were made of a material that was not drywall but aspired to drywall's energy. The ceiling panels emitted light of a frequency that was almost fluorescent—it had the flicker, the faint hum, the slightly greenish cast—but was off by a margin just wide enough to make Vasquez's eyes feel as if they were trying to focus on two things at once.

Chairs lined the walls. They were cushioned in a fabric that was not fabric. Vasquez sat. The chair accepted her weight with the enthusiasm of a handshake from someone who has been told to be friendlier but hasn't internalized why.

A machine on the far wall dispensed numbered tickets. Vasquez had been instructed, during her first visit, to take a number and wait. She took a number. The ticket read:

**Ξ-4421:7(b)/∞-adj.**

She sat back down. The chair readjusted beneath her, neither more nor less comfortable, as if it were iterating toward a concept of comfort it had studied but never experienced.

The Auditor arrived. It came through a door that had not been there and that, afterward, wasn't there again. It was vast the way a storm system is vast—present across more of your visual field than you expected, occupying a space that wasn't precisely space. Vasquez had met Auditors before. They were difficult to describe afterward. Not because they were indescribable—they had features, or at least attributes—but because the features didn't cohere into a memory. You could describe any individual aspect (the shifting blue-gray surfaces, the sense of enormous filing cabinets stacked inside other filing cabinets, the impression of reading glasses perched on something that was not a nose) but the whole refused to assemble. They were a collage that resisted being a portrait.

This one carried a file. The file was thick. The file was, Vasquez realized, the entirety of Miriam Fontaine-Park's seventeen years of Species Continuity Extensions, printed on a medium that was not paper, bound in a material that was not cardboard, and flagged, on its cover, with a symbol that Vasquez recognized from Miriam's rejection-code glossary.

The symbol meant: NONCOMPLIANT — SEE ATTACHED.

"Captain Elena Vasquez," the Auditor said, in a voice that sounded like a hold message you'd been listening to so long you'd mistaken it for your own thoughts. "Representing Continuity Petition: Humanity (Sol-3), Reference Number [a string of notation that Vasquez heard as a pressure change behind her eyes]. Your species' application for Continued Existence under Statute 7,702,441, filed repeatedly across the last seventeen standard revolutions of your former primary orbital body—"

"Years," Vasquez said. "We call them years."

"—has been reviewed and found to be procedurally deficient."

The Auditor opened the file. Pages—or their equivalents—fanned out in the space between them, hanging in the air like a shuffled deck frozen mid-cascade.

"Each of the six thousand two hundred and eleven Species Continuity Extensions submitted by your Humanities Liaison were filed using Form 27-B," the Auditor said. "Form 27-B was deprecated at the conclusion of the previous Cosmic Fiscal Cycle. The current form is 27-B Revised, Subsection Omega."

Vasquez did not blink. She had spent eleven years as a naval officer before the archiving, during which time she had received enough bad news in official language that her face had developed the structural rigidity of a woman who has been told, by successive commanding officers, that the budget has been cut, the deployment has been extended, the shore leave has been cancelled, and the person responsible for these decisions is in a meeting and cannot be reached.

"Fine," she said. "We'll refile. Give us the revised form."

"Form 27-B Revised, Subsection Omega, requires a Precursor Endorsement Stamp."

"Where do we get one?"

"The Precursor Endorsement Stamp was issued exclusively by certified representatives of the Precursor Civilization, Designation Zero-Alpha, who designed and authorized the current Auditing framework."

The Auditor paused. The pause had the particular quality of a customer-service representative preparing to deliver the part of the explanation that will make you ask for a manager.

"The Precursor Civilization was archived."

"Archived," Vasquez repeated.

"Their Resource Allocation Permit was revoked during cycle [notation that registered as a dull ache in Vasquez's temporal lobe]. Their representatives are no longer available to issue Endorsement Stamps. As a consequence, Form 27-B Revised, Subsection Omega, cannot, under current procedural guidelines, be fully completed."

"So the form you need us to fill out requires a stamp from a civilization that no longer exists."

"That is procedurally accurate."

"And every filing we've done for seventeen years is invalid."

"Form 27-B was *deprecated*, Captain. It was not *invalidated*. There is a distinction. Deprecated forms remain in the processing queue but are routed to a secondary review pathway whose current estimated processing time is—" The Auditor consulted something that flickered at the edge of Vasquez's perception. "—four hundred and eleven standard revolutions of your former primary orbital body. Approximately."

"Four hundred years."

"Approximately."

Vasquez stood. The chair she'd been sitting in made a sound that was almost, but not quite, a sigh.

"I want to speak to a supervisor."

"Supervisor consultation is available by petition. Current estimated wait time for supervisor consultation is—"

"Four hundred years."

"Three hundred and eighty-seven. There has been some recent movement in the queue." The Auditor shifted—a motion that was less physical movement and more like a paragraph being reformatted. "Captain, I want you to understand that this office—"

"Your *office*—"
