# Perdition Gulch

## I.

The aquifer remembered the sea.

Three hundred feet beneath the alkali flats and the bunchgrass and the cracked red clay of Perdition Gulch, water moved through Permian limestone at a pace that made centuries look impetuous. The water had been there before the grass, before the antelope, before the people who hunted the antelope and the people who shot the people who hunted the antelope. It moved laterally, a few inches per year, filtering through rock that had once been the floor of an ocean so vast it had no opposite shore. The aquifer did not think, exactly, but if it had, its thoughts would have been limestone thoughts—calcium-slow, pressing, old. It would have thought: *I was here when you arrived. I will be here when you are not.*

Above it, the drought tightened.

It had been tightening for three years, though the word *tightening* suggests a deliberateness the residents of Perdition Gulch would not have granted it. They said, *Dry spell*, and *Seen worse*, and *Rain'll come*. They said these things the way men say *It's nothing* when they feel the first bright wrongness in their chests. The Pecos ran low. The stock tanks filmed over with green scum. The cottonwoods along Caballo Creek dropped their leaves in August, months early, as though embarrassed to keep pretending, and stood with their white limbs exposed like bones in an open grave.

From sufficient altitude—the altitude of hawks, or of basalt, or of narrative—Perdition Gulch in the autumn of 1887 resolved into a geometry of need. The town occupied a bench of high desert at the confluence of two arroyos that carried water perhaps forty days a year and carried dust the other three hundred twenty-five. Sixteen wooden buildings fronted a main street that ran roughly north-south. A church with no pastor. A land office. A feed store with a sagging porch. A saloon called The Resurrectionist, named either in blasphemy or in hope; no one remembered which. Behind these, a scatter of adobes and jacales spread south toward the mines—copper, mostly, with a little silver that had never quite justified the speculation it attracted. The mines employed seventy-three men. The town held perhaps two hundred souls, if you counted the women and children, which the territory's census did intermittently and without enthusiasm.

It was a town like a hundred others in the southern territories, which is to say it was a town that existed in a state of provisional argument with the landscape, and the landscape was winning. But Perdition Gulch had held longer than the odds suggested. Its water rights were better documented than its neighbors'. Its miners were injured less often and recovered faster. Its disputes resolved before they became feuds, its strangers were assessed with an accuracy that bordered on the prophetic, and its two most dangerous men—brothers, or at least something like brothers—had a habit of appearing at the precise locations where violence was about to occur, approaching from two directions simultaneously, in a way that made even drunk men reconsider.

This was not luck. It was architecture.

*

Eliza Crane sat behind the oak desk in the land office, her pen moving in a hand so small and steady it might have belonged to a watchmaker. She was transcribing a water-rights claim from the Dominguez survey of 1851—not from the original document, which had been lost in a courthouse fire in Santa Fe eleven years earlier, but from the version of it that existed, complete and unerring, in her memory. She could see the document as clearly as she saw the desk. More clearly. The desk was scratched and dull. The document was pristine: every serif of the surveyor's hand, every water stain on the foolscap, the place where the ink had run thin and Dominguez had stopped to sharpen his quill. She had read it once, in 1876, for nineteen minutes. It had never dimmed.

She was forty-one. She wore her hair pulled back with a severity that was not vanity but efficiency; she had remembered every strand that had ever fallen across her eyes while she worked, and the accumulation of those memories was maddening in a way she could not explain to anyone who did not carry the weight of total recall. In the top drawer of her desk, beneath a false bottom she had installed herself, lay a stack of journals—forty-seven of them, calf-bound, written in a cipher of her own devising. She did not need them. She needed them absolutely.

Eliza finished the transcription, blotted it, and set it in the claims file. She opened the next folder. A dispute between the Carrizo Mining Company and a rancher named Flood over subsurface rights to a spring. She already knew how this would resolve—she had cross-referenced the original Mexican land grant, the territorial amendment of 1858, and three private letters between the surveyor and the territorial governor that she had read once, in a stack of documents a clerk had left unattended for four minutes in 1879. Carrizo had no claim. The spring was Flood's. She began to write the summary that would, if the territorial judge accepted it, settle the matter without trial.

She did not think of herself as gifted. She thought of herself as cursed and useful, which in Perdition Gulch amounted to the same thing.

*

Behind a curtain of unbleached muslin in the back room of what served as the town's medical office—a generous term for a room with a table, a cabinet of patent medicines, and a lamp that smoked—Samuel Voss set the broken wrist of a miner named Coogan. Coogan was drunk, which helped. Coogan was also frightened, which didn't.

"Hold still."

"I'm holding, I'm holding—Christ, Doc—"

Samuel's hands moved with a precision that was not learned but intrinsic, the way a river finds the lowest point in a landscape. He felt the bones through the skin: the lunate displaced, the scaphoid cracked, the radius intact but bruised. He felt it the way another man might feel cold or heat—not as information but as *sensation*, the wrongness registering in his own wrist as a sympathetic ache that was also a map. He aligned the bones. He held them. He did not pray, because what happened next had nothing to do with prayer.

Under his hands, the bones moved. Cartilage swelled to fill the gap. The hairline crack in the scaphoid knit itself in a process that, if slowed to visibility, would have looked like frost forming on glass—crystalline, branching, inevitable. The swelling in Coogan's wrist went down as though someone had opened a drain.

"Flex it," Samuel said.

Coogan flexed. His eyes went wide. "How in the—"

"Clean break. Set easier than most." Samuel turned to the cabinet, washed his hands in the basin. His back was to Coogan, which meant Coogan could not see his face, which meant he did not have to compose it. He dried his hands on a cloth that was not quite clean. "Keep it wrapped for a week. No swinging a pick for two."

Coogan left marveling at his luck. They always did. Lucky the break was clean. Lucky the doc had good hands. Lucky, lucky, lucky. No one said *miraculous*. Samuel had been in Perdition Gulch for nine years, and in that time, no one had died of any wound or sickness he had touched, and this fact existed in the town's consciousness the way the aquifer existed beneath the ground—essential, unexamined, deep.

After Coogan left, Samuel stood at the basin for a long time. He was looking at his hands. Specifically, he was looking at the back of his left hand, where that morning he had scraped his knuckles on the doorframe—a nothing injury, the kind a body resolves without being asked.

The scrape was still there. It had been six hours.

He pressed it with his thumb. It stung. He noted the sting. He set the observation aside in the place where he kept things he was not yet willing to examine.

*

On the porch of Auerbach's Feed & Supply, a boy sat with his legs dangling over the edge, kicking his heels against the boards. He was ten. His name was James Dunbar, though no one called him anything but the Dunbar boy, or just *boy*, or, among the five, *the child*, as though his youth were not a temporary state but a permanent condition, a role he filled in a structure that needed one. He was eating a biscuit he had taken from the counter inside while Mr. Auerbach was weighing flour. He had not stolen it, exactly. He had known, in the way he knew things, that Mr. Auerbach was going to offer him one anyway—had felt the small warm impulse form in Auerbach's mind like a bubble rising in still water—and had simply abbreviated the transaction.

The town murmured inside his skull.

It always did. It was worse on dry days, when the air was clear and hard and sounds carried farther—not sounds, exactly, but the things beneath sounds, the things that generated sounds. On wet days, which he barely remembered, there was a softness to it, a muffling. Today the murmur was sharp. Mrs. Auerbach, in the back room, thinking about the dress fabric she'd ordered from El Paso and whether the color would be right and whether her sister would be jealous: a stream of *blue, no, more like indigo, Katherine's face when she sees—*. Three miners walking past, their thoughts sun-stupid, copper-heavy: *shift ends four o'clock, four o'clock, goddamn elbow, four o'clock*. The sheriff, two buildings down, behind his desk, his thoughts a low guilty drone: *bottle in the drawer, just one, not yet, maybe at noon, not yet, now—*. Father out: the Mexican woman who took in laundry, hanging sheets behind her house, her mind moving in Spanish that the boy understood not as language but as *feeling*, a texture like rough cotton, homesick, angry, resigned.

He could hear maybe a hundred minds right now, if he let himself. He usually didn't. He had learned, over the years—the five had helped him learn—to hold the murmur at the level of background noise, the way a person who lives near a river eventually stops hearing the water. He listened for spikes. Fear spiked. Rage spiked. Intent spiked—the hard bright thought of a man who had decided to do something, as opposed to the muzzy half-thoughts of men who merely wished.

Today nothing spiked. The town was docile. Bored. Thirsty. The biscuit was good.

But there was something under the murmur today—a frequency he couldn't quite resolve, like trying to hear a conversation in the next room through a thick wall. It was coming from the direction of the land office. No. From everywhere. No. From *under* everything, as if the murmur itself had a subfloor and something beneath it was shifting. He chewed the biscuit. He frowned. He kicked his heels against the boards and tried to listen harder, but the harder he listened, the more the thing receded, like a word on the tip of a tongue that flees the effort to recall it.

He let it go. He finished the biscuit.

He would be thirty before he understood what that low shifting sound had been. By then he would hear nothing at all.

*

South of town, at the mouth of a draw that led up to the Carrizo copper workings, a man named Lyle Tynan walked east along the road toward the assay office. He was twenty-seven, tall, narrow, with a stillness about him that people mistook for calm and that was in fact the particular tension of a man listening to something no one else could hear. At the same moment, half a mile to the north, on the opposite side of the main street, his brother Nash Tynan—same height, same stillness, same pale eyes with their unsettling clarity—walked west along the road past the livery. They were not twins. They were not, as far as either of them could determine, brothers in the biological sense. They had arrived in Perdition Gulch within a week of each other in 1881, strangers who recognized each other on sight the way a left hand recognizes a right, and they had never been fully apart since.

They did not need to be near each other. That was the point. They could be on opposite sides of a mountain and still move in the same rhythm, still feel the pressure of each other's attention like a hand on the back of the neck. When Lyle turned his head to check the angle of the sun, Nash turned his head at the same instant, same angle, same involuntary squint. When Nash stopped walking to retie his bootlace, Lyle paused mid-stride, shifted his weight, waited—not consciously, not deliberately, but the way a flock of starlings shifts direction: all at once, all together, through a mechanism that has no name and needs none.

They were the town's perimeter. When trouble came to Perdition Gulch—and trouble came regularly, because the mines attracted rough men and the territory's law was thin and brittle as the crust over a lava tube—the Tynan brothers materialized at its edges, bracketing the threat. A drunk miner pulling a gun in the saloon would find Lyle at the front door and Nash at the back. A group of riders approaching with undeclared intentions would find one brother on the ridge above the road and the other at the ford below it. The sheriff, who was not stupid even when he was drinking, had stopped asking how they knew. He used them the way a carpenter uses a level: without understanding the physics, trusting the result.

Today they walked their separate routes and felt the town between them like a web they'd each hold one side of. The web was intact. The web was taut. But Lyle—or was it Nash—felt, in the place where their shared awareness lived, a faint slack. A thread loose somewhere. A vibration that didn't carry properly. He couldn't locate it. He didn't try. The web had always held.

The drought pressed down on the town like a palm on a skull, even and patient, and the town went on.

## II.

This is what happened at the Arroyo Piedras on the night of April 11, 1884.

*

Seven families camped in the wash.

They were Nuevomexicanos—farmers from the Mesilla Valley who had been moving north toward land they'd been promised by a grant that existed as genuine paper in Santa Fe and as a vague rumor in the territories between. Thirty-one people. Eleven children. They had four wagons, sixteen head of cattle that were not worth the water they drank, and a single-shot Springfield rifle that the eldest man, a grandfather named Tomás Escalante, kept wrapped in oilcloth beneath his wagon seat. They camped in the arroyo because the walls cut the wind, and because the sand was flat, and because they did not know—had no reason to know—that the arroyo's geography, which provided shelter from the elements, also provided concealment for anything approaching from the south.

They made fires. They cooked beans. The children played in the sand at the edge of the firelight. A woman named Consuela Baca sang to a baby. The sound carried in the cold desert air the way sounds do when the atmosphere holds no moisture—clearly, without distortion, as though the air itself had been stripped of everything that could muffle or soften or forgive.

*
