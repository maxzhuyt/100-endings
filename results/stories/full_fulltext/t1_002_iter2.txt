### Exhibit B-1: Audio Diary of Carol Ann Freely — Entry 1

**[Tape 1, Side A. Recorded the evening of March 9, 1987. Background noise: kitchen sounds — running water, the clink of a dish being placed in a rack. A television is audible in another room, indistinct.]**

This is Carol Freely. It's about quarter to nine, Monday evening, March the ninth.

I'm recording this — I suppose I should say why I'm recording this. I've kept audio notes on long trials since the Harmon case in '79. It helps me catch errors in the transcript before certification. I review the day's session, I note anything I want to double-check against the tape, and I erase the diary when the case is closed. It's a professional practice. I've done it for eleven years and it's never been interesting to anyone, including me.

Today was the first day of *State v. Vance*. Eleanor Vance. The Vance Ritual Abuse Case, as the *Braxton Democrat* has been calling it for five months, though Judge McClatchy has instructed everyone to use the docket number.

*(Pause. Water shuts off.)*

I'd seen her photograph in the paper, of course. Everyone has. The photograph they keep using is from her teaching certificate — she's smiling in it, she's wearing a blouse with a Peter Pan collar, and if you didn't read the caption you'd think it was an ad for the continuing education program at Glenville State. That's part of why the paper uses it, I think. The contrast.

She doesn't look like the photograph.

She was brought in through the side door at 8:47. I know the time because I'd just synchronized my steno machine's clock — I always do, first morning of a trial. Judge McClatchy keeps his courtroom cold, fifty-eight, sixty degrees, and the fluorescents have a cycling hum that I can hear through my headset. It's an E-flat. I've checked. After enough years in that room you learn its pitch the way you learn the pitch of your own refrigerator.

Eleanor Vance was led to the defense table by a female deputy. She sat down. She placed her hands on the table, one on top of the other, left over right. She looked at Mr. Fein, her attorney. He was arranging papers. She waited.

I've recorded arraignments where the defendant wept, where the defendant screamed, where a defendant tried to climb over the bar. In 1983 a man named Dwayne Skaggs removed his shoes and threw them at the prosecutor, and I had to transcribe Judge McClatchy saying the words "Mr. Skaggs, put your shoes back on," which I believe may be the finest sentence I've committed to the record.

Eleanor Vance sat with her hands folded and waited for her attorney to finish arranging his papers.

That's all I have to note. The transcript is clean. I'll review the afternoon session tape tomorrow before we resume. My job is to record what is said, not what is meant, and today nothing was said that presented any difficulty.

*(Pause. Television volume increases briefly in the other room — a laugh track — then lowers again.)*

Tommy has school tomorrow. I need to go tell him to brush his teeth.

**[End of Entry 1.]**

### Exhibit B-2: Audio Diary of Carol Ann Freely — Entry 2

**[Tape 1, Side A (continued). Recorded late evening, March 9, 1987. Background noise: house quiet. A clock ticking.]**

Carol Freely, supplemental note, same date. It's — I think it's about eleven-thirty. Everyone's in bed.

Just a technical note for the transcript review. The defendant's outburst during the prosecution's opening. I transcribed it phonetically for my own backup copy before McClatchy ordered it stricken. This is standard — I always capture first, then apply the Court's redaction to the certified version. What goes to the clerk is clean.

The phonetic transcription gave me some trouble, which is unusual. Eleven years, and the only human sounds I've had real difficulty with are stroke victims and one gentleman from Braxton County who was missing most of his soft palate. The Vance outburst wasn't like either of those. The phonemes were — I want to say *clean*. Distinct. I could differentiate the consonant boundaries without difficulty. The vowel sounds were open and sustained. As a matter of pure phonetic capture it should have been straightforward.

The difficulty was that the rhythm was too regular. Like someone reciting something they'd memorized but no longer understood. I've transcribed people speaking in tongues — the Holiness churches up in Clay County, the Stover case — and glossolalia has a recognizable texture. It accelerates. It clusters. It rides on the speaker's breathing and you can feel the emotion shaping the sounds even when the sounds don't mean anything.

This wasn't that. The rhythm was steady. The intervals between syllable groups were consistent. If I'd been transcribing it as verse, the line breaks would have fallen at regular intervals.

It's a technical observation. I note it for the transcript review because if she does it again, I want to be prepared for the phonetic challenge.

That's all.

**[End of Entry 2.]**

**DAY 2 — AFTERNOON SESSION**

*Testimony of Dr. Patricia Kern, child psychologist, for the prosecution.*

---

MR. KESSLER: Dr. Kern, in your clinical evaluation of the children, did you identify patterns consistent with ritualistic abuse?

DR. KERN: Yes. The children's disclosures contained a number of elements that are consistent with what the literature identifies as hallmarks of satanic ritual abuse. Specifically: references to animal sacrifice, to being made to participate in chanting, to the presence of robed figures, and to being told that a "bad thing" would happen to their families if they told anyone.

MR. KESSLER: And these disclosures were made independently by the children?

DR. KERN: Each child was interviewed separately, yes.

MR. KESSLER: Were the questions you used based on any established methodology?

DR. KERN: I used an adapted version of the protocol developed by Dr. Roland Summit and the interview framework recommended in the 1984 Childhelp training manual.

MR. KESSLER: And in your professional opinion, do these disclosures reflect genuine experiences?

DR. KERN: In my professional opinion, yes. Children of this age group do not fabricate narratives of this specificity and internal consistency without an experiential basis.

MR. FEIN: Objection. The witness is stating a conclusion that is—

THE COURT: Overruled. The witness is testifying within her area of expertise. Continue.
