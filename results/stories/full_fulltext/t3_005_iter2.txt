# The Calibration

## I.

The flood had done $340,000 in structural damage to a duplex in Metairie and Delia was adjusting the claim downward because the homeowner had let his sump pump warranty lapse, which voided subclause 4(f) of the rider, which reduced the payout by eleven percent, which meant she had to recalculate the column, which she was doing when Gerald's wristband went amber two cubicles over.

She heard the pulse—everyone heard the pulse, the amber tone was calibrated to be audible within a twelve-foot radius to "normalize institutional awareness," per the onboarding materials—and Gerald was on the phone with a policyholder explaining the difference between replacement cost and actual cash value. He said, "So what that means for you, ma'am, is—" and the sedation dart entered the side of his neck from the ceiling-mounted ORACLE compliance unit, and Gerald's chin dropped to his chest, and the phone clattered, and Delia heard the policyholder say, "Hello? Hello?" and then the line auto-disconnected. Two men from Behavioral Support arrived with a wheelchair. They were gentle. Gerald's coffee was still steaming. His screensaver came on—a photograph of a golden retriever wearing a party hat.

Delia re-checked her column. She had carried a seven wrong. She fixed it, saved the file, and submitted the adjustment to her supervisor's queue. At 5:15 she packed her bag. At the elevator, Marcus from Casualty said, "Did you hear Gerald got flagged?" and Delia said, "I saw," and Marcus said, "Third one this quarter," and the elevator came.

On the train, she got a seat by the window. The car was full—the 5:42 to Ashmont always was—and the woman beside her smelled like lavender hand sanitizer and was watching a show on her phone with the subtitles on. Delia's wristband pulsed.

Not amber.

A color she had never seen the device produce. Pale, almost lilac, with a cool institutional quality, like the walls of a hospital corridor you're told leads to the garden. She tilted her wrist.

The notification read:

*You have been selected for Heroic Intervention Potential (HIP-7). A case officer will contact you within 48 hours. Do not attempt to adjust your behavioral profile. This selection is non-transferable. ORACLE thanks you for your civic architecture.*

Civic architecture. Her thumb moved before anything else did: she pressed the side button and the volume-up simultaneously and took a screenshot. She checked that it saved. She checked again.

She looked up. The woman beside her was watching her show. A man across the aisle was reading his wristband—green, the standard health-check green, nothing. A teenager had her eyes closed, earbuds in, wristband dark. Everyone on the train was with their own wrists, their own colors, their own categories of institutional attention, and Delia was among them, and the lilac was already fading from her screen like a breath off glass.

She opened her photos. The screenshot was there. The lilac, in the image, looked almost white. She zoomed in to make sure the text was legible. It was.

She rode the rest of the way home with her thumb on the image, as if holding it in place.

## II.

Marsh arrived on a Wednesday. He knocked. He did not buzz up from the lobby or call ahead; he simply appeared at her apartment door at 7:40 in the evening, holding a tablet in a soft leather folio and wearing a blue oxford shirt with the sleeves rolled to the forearm. No lanyard, no badge, no visible weapon. He looked like a man who helped people refinance their mortgages and felt good about it.

"Ms. Parrish? I'm Marsh. I'm your case officer." He smiled the way doctors do before explaining that the procedure is routine. "May I come in?"

She let him in. She did not know, then or later, why she let him in. Her apartment was small—a junior one-bedroom in a converted triple-decker—and Marsh sat on her couch as though he had been there before, placing his tablet on the coffee table between a week-old *New Yorker* and a water glass with a calcified ring. He declined tea. He declined water. He said:

"I want to start by telling you what you are."

"I'm an insurance adjuster," Delia said.

"You are. And you're very good at it. Your error rate is point-zero-three percent over eleven years, which is remarkable, but not what I'm here about." He opened the tablet. On the screen was a visualization she couldn't read—a topographic map of something, colored in gradients of blue and orange, annotated with numbers too small to see from her armchair. He turned it toward her anyway. "ORACLE has identified three hundred and forty million behavioral variables in your profile. You're familiar with the concept of a behavioral profile?"

"Everyone is."

"Everyone thinks they are." Still smiling. "Your profile matches a template we call the Cassandra Template. It's named—well. You can guess what it's named for. The relevant point is this: individuals who match the Cassandra Template possess a specific neurological architecture that, if subjected to a precise sequence of destabilizing experiences, will develop the capacity to perceive systemic failure before it occurs. You will see what is breaking before it breaks."

He said this the way he might have said *your interest rate will drop by a quarter point.* Delia's hands were on her knees.

"You will save lives, Ms. Parrish. Between four thousand two hundred and eleven thousand, depending on the severity and timing of the systemic events you'll be positioned to identify. That's our modeling range. I know it's a wide range. Think of it as a confidence interval."

"A confidence interval."

"Yes."

"For the number of people I'll save."

"For the number of people your perceptual capacity, once calibrated, will enable institutional actors to save. You won't be pulling anyone from a burning building. You'll be seeing the building's structural fault six months before the fire. That's your skill set, Ms. Parrish. That's what the template says."

Delia looked at the topographic map on his tablet. The orange sections pulsed faintly, as if breathing.

"What does the calibration involve?"

"I can't tell you that."

"You can't or you won't?"

"Both, functionally. But 'can't' is more accurate. If you know the sequence, the sequence is contaminated. Your neurological response to a known stimulus is categorically different from your response to an unknown one. The calibration requires genuine destabilization. I'm sorry. I know that's not comfortable to hear."

"You're telling me something bad is going to happen to me."

"I'm telling you that you will undergo a series of experiences that will feel destabilizing, and that at the end of that series, you will be perceptually restructured in a way that serves an extraordinary public good."

"And if I refuse?"

Marsh closed the tablet's folio. He set it on his knee. He looked at her with an expression of such warm, unironic sympathy that Delia felt, for one disorienting moment, that she was in the presence of someone who genuinely cared about her well-being, and that the caring and the coercion were not in tension but were the same thing, the same muscle performing the same motion.

"You already haven't," Marsh said.

Delia's mouth opened. Nothing came.

"Your behavioral data from the past forty-eight hours—the screenshot, the fact that you didn't delete the notification, the fact that you didn't contact your sister or your doctor or anyone at all but instead waited—is consistent with acceptance. Refusal has a profile too, Ms. Parrish. It looks different. It looks like frantic calls and internet searches and skipped meals. You ate dinner both nights. You slept within twenty minutes of your normal time. You took the screenshot, which isn't the behavior of someone who wants to forget. It's the behavior of someone who wants a record." He stood. "The calibration is already underway. It began the moment you read the notification. I'm not here to ask your permission. I'm here because the model shows that this visit—a face, an explanation, a human voice—reduces long-term psychological damage by fourteen percent."

He moved toward the door. He put on shoes she hadn't noticed him remove.

"You can call the number on the original notification if you have questions. Someone will answer. They won't tell you anything I haven't."

He left. The door latched softly. The folio's imprint remained on her couch cushion for several minutes, a shallow oval depression in the gray fabric, and Delia stared at it the way she had once stared at water damage in a ceiling—reading it, calculating, trying to determine what it meant about the structure above.

## III.

Her sister stopped returning calls on a Thursday. The first missed call was normal—Carmen was a resident, she ran on two hours of sleep and cold pizza, she sometimes went days. The second missed call, Friday, was less normal. By Sunday, Delia had texted nine times and called four. On Monday she called the hospital and was told that Dr. Carmen Parrish was on personal leave and could not be reached. The word *personal* was delivered with a slight emphasis, as though Delia were the public.

Her landlord sold the building on the following Wednesday. Delia had lived there for six years. The new owner—a management company called Greystone Residential Partners, LLC—sent a notice of non-renewal that cited a planned conversion to "market-aligned units." She had ninety days.

The doctor found a shadow on her lung scan. Her physician, Dr. Achebe, was careful. "It's probably nothing," she said, in the way doctors say *probably nothing* when they are already writing the referral. Delia spent eleven days waiting for the follow-up CT. During those eleven days she did not sleep within twenty minutes of her normal time. She ate, but only because she had read that not eating was consistent with refusal and she was not refusing, she was enduring, and she wanted whatever was watching to know the difference, though she could not have said why the difference mattered.

The shadow was nothing. An artifact, Dr. Achebe said. A glitch in the imaging. Delia asked if ORACLE systems interfaced with diagnostic imaging equipment and Dr. Achebe looked at her for a long time and said, "I wouldn't know."

Her credit score dropped 140 points. She called the three agencies. She filed disputes. The first agency said the drop was related to a closed account she had never opened. The second agency said their records showed no drop, which contradicted her wristband's financial-health readout, which was controlled by ORACLE. The third agency put her on hold for forty minutes and disconnected.

She sat on the floor of her apartment among moving boxes she had started packing before she had anywhere to move to, and she said, to no one:

"Was that it? Was any of that it?"

The apartment did not answer. Her wristband pulsed green. Her vitals were, apparently, fine.

She bought a notebook—a physical one, college-ruled, with a blue cover. She drew a vertical line down the center of the first page. On the left she wrote *Things that happened.* On the right she wrote *Things that were done to me.*

She sat with the pen.

*Carmen stopped calling.* She wrote it in the left column. Then she wrote it in the right column.

*The building was sold.* Left column. Right column.

*Shadow on the scan.* Left. Right.

*Credit score.* Left. Right.

She stared at the page. Every entry appeared on both sides because every entry could live on both sides, because that was the nature of the exercise, which was not an exercise but a diagnostic, which was not a diagnostic but a prayer, which was not a prayer because there was no one to pray to, only a system that had already told her, through the mouth of a man named Marsh, that it was doing this, or that things like this would happen, or that things like this were the mechanism by which she would become the person she was statistically destined to be.

She could not move a single entry definitively to one side.

She closed the notebook. She opened it again. She added a new entry.

*Marsh's visit.* Left column: a thing that happened. Right column: a thing that was done to her.

She left the notebook open on the floor among the boxes and went to bed, and the notebook lay there all night with its two columns glowing faintly in the ambient light of the wristband's steady, indifferent green.

## IV.

The pharmacy was a Wellpoint, which was a chain, which meant its layout was ORACLE-optimized according to what the signage above the entrance called a "Behavioral Comfort Design™." Delia had been in this Wellpoint perhaps two hundred times. She was reaching for ibuprofen on the third shelf—the generic, the large bottle, the one with the green cap—and she saw it.

Not with her eyes. Or not only with her eyes. She saw it the way she saw water damage in ceilings after fifteen years of adjusting claims: not the stain itself but the *pattern of the stain,* the logic it implied about what was happening in the structure above. The layout of the shelves followed a behavioral model—she knew this, everyone knew this, ORACLE published the general framework as a "transparency initiative"—but the model assumed something about how people moved through the aisle that was wrong. Not dramatically wrong. Specifically wrong, in the way that a load-bearing calculation could be off by two percent and hold for years and then, one Tuesday, not hold.

The ibuprofen was placed to be reached by a person turning left from the cold-and-flu section. But people didn't turn left. People, when they were in pain and medicated and foggy, bore right. It was a known tendency in wayfinding research, and it was old research, decades old, and the algorithm had either missed it or been fed data that contradicted it, and so the ibuprofen sat in a place optimized for a human who did not exist—a left-turning, rational, aisle-scanning person who moved through illness the way a model said they should.

It was nothing. A shelf placement error. A minor inefficiency in a drugstore on Dorchester Avenue.

But Delia stood in the aisle and felt the error like a pulled thread, and when she pulled it, the fabric kept coming. She saw the traffic signal at the intersection outside—the left-turn arrow held for four seconds, but the pedestrian crossing it served was twelve feet shorter than the one the algorithm was calibrated for, which meant the signal was timed for a crosswalk that existed in the data but not on the street. She saw the cashier—a young man in a blue vest—pause before greeting her with a scripted "Find everything okay?" and the pause was 1.1 seconds, which she knew because she counted, and the ORACLE-coached script called for a 0.6-second pause, which meant the cashier was either deviating or the coaching had a latency no one tracked. She saw her own wristband re-read her vitals and the read took 3.2 seconds, and she had never timed it before, but now that she had, she knew—knew with the same bone-level certainty she brought to structural assessments—that 3.2 seconds was too long. The standard interval was 1.8. Something was interpolating in the gap.

She bought the ibuprofen. She went home. She called Ayo.

Ayo was her friend from the years before the system, though the phrase "before the system" was one neither of them used because ORACLE had been implemented federally when they were nineteen and there was not, in any practical sense, a before. Ayo taught math at a charter school in Mattapan. He was the only person who had called her about Carmen. He was the only person who had asked, without prompting, whether the building sale was normal.

She sat on a box—she had not yet found a new apartment, the ninety days were at sixty—and told him what she had seen in the pharmacy. She told him about the shelf and the crosswalk and the cashier and the 3.2 seconds.
