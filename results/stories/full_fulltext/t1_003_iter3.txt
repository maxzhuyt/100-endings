# The Collectors

## I.

Margot's hallway was four inches wider than her body if she turned sideways and exhaled. She navigated it the way a kayaker navigates a slot canyon — by feel, by memory, by the absolute faith that the walls, though pressing, would not collapse today. The walls were not walls. They were newspapers, sorted by date and region, January 2009 through last Thursday, Pacific Northwest on the left, Mountain West on the right, a system so intuitive to her that she could pull the Boise *Statesman* from March 14, 2016 without looking, the way a concert pianist finds a chord in the dark. She was re-sorting a subsection — Idaho weeklies had gotten mixed in with the Montana dailies during a moment of weakness last spring — when she heard the mail slot clap shut. The envelope was on the floor between a tower of *National Geographics* and a bin labeled COUPONS (VALID). Block letters. No return address. She turned it over, slit it open with her thumbnail. One sentence: *Come get what you buried, or I'll dig it up for everyone.* A date. Coordinates. She read it again. Then she slid it into its own labeled bin — CORRESPONDENCE (UNCLASSIFIED) — and pushed the bin into the wall of Idaho weeklies, where it fit as if a space had always been waiting for it, which, in a sense, it had. There was always a space. The space was the problem.

---

Pilar was fifty-nine seconds into a CVS on Lexington Avenue when the lipstick slid into her coat pocket. It was a shade called Brick Kiln, which she did not need, did not want, and would never wear — she had forty-one lipsticks at home in shades ranging from Dusty Mauve to arterial red, all of them stolen, all of them unopened, each one sealed in its original packaging with the electromagnetic tag still attached because removing the tag would mean she intended to use it, and she did not intend to use it, she intended to *have* it, which was a different verb that lived in a different part of her brain. The cashier didn't look up. They never looked up. Pilar exited the store with the lipstick and her purchased items — a bottle of water and a pack of gum, the purchased items were always negligible, a tithe to the transaction gods — and walked home, where the envelope was wedged under her apartment door. Block letters. No return address. *Come get what you buried, or I'll dig it up for everyone.* She read it on the kitchen counter and then read it again, and then she folded it once and slipped it inside the interior pocket of a Theory blazer she'd lifted from a Nordstrom in White Plains the previous October, which hung in the very back of a closet she rarely opened, among thirty other garments she had never earned and would never wear. It disappeared into the pocket like a shoplifted thing. Like something no one would miss.

Kit was kneeling on the bathroom floor at 1:47 a.m. with two fingers in her mouth and a bottle of blue Gatorade on the sink ledge — the Gatorade was for after, it was always for after, a flavor she now associated exclusively with the sour emptiness that followed — when she heard the sound of something sliding under the front door. She finished. She rinsed. She drank the Gatorade in four long swallows. The envelope was facedown on the tile of the entryway. Block letters. No return address. *Come get what you buried, or I'll dig it up for everyone.* She read it standing up. She read it again sitting on the edge of the bathtub with the overhead light buzzing and her ribs aching. Then she put it in a Tupperware container — the good kind, the kind with the locking tabs — and sealed it and walked to the kitchen and placed it in the back of the freezer behind a bag of edamame and a pint of ice cream she kept there the way a sober person keeps a bottle on a shelf, to prove she could be near it without opening it, though she had opened it twice, and refrozen it, and opened it again, and the ice cream now had a geological cross-section of freeze-thaw layers that recorded her losses like tree rings. The letter went behind the ice cream, vacuum-sealed, airtight, preserved. The way she preserved everything: by making it cold enough to stop.

Danny was up forty-three dollars and down the entire architecture of his evening when the notification hit his phone at 3:11 a.m. He was not winning. This was arithmetically true — he was up forty-three dollars against a lifetime deficit that, if you included the car and the first apartment and the thing with the retirement account, sat somewhere north of sixty thousand — but his nervous system did not process arithmetic. His nervous system processed *streaks*, and he was on one: three parlays in a row, all underdogs, the sweet hallucinatory logic of pattern where no pattern existed. He tapped CONFIRM BET on a four-leg accumulator involving two minor-league baseball teams he could not have found on a map, and then he saw the email notification at the top of his screen. No subject line. No sender. He opened it: *Come get what you buried, or I'll dig it up for everyone.* A date. Coordinates. He read it. He read it again. He closed the email, opened the betting app, stared at the spinning wheel of a live result, felt absolutely nothing shift in his chest because the letter had landed in the same neural slot as every other long-odds proposition he'd ever received — a thing that probably wasn't real but whose potential consequences were too catastrophic to ignore, which was, biochemically, indistinguishable from a bet. He folded a printout of the email — he printed it, he didn't know why — and slid it into his poker chip case, the leather one his father had given him ironically and he had kept sincerely, between the hundred-dollar chips he used as worry stones when he couldn't sleep. The letter sat there among the chips like a marker. Like a debt someone had finally called.

## II.

The gate was chain-link, eight feet tall, leaning forward at a five-degree angle that made it look like it was trying to tell you something confidential. The sign that had once read CAMP ARROW LAKE hung from a single remaining bolt, the first two words intact, the third reduced to a jagged L and a suggestion of a K, the A long gone. The camp's name, or what was left of it, read CAMP ARROW L, which sounded to Pilar, standing in the gravel lot at dusk with a duffel bag over one shoulder, like a place that did not know how to finish its own sentences. She'd gotten there first. She was always the first to arrive and the last to be noticed.

A truck pulled up second. The woman who climbed out was shorter than Pilar remembered and carrying three bags — a rolling suitcase, a canvas tote with magazines fanning from the top, and a backpack so overstuffed that its zippers had been reinforced with binder clips. Margot. She had the same face, just more of it somehow, the features crowded by a kind of fullness that Pilar associated not with weight but with accumulation, as if her body, too, had been unable to stop collecting.

"Pilar?"

"Margot."

"You look —"

"Don't."

"I was going to say 'exactly the same.'"

"Worse. You were going to say I look exactly the same but mean that I look worse."

"I was going to mean it as a compliment."

"A compliment is when someone says something kind that they also believe. That's not what was about to happen."

A sedan arrived. Kit emerged slowly, in segments, the way someone exits a vehicle when their body is a series of decisions rather than a single organism. She was thinner than she should have been and wearing a hoodie in the August heat. She raised a hand in a wave so tentative it might have been a flinch.

"Kit," Margot said.

"You all got one, too," Kit said, which was not a question.

"No, we drove nine hours because we missed the mosquitoes," Pilar said.

Danny came last, on foot, from the direction of the road, as though he'd been dropped off or had walked from somewhere buses stop. He wore a jacket that had once been expensive and sneakers that had never been. His eyes moved the way eyes move across a craps table — tracking everything, calculating nothing, hoping that attention alone would change the odds.

"Danny," Margot said.

"Nobody calls me Danny anymore."

"What do they call you?"

"Daniel."

"Nobody's calling you Daniel," Pilar said.

They stood in a loose semicircle near the gate and performed the ritual of people who had once known each other completely and now knew each other not at all: they cataloged damage. Each of them did it silently, reflexively, with the coiled precision of someone examining a rival's hand. Pilar noted the thinness of Kit's wrists. Kit noted the overstuffed geometry of Margot's luggage. Margot noted that Danny's jacket had a torn lining he'd tried to repair with tape, which meant money was a problem, which meant the gambling was still happening. Danny noted that Pilar's duffel bag hung on her shoulder in a way that suggested she had packed light, which was wrong, which meant the weight she was carrying was in her pockets, which he could see were full — bulging, in fact, with shapes that were not hands.

Nobody said *I missed you*. Nobody said *I hate you*. These were both true and therefore too simple to speak.

"So," Kit said. "Fifteen years."

"Sixteen," Margot corrected. Margot always corrected. She had the kind of mind that experienced imprecision as a splinter.

"Last time was Brian Kowalski's wedding," Danny said. "Where you —"

"We agreed not to talk about Brian Kowalski's wedding," Pilar said.

"We agreed not to talk about what happened *at* Brian Kowalski's wedding. The wedding itself is fair game."

"Nothing about Brian Kowalski is fair game. Brian Kowalski is a closed subject."

Danny raised both hands. "Withdrawn."

They faced the gate. Pilar reached out and touched the chain-link with her right hand, her fingers fitting easily between the diamond-shaped gaps, though there had been a time, and they all remembered it, when her fingers had been small enough to push all the way through to the second knuckle — when the fence had been a thing you could almost pass through, almost dissolve into, if you were young enough and your bones hadn't finished hardening.

A shard of the old sign — the missing A, it turned out, or a piece of it, wedged into the gravel near the gatepost — found its way into Pilar's jacket pocket as she steadied herself climbing over a root. She didn't look at it. She didn't feel herself take it. Her hands had their own economy, and they collected debts she never authorized.

Margot pushed the gate open. It moved with a groan that was almost vocal, almost a word. They walked through, single file, Margot first because she was closest, Danny last because he was calculating whether this was the kind of situation where the last person in has the best odds or the worst.

They were twenty yards up the path, in the blue-gray corridor between the pines, when Pilar stopped and said, "Did anyone else hear that?"

Hear what.

She walked back to the gate and pushed it. It didn't move. She pushed harder. Chain and padlock, exterior side. The sound she'd heard was the snap of the lock engaging.

"It's locked," she said.

Nobody spoke. The pines above them moved in a wind they couldn't feel at ground level, and the sound was like the shuffling of a vast, disorganized deck.

## III.

The lodge smelled like mildew and Pine-Sol and that specific olfactory ghost that haunts any building where children once slept in large numbers — a sweetness layered over something sour, sugar over sweat, the scent of adolescence composting. They stood in the doorway and let their eyes adjust.

The walls had been redecorated.

That was the wrong word. The walls had been *curated*. Floor to ceiling, every surface that was not window or door had been covered in laminated photographs, eight-by-tens and five-by-sevens and the occasional panoramic strip, each one mounted with museum-grade precision, each one annotated in a small, cramped handwriting that required them to step close to read. The photographs were from that summer. Their summer. And in each one, a face had been circled in red marker — sometimes one of theirs, sometimes a counselor's, once a photo of the lake itself with the surface of the water circled, as though the lake were also a suspect.

Timestamps in the margins. June 14: *Pilar takes extra dessert from the canteen for the second consecutive day. Pattern emerging. See notes.* June 22: *Margot refuses to discard her broken lanyard. Keeps it in her left pocket. Possible attachment pathology. Monitor.* June 29: *Kit excuses herself from dinner 6th time in 12 days. Always exactly 11 minutes. Returns with red eyes. No one asks.* July 3: *Danny bets Marcus two desserts he can hold his breath longer. Wins. Bets again immediately. Loses. Bets again. Dynamic suggests escalation threshold consistent w/ early-onset compulsive wagering. Recommend intervention.*

"He was *diagnosing* us," Kit whispered. "We were twelve."

"We were eleven," Margot said. "I was twelve. The rest of you were eleven."

"That's not the point, Margot."

"I know it's not the point. But it's the fact."

The television above the fireplace turned on. The screen was blue for a moment — the color of public-access dead air, the color of a channel that doesn't exist — and then a face filled it. He was older. The jawline had softened, the hair had gone to a white so clean it looked deliberate, and he wore a fleece pullover that Pilar recognized from a brand that cost four hundred dollars at retail. But the eyes were the same: the pale, focused, disconcertingly *interested* eyes of a man who believed that looking at you hard enough was the same as understanding you.

"Hey, troopers," he said.

Nobody breathed.

"I know," he continued, raising a palm to the camera as though anticipating objection. "I know this isn't how you expected to spend your weekend. But I want you to hear me when I say this: I am so, *so* glad you came."

"He's talking to us like we're still eleven," Danny said.

"Shh," said Kit.

"Midge," the counselor said, looking directly into the camera, and Margot flinched at the nickname so hard her shoulders hit the wall behind her. "Sticky." Pilar closed her eyes. "Bird." Kit pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. "Ace." Danny went still.

"I've kept your seats warm," the counselor said. He smiled. The smile was the worst part. It was genuine. "It's been a long time, and I know you've all been struggling. I know because I've been paying attention. That's what I do. That's what I've always done."

He explained. He used the voice that Pilar remembered from trust falls and reflective journaling sessions and the time he'd sat with Kit on the infirmary steps after she'd eaten herself sick on s'mores night and, rather than calling her parents, had simply said, *You know, Bird, your body is trying to tell you something, and I think we should listen.* It was the voice of care. Radical, obsessive, meticulously documented care.
