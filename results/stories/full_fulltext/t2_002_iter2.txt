# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

## I.

The violation notice was printed on fluorescent orange cardstock, laminated, and zip-tied to the railing at a height of forty-two inches—the precise height specified in Section 7.3.1 of the Willowbrook Commons Design Standards for "temporary signage affixed to common-area-adjacent structures." It fluttered slightly in the October wind like a thing that knew it was being looked at.

Meredith Chen set her pharmacy bag on the porch step and read it.

> **NOTICE OF VIOLATION — WILLOWBROOK COMMONS HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATION**
>
> **Property:** Lot 23, 414 Briar Elm Court
>
> **Owner(s) of Record:** Harold R. Chen & Meredith L. Chen
>
> **Violation:** Exterior Structural Modification constructed without prior submission of Architectural Review Application (Form ARA-1), in contravention of Section 4.11.2(b) of the Willowbrook Commons Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions, Amended March 2011. Specifically: wooden access ramp, approx. 14 ft. in length, attached to front-facing elevation, featuring non-approved railing material (pressure-treated pine; approved materials listed in Appendix C) and exceeding the maximum 4-inch lateral encroachment into the Common Setback Easement as defined in Exhibit B, Plat Map, recorded Deed Book 4417, p. 231.
>
> **Remedy Required:** Removal of unauthorized structure within fourteen (14) calendar days of notice date, OR retroactive submission of Form ARA-1 with applicable fees. Failure to comply will result in continuing fines of $50.00 per day, assessed retroactively to date of installation (September 22).
>
> **Notice Date:** October 3
>
> **Days Accrued as of Notice:** 11
>
> **Balance Due:** $550.00

Through the screen door, Harold was asleep in the wheelchair the ramp had been built for. His chin rested on his chest. The oxygen concentrator beside him produced its rhythmic mechanical exhale—a two-beat pulse, like a word it kept trying to say. His hands were folded in his lap the way they were always folded now, curled slightly inward, as though holding something he'd already set down.

Their son, Michael, and their neighbor Frank Gutierrez had built the ramp over a single weekend in September. Frank had a truck. Michael had taken the red-eye from Denver. They'd worked from a YouTube video and a set of ADA specifications Michael had printed at the FedEx in the airport. The lumber was from Home Depot. The screws were from Frank's garage. The ramp smelled, for the first week, like a deck someone might sit on in the evening with a drink, talking about nothing.

Meredith read the notice a second time.

She noted the retroactive assessment, the undefined term "Common Setback Easement" applied to a private lot's front elevation, the citation of Appendix C for railing materials in a section governing fences. She noted that Form ARA-1 had a processing time, listed elsewhere in the bylaws, of sixty to ninety business days, and that the "fourteen calendar days to comply" would expire well before any application could be reviewed. She noted the signature at the bottom: Douglas P. Whitmore, President, Willowbrook Commons HOA Board of Directors. The signature was practiced. It had a little flourish on the final *e*.

She folded the notice into exact thirds—edges aligned, creases sharp—the way Harold had taught her to fold business correspondence when she was twenty-six years old and he was a junior partner at a firm that still used carbon paper. She picked up the pharmacy bag. She opened the screen door quietly so as not to wake him.

In the second bedroom, which had been Harold's office and was now half office and half the place where the medical supplies lived, she opened the top drawer of the filing cabinet. Forty-one years of tax returns, organized by year, tabbed in Harold's handwriting. 1983 through 2024. Every deduction annotated. Every receipt cross-referenced. She placed the folded violation notice in front of the 2024 tab and closed the drawer.

This was, though she had not yet decided it would be, the first entry in a different kind of file.

Meredith Chen was seventy-one years old. She had spent thirty-three years as an actuary for a mid-tier insurance company in Connecticut before they moved to Willowbrook Commons for the single-story floor plan and the proximity to Harold's pulmonologist. She had spent her professional life calculating what actuaries calculate: the probability-weighted present value of future loss. The expected cost of things going wrong. The mathematics of *when*, not *if*. She was, by training and by temperament, a woman who understood that every system contained the terms of its own failure, and that the failure was never a surprise if you read the tables correctly.

She did not cry. She did not call Michael, who would be angry and loud and want to fly back from Denver to do something imprecise. She did not call the number listed on the notice for "questions regarding this assessment." She did not go to the window and look at the ramp, which Harold used every morning to reach the driveway where he liked to sit and watch what he called the "morning shift"—the joggers, the dog walkers, the school bus at seven forty-five.

She closed the filing cabinet. She went to the kitchen and began preparing dinner: broiled salmon, rice, steamed broccoli cut small enough that Harold could manage it with a fork in his weakened grip. While the rice cooked, she stood at the counter and performed a calculation that required no paper.

The fine was fifty dollars per day, retroactive. The ramp had been installed on September 22. If the fine ran until the next annual meeting—listed in the bylaws as "the first Saturday in November," which this year fell on November 2—the total accrued penalty would be $2,050. If the fine ran until the ninety-business-day processing window for a retroactive ARA-1 application closed, which would be approximately late January, the total would be $6,350. If Harold died before either date—the pulmonologist had said "months, not years," which actuarially Meredith interpreted as a median of four to seven months with a right-skewed distribution—the question of the ramp's necessity would become, in Doug Whitmore's framing, moot.

She served Harold dinner. He woke when she touched his shoulder. He smiled at her the way he always smiled at her, which was the way he had smiled at her when she was twenty-six years old and thought that a man who kept carbon copies of everything was the most trustworthy man she had ever met.

She did not mention the notice.

After dinner, after Harold was asleep again, she went back to the filing cabinet and retrieved it. She sat at Harold's desk with a legal pad and a mechanical pencil—his mechanical pencil, 0.5mm lead, the brand he'd used since 1979—and she began to read Section 4.11.2(b) of the Willowbrook Commons Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions, Amended March 2011.

She read it the way she had been trained to read: not for what the words appeared to say, but for what, under pressure, they could be made to mean.

## II.

Linda Pak was the kind of person whose posture told you she had once been required to sit upright for nine hours at a stretch. Even now, three years after the disgrace, she held herself at the diner booth with the erect stillness of a woman whose spine had been shaped by professional obligation. Her fingers, when they were not occupied, rested on the table in a configuration that Meredith had come to recognize as muscle memory: the home position for a stenotype machine.

They had been having Tuesday lunches for two years and four months. Meredith had begun the tradition the week after the story appeared in the *Willowbrook Courier*—a five-paragraph item, below the fold, under the headline "Court Reporter Dismissed Following Transcript Irregularities." The irregularities were these: during the deposition phase of a custody dispute in which a father's attorney had asked the mother whether she had "ever used physical discipline on the minor child," and the mother had answered "No," and the father's attorney had said "Let the record reflect the witness's answer," Linda Pak—court stenographer for the Twelfth Judicial District, seventeen years, no prior disciplinary actions—had entered into the official transcript:

> [Witness answers in the negative. Witness is crying. Attorney for petitioner does not pause. The child's name is Alma. She is four.]

She had been terminated for cause. The transcript was stricken. The notation was cited in the dismissal letter as "editorialization inconsistent with the duties and ethical obligations of a court reporter, whose function is to produce a verbatim record of proceedings and not to characterize, interpret, or annotate the emotional or contextual circumstances of testimony." Linda had not contested the dismissal. She had, she told Meredith once, understood. "They were right," she said. "That's not what the record is for."

"What is the record for?" Meredith had asked.

"The record is for later," Linda said. "The record is so that afterward, someone can look at what happened and see it exactly. No more, no less."

"And what you wrote—was it exact?"

Linda had looked at her coffee. "Her name was Alma," she said. "She was four."

This was the lunch at which Meredith recruited her.

She did not begin with the violation notice. She began with the salmon, which she had overcooked the night before, and with Harold, who was having a bad week—more fatigue, more difficulty with the fork, a new prescription that made him nauseous in the mornings. Linda listened. Linda always listened. That was, Meredith had come to understand, the thing about stenographers: they were professionals of receptivity. Their entire skill was the translation of other people's words into marks on a page, and the discipline of that skill had made Linda into the most careful listener Meredith had ever met. She did not interrupt. She did not paraphrase. She waited for you to finish and then she said what she thought about the actual thing you had said, not the thing she assumed you meant.

"I need someone who can keep a record," Meredith said.

"Of what?"

Meredith placed the violation notice on the table between their plates. Linda read it. Her fingers, resting on the Formica, shifted slightly—a phantom keystroke.

"This is wrong," Linda said.

"It's wrong in at least four ways I've counted so far," Meredith said. "But I need the wrongness documented. Verbatim. Exactly. Not for the court. For later."

"What's happening later?"

"I'm going to the annual meeting. I'm going to take the HOA board apart piece by piece. And I need someone there who can write down what actually happens in the room, because Doug Whitmore has been writing the record of this community for fourteen years, and his version is the only one that exists."

Linda looked at the notice again. She turned it over, as though checking for additional text. Then she looked at Meredith with an expression that Meredith, who had spent her life reading actuarial tables and not human faces, nonetheless understood completely.

"I still have my machine," Linda said.

Gerald Fortinbras was in the 341.2s—International Law, Conventions and Treaties—when Meredith found him at the Willowbrook branch of the county library. He was not reading. He was shelving. He had been a volunteer at the library for six years, having been politely but firmly declined for paid employment on three occasions. He shelved two shifts a week, four hours per shift, and his shelving was immaculate—spines flush, Dewey Decimal sequence unbroken, misshelved items returned to their correct locations with the silent urgency of a man performing triage.

The librarians tolerated Gerald because his shelving really was excellent and because he had, over six years, never once complained about the work itself. He complained, at length and on a predictable schedule, about the library's signage ("the holds shelf says 'HOLD'S'—possessive—as though the shelf itself owns something"), about the meeting room booking system ("a first-come-first-served policy without a cancellation penalty incentivizes speculative reservations"), and, inevitably, about procedure. Gerald Fortinbras believed in procedure the way other men believed in God—totally, with anguish, and with the constant suspicion that the world had abandoned the faith.

He had been ejected from two meetings of the Willowbrook City Council. The first time, he had risen during public comment to note that the council had voted on a zoning variance without a roll call, in violation of the Ralph M. Brown Act, California Government Code Section 54953(c). He was correct. The council amended the record after the fact. The second time, he had objected to the mayor's use of "general consent" to approve the minutes of the previous meeting on the grounds that general consent requires the chair to ask whether there are objections, which the mayor had not done. "You just said 'Hearing no objections, the minutes are approved,'" Gerald had said, rising from his seat. "But you didn't hear no objections because you didn't give anyone the opportunity to object. The absence of objection is not consent when you have foreclosed the possibility of objection." He was removed by a deputy who called him "sir" in the way that people call you "sir" when they are about to put their hand on your elbow.

The Rotary Club incident was more obscure. Gerald had attended three meetings as a prospective member before raising a point of order during the election of the club's sergeant-at-arms: the constitution required nominations from the floor, but the president had presented a single candidate and called for an acclamation. "An acclamation is not an election," Gerald had said. The Rotary Club of Willowbrook had, in its sixty-three-year history, never had a contested election for sergeant-at-arms. Gerald was not invited to a fourth meeting.

He was a small man—five foot six, thin through the shoulders, with reading glasses that he pushed up his nose with his index finger in a rhythm so regular it appeared autonomic. He wore short-sleeved button-down shirts in colors that had once been patterns and had, through decades of laundering, become suggestions. He was sixty-eight years old. He had retired from a career in municipal code enforcement, a fact that surprised nobody.

"Gerald," Meredith said.

"Section 341.2," Gerald said, not looking up. "Whoever was here last put *The Law of Nations* after *Geneva Conventions: A Commentary*. Alphabetical by title. As if Dewey hadn't thought of that."

"I need to talk to you about a procedural matter."

Gerald looked up. He pushed his glasses up his nose.

She showed him the violation notice. He read it once. He read it again. He turned it over. He held it at arm's length, then close.

"This cites Section 4.11.2(b)," he said.

"Yes."

"Section 4.11.2(b) delegates enforcement authority to the Architectural Review Committee."

"Yes."

"The Architectural Review Committee is constituted under Article VIII, which requires annual reappointment by the board, with terms expiring December 31 of each year."

"Yes."

"When was the committee last reappointed?"

"I don't know yet. That's one of the things I need to find out."

Gerald set the book in his hand—a volume on EU regulatory law—into its correct position without looking at the shelf. His fingers found the gap the way Linda's found the keys.

"What, precisely, are you proposing?"

"I'm proposing that the annual meeting is in four weeks, and that someone needs to ensure it is conducted in strict accordance with the governing documents, Robert's Rules of Order Newly Revised, Twelfth Edition, and applicable state law."

"Someone," Gerald said.

"You've read Robert's."

"I have read Robert's. I have read Robert's in its entirety. I have read the original 1876 edition, the 1915 revision, the 1970 Scott, Foresman edition, the ninth through twelfth editions of the Newly Revised, and the *Robert's Rules of Order In Brief* condensation, which I consider an abomination."

"I know."

"The Twelfth Edition alone is 714 pages. Do you know how many people in the average American homeowners association have read 714 pages of parliamentary procedure? I will tell you. The number is zero. The chairs of these meetings do whatever they want because no one in the room knows enough to stop them."
