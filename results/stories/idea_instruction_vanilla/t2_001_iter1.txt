# What the Fire Left

The thing about losing everything you own in a fire is that people keep trying to give you stuff. Toothbrushes. Donated coats that smell like someone else's basement. A flyer on bright green paper that says **MANDATORY** in letters big enough to read from space, which is how Mom and I ended up at the Elk Ridge Community Center on Christmas Eve eating off paper plates with a man who would later turn out to not technically be a man.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

The community center was the kind of building that looks like it's been apologizing for existing since 1974. Drop ceiling, half the tiles water-stained. Fluorescent lights that made everyone look like they'd been dead for a week, which, given that we'd all been living in the Red Cross shelter at the middle school gym for eleven days, wasn't far off. Someone had put up decorations—tinsel garlands stapled crooked along the cinder-block walls, a Christmas tree in the corner that leaned like it was trying to eavesdrop on the parking lot—and there were long folding tables with paper tablecloths that had little holly berries printed on them, and the whole room smelled like canned green beans and industrial carpet cleaner and, underneath everything, like pig.

The pig was Hambone.

I need to talk about Hambone.

Hambone belonged to Mrs. Gladys Tretter, who was running the dinner because she ran everything at the Elk Ridge Community Center, including a senior water aerobics class and something called "Emotionally Centered Pottery" that I did not ask about. Mrs. Tretter was shaped like a mailbox and had a voice that could cut sheet metal, and she'd brought Hambone because Hambone was her certified emotional-support animal and also because, I think, no one on earth had the horsepower to tell Gladys Tretter she couldn't bring a pig to a Christmas dinner.

He was big. Not cute-pig big. Not teacup-pig big. Hambone was the size of a Labrador retriever who'd made some poor life choices, mottled pink and grey with coarse white bristles and small black eyes that held, I swear to God, *opinions*. He wore a red bandana around his neck that said SUPPORT ANIMAL and he moved through the community center like he had a lease on it, his trotters clicking on the linoleum, leaving a faint trail of slop and moisture everywhere he went because Mrs. Tretter kept "treating" him from a Tupperware container of what looked like oatmeal mixed with applesauce, and Hambone was not a neat eater.

Mom and I sat in the back corner. Mom had her coat on even though it was warm inside, the donated coat, the one that smelled like someone else's life, and she kept pulling the sleeves down over her hands and then pushing them back up again, over and over, like she was trying to figure out whose arms those were. She'd already tried to talk to me six times in the car. I know because I counted. I wasn't talking. I hadn't been talking. Not since the night the fire trucks came and I stood in the parking lot in my socks watching our apartment building turn into something it could never come back from, the windows going orange one by one like eyes opening, and I opened my mouth to scream and nothing came out and nothing had come out since.

The doctors said it was psychological. Mom said it was temporary. I didn't say anything, because that was the whole point.

So I watched.

That's what I did now. I watched everything, because when you stop talking, the watching gets very loud.

I watched the Huang family come in—Mr. and Mrs. Huang and their three boys, who were five and seven and seven because two of them were twins. I watched old Mr. Kopecki, who'd lived on the fourth floor and who kept touching his shirt pocket where he used to keep a photograph of his wife that didn't exist anymore. I watched the Darbys, who'd been fighting since the shelter and were still fighting now, quiet-fighting, the kind where you smile at other people and then turn back to each other with faces like January. I watched a teenage boy I didn't recognize sit down, put in earbuds, and commence the project of not being here, which I respected.

And I watched Dale.

Dale had moved into 2F about four months before the fire. I knew this because Mom had made me bring him a plate of cookies when he moved in, back when I still talked, and he'd answered the door like a man who had never previously encountered the concept of a knock. He was tall. Thin. Blondish, in a way that wasn't quite committed—like his hair was doing an impression of blond and hadn't fully rehearsed. He had a narrow face and wore khakis and a button-down shirt every single day, even to check the mail, even to take out the trash, as if he'd been told that this was the human uniform and was not going to risk deviation. He smiled too much. Not in a creepy way. In a trying way. Like every smile was a quiz he wasn't sure he was passing.

He'd been perfectly nice about the cookies. He'd said, "These are. Yes. Thank you. I eat these." And Mom had said afterward, "Well, he's a little odd," and I'd said, "Mom, he literally said 'I eat these' like he was confirming it for himself," and she'd laughed, and that was four months ago, and I will not talk about those four months because there's a wall in my head and the before-times are on the other side of it.

Anyway. Dale was here tonight because Dale's apartment had burned too. He was sitting three tables away from us, by himself, wearing khakis and a button-down shirt because of course he was, and he looked nervous. Not fire-nervous. Not sad-nervous. Dale looked nervous the way a long-tailed cat in a room full of rocking chairs looks nervous, which is a saying my grandmother used to use, except in Dale's case the rocking chair was a pig.

Dale was watching Hambone.

Hambone was watching Dale.

I saw this. I saw this because I was watching everything, and what I saw was: every time Hambone circuited the room on his Tupperware-fueled rounds, he slowed down near Dale. Not near anyone else. He'd cruise past the Huangs without a glance, trundle by Mr. Kopecki like he wasn't there. But when he got to Dale's table, those little black eyes locked on, and his bristled ears went forward, and something in his pig-brain lit up like a dashboard warning light.

Dale saw it too. Dale watched Hambone the way you watch a wasp that's landed on your arm.

The first incident happened during the opening prayer.

Mrs. Tretter made us all stand, which was a production because folding chairs don't cooperate and Mr. Kopecki's knees don't cooperate and the Huang twins actively don't cooperate, and Mrs. Tretter stood at the front of the room with her hands clasped and said, "Lord, we thank you for bringing us together in this time of trial," and Hambone chose this moment to barrel through the gap between tables four and five, heading for Dale's table because Mrs. Tretter had set her Tupperware slop container down right next to Dale's paper plate.

Dale saw him coming. You could see the calculation—move left, move right, noble sacrifice of the paper plate—but Hambone was faster than he looked and also the floor was damp because Hambone's earlier rounds had left a trail of oatmeal-applesauce residue on the linoleum, and when Dale stepped back his left foot hit the slop trail and went out from under him like he'd stepped on a banana peel in a cartoon.

He went down hard. Knee, hip, elbow, the full sequential impact, and his folding chair went clattering sideways and he hit the table leg and the table jumped and three paper plates slid off the end with a soft shuffling sound, and everyone turned to look, and Mrs. Tretter said, "Hambone, *heel*," which Hambone did not do because Hambone had located the Tupperware and was rooting at it with the single-mindedness of a creature who has never once in his life had a higher calling.

"You okay there, hon?" Mrs. Tretter said.

Dale got up fast. Too fast, actually—he kind of sprang, in a way that didn't look like the way a tall thin man in khakis should move, more fluid than that, more *elastic*—and he said, "Fine, yes, I'm fine, completely fine, I have legs and they work," and he smiled his quiz-smile and everyone went back to the prayer.

But I was watching. And for just a second, maybe two, when Dale was on the ground and the impact was still rattling through him—his left ear changed.

It went *pointed*. Not a little pointed. Pointed like a drawings-of-elves pointed, the cartilage sharpening and elongating and the whole ear sweeping upward by a good two inches, and then Dale's hand flew up to the side of his head and he pressed his palm against it and when his hand came away the ear was normal again. Regular. Roundish. The ear of a man named Dale who wears khakis.

He looked around the room.

No one had seen. They were all bowing their heads, or wrestling children, or staring into the middle distance that was the thousand-yard stare of people who'd lost their homes eleven days before Christmas.

No one had seen except me.

And Hambone.

Hambone had pulled his snout out of the Tupperware and was staring at Dale with those little black eyes, applesauce on his bristles, and I am telling you—I am telling you as someone who watches everything now, as someone who has nothing to do but catalog the world because the part of me that turns observations into words and sends them out my mouth has gone dark—that pig *knew*.

I didn't know what he knew. But he knew it.

The dinner was served buffet-style from a long table against the wall, and it was the kind of food that means well: a turkey that had been bravely cooked by someone who was still learning what turkeys require, a vat of mashed potatoes, green beans from a can, dinner rolls from a bag, a mac and cheese that was actually incredible because Mrs. Tretter had made it herself and whatever else you could say about Gladys Tretter, and you could say a lot, the woman understood cheese.

Mom got me a plate. She didn't ask me what I wanted because she knew I wouldn't answer, so she put a little of everything on it and set it in front of me and squeezed my shoulder and said, "Eat something, Lila, please," and there it was, my name in her mouth with all that worry behind it, and I ate a dinner roll so she'd stop looking at me like that.

Dale was in the buffet line. He was three people behind Mr. Kopecki, holding his paper plate with both hands like a steering wheel, and he was fine, everything was fine, except that Hambone had parked himself directly under the buffet table and was waiting.

Not eating. Not sniffing. *Waiting*.

Dale approached the mac and cheese. He picked up the serving spoon. He was executing a perfectly normal human buffet interaction, and then Hambone came out from under the table like a pink-grey torpedo and headbutted Dale directly in the back of the knees.

The sound Dale made was not a word in any language I know, and possibly not a word in any human language, period. It was a bark and a yelp and a kind of resonant *chirp*, like a tree frog, and he pitched forward into the buffet table and the table slid and the vat of green beans sloshed and Dale's hands went out to catch himself and his paper plate went up and the plate went into the mashed potatoes face-first, and Dale's torso was across the table and his legs were behind him and Hambone was standing there like a prizefighter who'd just delivered a clean hit and wanted the ref to count.

"Oh, for the love of—Dale, honey, I am *so* sorry," Mrs. Tretter said, bustling over. "Hambone! Hambone, we do NOT headbutt guests! That is NOT emotional support!"

Hambone looked at her with zero remorse.

Dale extracted himself from the buffet. He was wearing mashed potatoes on his shirt. A green bean clung to his collar like a little damp epaulette. "It's fine," he said. "Totally fine. Animals love me. It's a—it's a whole thing."

But I saw it.

When he'd hit the table, when the impact had slammed through his ribs and his hands had gone flat on the tablecloth—the skin on the backs of both hands *changed*. It went textured. Pebbled. A grey-green color, like river stones, like the underside of a lily pad, and it climbed from his knuckles to his wrists in a wave, and tiny ridged scales stood out for just a moment before Dale yanked his hands back and shoved them in his pockets and the khaki fabric hid everything.

He turned around, scanning the room.

An old man was trying to cut turkey with a plastic knife. The Darbys were quiet-fighting over the dinner rolls. The Huang twins were building a structure out of green beans. Mom was staring at her plate with the look she got now, the look that meant she was doing math in her head—insurance math, what-do-we-do math, the math of a life that has to be rebuilt from a standing start.

Nobody was watching Dale.

I was watching Dale.

He looked right at me.

It was only a second. His eyes met mine across the community center, and I saw something in them that I recognized, not because I'd seen it before in someone else but because I felt it behind my own eyes every single day since the fire: the terror of being seen, and the terror of not being seen, at exactly the same time.

Then he turned back to the buffet and got a new plate and piled it calmly with food, as if everything about him was regular, as if he had not just briefly been something else, as if Hambone was not still standing six feet away, staring at him like a district attorney building a case.

The gift exchange was Mrs. Tretter's idea, and it was the kind of idea that is generous in theory and chaotic in practice. She'd collected donated presents—wrapped in newspaper because wrapping paper costs money and these were charity gifts and no one was pretending otherwise—and put them in a pile under the leaning Christmas tree, and the plan was that she'd call names and people would come up and pick a gift and everyone would clap and it would feel like Christmas, it would feel like *something*, it would push back against the vast grey nothing that was our collective situation for just a few minutes.

It did not go as planned, but that wasn't Dale's fault. Or maybe it was. Honestly, it was Hambone's fault. Everything was Hambone's fault.

Mrs. Tretter called Mr. Kopecki first, and he went up and chose a small newspaper-wrapped box that turned out to be a travel chess set, and he held it in both hands and his chin did a thing and Mrs. Tretter said, "Merry Christmas, Walter," and he sat down. She called the Huang boys, and they each got a gift—Hot Wheels for the twins, a coloring book for the five-year-old—and Mrs. Huang mouthed *thank you* in a way that filled the whole room and emptied it at the same time. She called one of the Darbys, and then the teenager with the earbuds, who actually took out one earbud for the occasion, which was practically a standing ovation.

She called my name, and Mom nudged me, and I went up. The room felt very large and very bright and I could feel everyone watching me, or not watching me because they were polite enough not to, and I picked a gift at random and brought it back to the table and unwrapped it. It was a journal. Blank pages. I stared at it. Someone in a donation drive had thought: *a kid who's lost everything might want to write things down*. And they were maybe right, but the blankness of those pages hit me like a second fire, all that empty space where something was supposed to be.

Mom rubbed my back. I didn't open the journal.

Then Mrs. Tretter called Dale.
