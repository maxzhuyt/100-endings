# "Every Future We Tried to Preserve": An Oral History of the Yoon-Kessler Temporal Displacement Project, September 2019–March 2021

**Compiled and annotated by Riya Patel, DPhil (Oxon)**

*Published in the Journal of Speculative Physics and Its Historiographies, Vol. 14, No. 2*

**Peer review status:** Accepted with revisions. Reviewers' objections—that the primary source material is, by the compiler's own admission, potentially acausal—are noted and left unresolved. ## Editor's Note

Dr. Patel submitted this manuscript on 11 January 2023, seventeen months after the events she describes and nine months after the dissolution of the research group informally known as "the Shed." She was, at the time of submission, a third-year DPhil candidate in the Department of Physics at the University of Oxford, supervised by Dr. Clara Yoon until Dr. Yoon's leave of absence in March 2021. Dr. Patel has since transferred to the Department of History and Philosophy of Science, where she is, at last report, still enrolled.

The documents presented here have been authenticated to the extent authentication is possible. Several text messages were recovered from devices whose internal clocks display timestamps that precede their manufacture. We have chosen to reproduce them as received.

—The Editors

## Abstract

This paper reconstructs the final eighteen months of the Yoon-Kessler Temporal Displacement Project through primary source analysis, including grant applications (submitted and unsubmitted), transcripts from licensed couple's therapist Dr. Naomi Ashford, text messages recovered from four mobile devices, laboratory notebooks, and first-person interviews conducted by the compiler between June and December 2022. The account focuses on the period from September 2019, when Dr. Clara Yoon (Department of Physics, Oxford) and her husband Martin Kessler-Yoon (Department of English, Oxford Brookes) began construction of an unlicensed temporal displacement prototype in the potting shed behind their home at 14 Boars Hill Lane, Oxfordshire, to the night of 7 March 2021, Martin's forty-third birthday, when the project reached its terminal event. The compiler acknowledges a first-person stake in the narrative and has attempted, where possible, to let the documents speak without interpolation.

**Keywords:** temporal displacement, Yoon-Kessler paradox, self-experimentation, research ethics, dissolution

## I. "A Shed Is Just a Room That Hasn't Been Taken Seriously Yet"

**From: UKRI Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council, Application No. EP/T0337841, submitted 14 September 2019. Principal Investigator: Dr. Clara Yoon. Status: Rejected.**

> **Project Summary:** We propose a thirty-six-month programme to investigate controlled retrocausal displacement of macroscopic objects using a modified Alcubierre framework adapted for closed timelike curves in locally flat spacetime. Preliminary results suggest that the energy requirements for sub-second displacement of objects under 0.5 kg are achievable using commercially available superconducting materials, provided the displacement envelope is constrained to a radius of less than 12 metres.
>
> **Justification of Resources:** The project requests £1.2M over three years to fund one postdoctoral researcher, one DPhil student, equipment and materials, and the conversion of an existing outbuilding at the PI's residence into a suitable laboratory space. The choice of the PI's home as the research site is motivated by the need for continuous monitoring of the displacement field, which requires proximity measured in seconds of response time, a constraint incompatible with commuting to a university facility. The outbuilding in question is a potting shed measuring approximately 4m × 6m, with mains electricity and a concrete floor installed in 2017.
>
> **Ethical Considerations:** No human subjects are involved in the proposed research. All displacement experiments will be conducted on inorganic test objects (brass spheres, ceramic tiles, sealed water samples).

**Reviewer 2 comment, appended to rejection letter, 3 November 2019:**

> The theoretical framework presented here represents either a profound misunderstanding of general relativity or a profound advance in it. I cannot determine which. The PI's CV is impressive—her 2016 paper on topological defects in quantum field theory was genuinely beautiful—but this proposal reads as though she has stepped off a cliff and is, at present, still looking down. I recommend rejection with strong encouragement to resubmit once the PI has decided whether she is falling or flying.

**Interview with Riya Patel (compiler), self-conducted, 4 June 2022. Recorded at Patel's flat, Jericho, Oxford.**

I should explain how I came to be involved, because the question of sequence matters here more than it usually does.

I met Clara in Hilary term 2019. I was a first-year DPhil student nominally working on quantum error correction, and I attended her departmental seminar on what she called "topological leakage"—the idea that information in certain quantum systems doesn't just decohere, it *goes somewhere*. Most of the audience was politely baffled. I was impolitely fascinated. I emailed her that evening. She replied at 2:47 a.m. with a seventeen-page PDF and the message: "Tell me what's wrong with this."

I couldn't find anything wrong with it. I transferred to her supervision that Trinity term.

Martin I met later, at a dinner party at Boars Hill Lane in July. He made a spectacular bouillabaisse and spent most of the evening asking genuine, unhurried questions about everyone else's work. He had that quality—rare in Oxford—of being interested in things without needing to appear clever about them. When I described my research he said, "So you're trying to stop the universe from forgetting things," and I remember thinking: yes, actually, that is exactly what I'm doing.

I didn't see the shed until September, after the grant was rejected. Clara had begun building anyway.

**From the laboratory notebook of Dr. Clara Yoon, Entry 4.11.19–9.11.19. Recovered from the shed, partially water-damaged.**

9 Nov. Test 23. Field at 70%, 9-second sustained burst. Object 4 (brass sphere, 22mm, 47.3g) displaced backward 9 seconds ± 0.3s. Spatial displacement nil within measurement error. Object appeared to exist in duplicate for the overlap interval — **two spheres on the platform simultaneously for approximately 8.7 seconds** before the "original" vanished at the moment the field activated.

Implication: the displacement does not move the object through time. It copies the object to an earlier moment and then erases the original at the moment of activation. Or — and I am writing this with a hand that I notice is shaking — there is no copy. There is one sphere that, from its own reference frame, has simply been *relocated* in time, and the apparent duplication is an artifact of our linear perception of the interval.

Martin arrived at 14:38. We stood in the shed for a long time not saying anything. Then he said, "We need to tell someone," and I said, "Not yet," and that was, I think, the first disagreement.

## II. The Team Assembles

**Interview with Dr. Luca Ferrara, former postdoctoral researcher, conducted 18 August 2022 at a café in Bologna, Italy.**

Clara contacted me in January 2020, just before COVID shut everything down. We'd overlapped at CERN in 2014—I was doing my doctorate, she was a visiting fellow. I trusted her judgment absolutely. When she told me what the device could do, I assumed she was describing a theoretical result. She sent me a video. I watched a brass sphere vanish and reappear. I watched it four hundred times.

She couldn't pay me. There was no grant. She said, "I have a shed, a husband who makes very good coffee, and a machine that breaks causality. Do you want in?" I was on a plane within a week, before the borders closed. I spent the first lockdown sleeping in their guest room, eating Martin's cooking, and helping Clara scale the displacement field from seconds to minutes.

Martin—I should talk about Martin. People want this to be a story about a genius wife and an idiot husband, and that's not what it was. Martin had a DPhil from Brookes in Victorian literature. He was working on a book about George Eliot and determinism, which, given the circumstances, was almost unbearably apt. He understood narrative. He understood consequence. He did not understand the tensor calculus or the modified field equations, and Clara was not good at explaining them because she experienced them as self-evident, the way some people experience music. She would say, "But it's *obvious* that the metric signature inverts across the displacement boundary," and Martin would nod, and I could see him filing it away as metaphor when it was not metaphor. It was the literal geometry of what was happening to spacetime in his potting shed.

He wanted to understand. That's what people miss. He read every paper Clara gave him. He filled notebooks with questions. But the gap was not closeable in the time they had, and I think they both knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person at every meal.

**Transcript excerpt, session 7, Dr. Naomi Ashford, Licensed Couples' Therapist. Clients: Clara Yoon and Martin Kessler-Yoon. 22 July 2020. [Transcript obtained with the written consent of Martin Kessler-Yoon. Dr. Yoon's consent was sought but could not be obtained. See Section VI for discussion.]**

> **DR. ASHFORD:** Martin, last session you mentioned feeling "architecturally excluded" from the project. Could you say more about what you meant by that?
>
> **MARTIN:** It's — yes. The shed was my idea, actually. Did Clara tell you that? Before any of this. I wanted a space for propagation. Seedlings. I'd been reading about heritage tomato varieties. And then one morning Clara said she needed it for an experiment, just for a few weeks, and I said of course, because that's what you do. And then the potting bench came out and the cabling went in and the concrete was poured and the few weeks became months and now there is a machine in my shed that apparently violates the second law of thermodynamics, and I am very, very proud of my wife, and I am also — I am standing in the kitchen listening to her and Luca speak a language I can't learn fast enough, and I can see through the window into the shed, and the light is on, and I am not in it.
>
> **DR. ASHFORD:** Clara?
>
> **CLARA:** What Martin is describing is accurate. I don't dispute it. What I — the thing I can't make him understand, and I realize how that phrase sounds in this room — is that the work doesn't *wait*. The displacement field has a stability window. When it's ready, you run the experiment. You don't schedule it around feelings. That sounds terrible.
