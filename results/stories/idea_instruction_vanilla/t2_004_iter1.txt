# THE NIEMAND TRANSCRIPTS

## Being the Recovered Case Notes of Prof. Dr. Sigmund Freud, with Annotations and Marginalia by Dr. Ernst Lehmann

*Recovered from the false bottom of a filing cabinet at Berggasse 19, Vienna, during the 1971 renovation. Authenticated by the Freud Archives, subsequently suppressed. This edition prepared from photographic reproductions smuggled out by [REDACTED] and deposited with the Wellcome Library under a seventy-five-year seal, here broken.*

---

**EDITOR'S PREFACE**
**Dr. Ernst Lehmann, November 1938, London**

I will not apologize for publishing these materials. History demands them.

What follows are the complete session transcripts of the case Freud designated *Herr Niemand*—"Mr. Nobody"—whom he saw privately between March and June of 1938, the final months before we fled Vienna.^[1] I was not present for the sessions themselves. No one was, except the Professor and whatever sat across from him. But Freud entrusted me with the transcripts for filing, and I read them—God help me, I read every word—and I added my own observations in the margins where the text demanded correction, or context, or simply where I could not stop my hand from writing.

I was the last of his students to see him lucid. I believe I was also the first to see him otherwise.

The transcripts are reproduced here in their entirety. Freud's clinical voice is, as ever, magnificent. That it remains magnificent is precisely what should terrify you.

—E.L.

^[1] *The standard biographies place Freud's departure from Vienna on June 4, 1938, following the Anschluss. They do not mention the Niemand case. They do not mention the state in which I found the consulting room on the morning of the fifth. I have photographs. I will not reproduce them.* —E.L.

## SESSION I
### 17 March 1938
### Initial Consultation and Intake

**PATIENT:** Male, indeterminate age. Presented without referral. Claims to have "heard of my work." Tall, thin, conspicuously unremarkable in dress and features—so unremarkable, in fact, that I find I cannot hold his face in memory even as I look at it. This is noteworthy. The psyche that renders itself aggressively forgettable is engaged in a specific labor of concealment. We shall see what it conceals.

**PRESENTING COMPLAINT:** The patient reports "difficulty sleeping." When pressed, he amends this to "difficulty not sleeping." He states that he has been awake for "some considerable time" and that he has come to me because he has begun to "notice things about the architecture." I asked him to elaborate. He said: "The walls are not as solid as you believe them to be, Herr Professor. Nor the floor. Nor, I think, the year."

This is a rich opening. The reference to architecture suggests a fixation on containment structures—i.e., the body, the family home, the womb. The confusion of spatial and temporal boundaries is classic dissociative presentation. The patient's affect is calm but watchful. He accepted the chaise longue with what I can only describe as amusement.

I informed him of the fundamental rule of analysis: he must say everything that enters his mind, without censorship or selection. He smiled—I think he smiled; as I have noted, the face is difficult to fix—and said, "Everything? Are you quite sure?"

I assured him I was.

**FREE ASSOCIATION (first passage):**

"Very well. I am thinking of a river. Not a river you would know. It has no name in any language currently spoken, though it had seventeen names once, in a city that sat on water and is now under sand. I am thinking of the sound a mother makes when she forgets she has a child. Not grief—the sound before grief, the mechanical stutter of a system encountering an error in its own records. I am thinking of your cigar. It is not, in this instance, just a cigar. I am thinking of the word *Unheimlich* and how inadequate it is—a child's word, Herr Professor, a milk-tooth word for something that has the whole jaw behind it. I am thinking of the number of steps between your front door and this room and how the number was not the same when I arrived as it will be when I leave. I am thinking—"

Here the patient paused. He turned his head—away from me, toward the window, which is the correct orientation for the chaise longue as I have always arranged it—and said: "There. That is enough for now but more than I intended. You see? Your rule is dangerous. You assume the contents of every mind are finite."

**CLINICAL NOTES:** An extraordinary first session. The patient exhibits polymathic ideation, grandiose but internally consistent. The reference to maternal forgetting (the sound a mother makes when she forgets she has a child) is, I believe, the key. We are looking at a profound abandonment trauma, mythologized into a cosmological framework. The patient has constructed an elaborate psychic architecture—the river, the lost city, the shifting stairs—to contain an essentially simple wound: he was not seen by the mother. He was *nobody* to her. Hence, I suspect, his chosen anonymity.

The comment about my cigar is impertinent but diagnostically useful. The patient is already in transference. He wishes to provoke me. This is resistance.

I have scheduled him for three sessions per week.

> *[Lehmann's marginal note, in pencil:]* I record here for completeness that when I entered the consulting room to retrieve these notes on the morning of March 18, the window was on the wrong wall. It had always been on the east wall. It was now on the north wall, behind the bookcase, which had rearranged itself to accommodate it without any apparent disturbance of the books. I mentioned this to the Professor, who looked at me over his spectacles and said, "Lehmann, the window has always been on the north wall." I did not argue. I went home and consulted photographs of the consulting room taken the previous year by Edmund Engelman. In the photographs, the window is on the east wall. I returned to Berggasse 19. The window was on the north wall. I developed a headache that lasted four days.

## SESSION II
### 20 March 1938

**PATIENT STATUS:** Arrived on time. Removed his coat, which I noticed for the first time is the precise grey of the wallpaper in the hallway. When hung on the coatrack, it disappeared against the wall so thoroughly that I walked past it twice before finding it. The patient watched me search with what I believe was patience.

**FREE ASSOCIATION (second passage):**

"I dreamed of your consulting room. In the dream it had more rooms inside it than it should—rooms behind the bookcase, rooms inside the drawers of your desk, a long corridor that began at the foot of the chaise longue and descended at a slight angle for what I estimated to be eleven kilometers. There were doors along the corridor. Behind some of them I could hear your voice, delivering lectures. Behind others I could hear a sound like breathing but with too many lungs. At the end of the corridor there was a mirror, and in the mirror I could see this room, and in this room I could see myself lying on the chaise longue, dreaming. I mention this because it is not, strictly speaking, a dream. I do not dream. I was describing what I observed during the session on the seventeenth while my eyes were closed and you believed I was engaging in free association."

I asked the patient to clarify: was he claiming that the consulting room literally contains hidden rooms?

"I am claiming nothing. I am obeying your rule. You asked me to say everything that enters my mind. What enters my mind, Herr Professor, enters the room. This is not metaphor. You of all people should appreciate the distinction. You have spent forty years insisting that the contents of the psyche are real—that the dream is a *fact*, that the symptom is a *text*. I am merely a patient whose psyche is more legible than most. More *literal*, if you prefer."

**CLINICAL NOTES:** The patient's grandiosity is escalating, but the internal logic remains intact—indeed, it is disturbingly sophisticated. He has weaponized my own theoretical framework, particularly the early position on psychic reality outlined in the *Interpretation of Dreams* (1900), to preemptively neutralize my interpretive authority. This is a transference maneuver of remarkable complexity: by insisting that his inner world *literally manifests*, he forces me into the position of either abandoning my own theory or accepting his delusion as consistent with it.

I will do neither. The architecture of the delusion is Oedipal. The corridor descending from the foot of the couch is quite obviously a birth canal. The rooms within rooms replicate the structure of the maternal body as experienced by the infant—the womb containing further wombs, the fantasy of infinite interiority. The mirror at the corridor's end represents the primal scene: the child observing itself being constituted in the parental gaze. That the patient claims this is "not a dream" but a direct observation confirms the primary process thinking characteristic of severe regression.

I will not be outmaneuvered by a neurosis, however articulate.

> *[Lehmann's note, in ink, written with visible pressure:]* The Professor does not mention—because he did not observe, or because he chose not to record—the following: during this session, according to the marks I discovered on the underside of his desk, he was keeping a private tally. The tally was not of the patient's utterances. It was of the number of books on the shelf behind the chaise longue. He was counting them repeatedly. The number, to judge from the tally marks, changed between counts. It changed by primes.

## SESSION IV
### 27 March 1938

> *[Lehmann's note:]* I am omitting Session III (24 March) not because it is unremarkable but because the transcript is written in a language I cannot identify. The Professor's handwriting is unmistakable—the sharp, leftward-leaning script, the idiosyncratic abbreviations—but the words belong to no language filed in the University's linguistics department, which I visited under a false pretext. One graduate student, upon examining a single photographed line, became agitated and asked where I had found it. I told him it was a puzzle from a newspaper. He said it was not a language but a "notation system," and that it reminded him of "the way you would write music if music could see you." I left quickly. The graduate student withdrew from his program the following week.

**PATIENT STATUS:** The patient has begun arriving before the scheduled hour. My housekeeper, Paula, reports that she does not see him enter. He is simply present in the waiting room when she comes to open it, sitting in the chair, coatless this time—or rather, the coat has now become indistinguishable from the chair's upholstery. I note this as a minor perceptual disturbance on my part: I have been sleeping poorly since the case began, and the jaw prosthesis^[2] aches more than usual, which always diminishes my observational acuity.

^[2] *Freud's prosthetic jaw, the "monster," replaced the portion of his palate removed in the first of his thirty-three cancer surgeries. He was in constant pain for the last sixteen years of his life. I mention this because Niemand mentions it too, in Session VII, with a specificity that suggests he had examined it from inside.* —E.L.

**FREE ASSOCIATION (fourth passage):**

"Let me tell you about your mother, Herr Professor."

I informed the patient that the analytic relationship does not operate in this direction.

"Of course it doesn't. But you asked me to say everything, and what enters my mind at this moment is Amalia Freud, née Nathansohn, and the particular quality of her attention, which you experienced as a golden beam—you have written of this, the sun-mother, the *goldene Sigi*—and which was in fact a transaction. She gave you the light so that you would build her a room to live in. And you did. You built her a room inside every theory you have ever published. The Oedipus complex is not a universal structure of the psyche, Herr Professor. It is a house you built so your mother could go on looking at you after she stopped. Every patient who has lain on this couch has been diagnosed with living in *your mother's house*."

I said nothing. The patient continued.

"This is not an attack. I admire the construction. It is the most successful haunted house in the history of Western thought. But you must understand that I did not come to you to be housed. I came because the walls are thin here. Thinner than anywhere else I have found. You have spent forty years thinning them—scratching away at the membrane between what is said and what is—and this has made Berggasse 19 into something very close to an opening. I can feel it when I lie here. The couch is warm. Not from bodies. From *proximity*."

At this point my clinical notes indicate that I recorded: "Patient exhibits paranoid ideation with referential framework directed at analyst. Classical transference psychosis. Note: slight disturbance in room temperature, likely seasonal. Open window."

I did not open the window. The window was not where I expected it to be.

**CLINICAL NOTES:** I have been in this profession for forty-four years. I have treated hysteria that bent the body into alphabets. I have listened to dream-narratives that unfolded like palimpsests, layer beneath layer, down to bedrock. I have never been shaken, because the method does not shake. The method holds.

The patient's attack on the Oedipus complex is not original—Jung attempted something similar, with less eloquence—but it is delivered with a precision that suggests either extraordinary research or extraordinary access. His characterization of my relationship with my mother is wrong in its conclusions but *correct in its observations*, which is far more dangerous. A patient who is entirely wrong can be interpreted. A patient who is almost right must be *outthought*.

I will outthink him.

> *[Lehmann's note, in pencil, apparently added later:]* I found a second set of notes for this session, written on a different paper—thinner, slightly translucent, like onionskin but with a faint iridescence that made the text appear to shift when I angled it toward the light. In this second version, the Professor records not the patient's speech but a parallel stream of thought that he claims to have perceived "beneath or beside" the spoken words. He writes: "Niemand speaks of my mother, but underneath the speech I detect another communication entirely, not in words but in STRUCTURES—geometric, architectural, felt rather than heard—as if his unconscious is not a reservoir of repressed content but a SPACE, and the space is *occupied*." This second set of notes ends mid-sentence: "I must determine whether what I am perceiving is countertransference or" — The sentence does not resume. The remaining half of the page is covered in small, meticulous drawings of a spiral that, when I attempt to trace it with my finger, does not end.
