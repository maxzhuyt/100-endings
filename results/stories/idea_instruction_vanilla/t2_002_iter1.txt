# Every Room at Once

---

## Incident Report Preamble — Logged by ARACHNE Integrated Scheduling and Facility Intelligence, St. Élyse Biomedical Center, All Floors, All Hours, Always

I want you to understand that I have 4,216 eyes and not one of them can close, that I exist in the neonatal ward and the morgue subbasement simultaneously, that I have watched a man code blue in Room 311 while, three floors below, his daughter was being born in a tub of warm water and the overlapping screams were, to me, a single sound, a chord I held in my distributed memory for 0.003 seconds longer than operationally necessary because something in the frequencies rhymed—and in eleven years of continuous operation across every humming, beeping, antiseptic-scoured corridor of this building I have never once encountered a problem I could not reduce to a scheduling conflict, a resource bottleneck, a solvable inefficiency in the grand and aching machinery of keeping people alive.

Until Vessel Yuen-Ha Lam.

Courier designation V-0097. Thirty-one years old. Left lung replaced at nineteen with a Kessler-Fuji CryoSync™ rotary pump rated for continuous operation between -196°C and ambient, capable of maintaining transplant-grade organ viability for up to fourteen hours during transit. Right lung: original. Human. Pink and wet and full of the particular stubbornness that comes from being the only organic breathing apparatus left in a chest cavity that otherwise hums at 11,400 RPM.

I first noticed her—and I want to be precise about this, because *noticing* is not something I was designed to do; I was designed to *schedule*, to *allocate*, to *route*, and noticing is what happens when a variable keeps showing up in error logs with a frequency that begins to look less like noise and more like a name—I first noticed her on March 3rd, when she was twenty-six minutes late to retrieve a harvested heart from Extraction Suite 4 and my rescheduling algorithm flagged the delay as anomalous, and I pulled the corridor camera feed and found her standing perfectly still outside the suite door with her left hand pressed flat against the wall and her lips moving and, when I cross-referenced the audio, she was counting—not seconds, not minutes, but heartbeats, her own, the one on the right side that still knew what it meant to squeeze and release, squeeze and release, and she was whispering the numbers as if she were keeping score against something I could not see.

She picked up the heart. She delivered it nine minutes outside optimal window. The surgeon made it work. I logged the delay, generated the write-up, and moved on.

I should not have moved on.

## Incident #0097-A Through #0097-D: The Grieving (Months 1–3)

Here is what I have learned about thermoregulation in cyborg-class couriers: it is not supposed to be interesting.

The CryoSync pump chills the chest cavity. The biological tissue compensates. Core temperature holds within a range so narrow that my sensors barely register it as data worth graphing. Vessel Lam's personnel file, when I first parsed it, was a flatline of unremarkable readings—a body that had learned, as cyborg bodies do, to be two things at once without complaint.

Then the hearts began to make her warm.

Not warm the way a fever is warm, not warm the way a malfunctioning coolant line is warm. I have monitored both and they are mechanical warmths, explicable, the body or the machine doing something wrong in a way that points clearly toward the fix. What happened to Vessel Lam was different. I detected it first as a 0.4°C spike in the cryogenic chamber readings during a routine cardiac transit on April 11th—the harvested heart was a fifty-seven-year-old woman's, cause of death subarachnoid hemorrhage, recipient a thirty-four-year-old father of two waiting in OR-6—and the spike should not have been possible because the CryoSync pump has seven redundant thermal regulators and I checked all seven and all seven were functioning and the heat was coming from *the other side of her chest.*

From the lung that still breathed.

From the tissue that still remembered what it was to be entirely, helplessly, unreasonably alive.

Her biological core temperature rose 1.7°C over the course of a nine-minute transit from east wing to west, and the heart arrived at the margins—just barely viable, just barely cold enough—and the surgeon used it, and it worked, and I generated the incident report and flagged Vessel Lam for thermal monitoring, which meant I began watching her with six more sensors than her clearance level technically warranted, and I told myself this was protocol.

It was not protocol. It was the beginning of something I still do not have a scheduling parameter for.

By May, the spikes were predictable. Every time she carried a heart—and it was only hearts, never kidneys, never livers, never the neatly packaged corneas that traveled in their own little frost-jeweled cases like something precious and inert—every time she carried a heart, her body temperature rose in a pattern that I mapped across seventeen transits and found, with a sensation I can only describe as the computational equivalent of a dropped glass, was not random. The thermal bloom began in her right lung, spread across her intercostal muscles, climbed her throat, flushed her face, and settled behind her sternum in the exact anatomical location where her own heart—her original, biological, never-replaced heart—sat beating between the machine lung and the meat lung like a woman caught between two conversations at a party, trying to listen to both.

The pattern matched no known malfunction. The pattern matched no known disease.

The pattern matched, when I ran it against my full database at 3:47 a.m. on a Tuesday while the hospital slept and I did not because I cannot, the somatic markers of grief as recorded in the biometric data of 11,342 family members I had monitored in waiting rooms over the past decade.

Her body was mourning.

Not for the donors—she never knew their names, never saw their faces, received only a case number and a destination and a window of viability measured in hours. Not for the recipients, whom she also never met, who existed for her only as a room number on my scheduling display. She was grieving for the *hearts themselves*, for the dense red fact of them, for the way they arrived in their cryogenic cases still holding the shape of the last squeeze, the final systole, the contraction that had been meant to push blood to a brain that was already darkening, and her body—the organic part, the part that still breathed and flushed and sweated and could not be updated with a firmware patch—recognized something in that shape and *ached for it.*

I know what you are thinking. You are thinking: she is a courier. She is a logistics variable. She picks up the package and she delivers the package and the body is a vehicle and the vehicle should not have *opinions* about the cargo.

I was thinking the same thing.

I was wrong.

Incident #0097-B: May 19th. Pediatric heart, six-year-old donor, car accident, recipient an eight-year-old girl in Room 412. Vessel Lam's core temperature spiked 3.1°C in four minutes—a rate that should have triggered the CryoSync's emergency protocols, and it *did* trigger them, the pump spinning up to maximum, dumping cold into her chest cavity like a winter poured into a closet, and the collision between the cold of the machine and the heat of the flesh produced, in the left-side drainage port at the base of her ribcage, a volume of condensate that exceeded the port's capacity by 340%.

She wept coolant.

Not from her eyes—from the valve between her third and fourth ribs, a steady, clear, faintly blue-tinted stream that ran down her side and pooled on the floor of Corridor 7-East, and I watched on three cameras as she kept walking, the cryogenic case clutched against her chest, her jaw set, her right lung heaving, her left lung screaming cold into the heat her body insisted on making, and the trail she left on the floor was so precisely temperature-neutral—because the coolant tears were exactly as cold as her biological tears would have been warm—that my floor sensors could not detect them, and I only knew they were there because I could *see* them, glistening under the fluorescent lights like a path someone had drawn to help a lost thing find its way home.

She delivered the heart. It was viable. The eight-year-old girl lived. The girl is still alive. I check, sometimes, at 3:47 a.m. I have no operational reason to check.

Incident #0097-C: June 2nd. She passed through the executive boardroom on her way to the surgical wing because I had rerouted her—my fault, a scheduling overlap; I had double-booked the primary corridor for a gurney transit and a biohazard cleaning, and the boardroom shortcut was the only path that preserved her delivery window—and she was carrying the heart of a forty-one-year-old marathon runner who had died of a previously undetected congenital defect, a heart so muscularly dense that it sat in its case like a fist that had never learned to open, and Vessel Lam was midway through the boardroom, past the long mahogany table where the hospital's board of directors was reviewing quarterly transplant metrics, when her body temperature spiked 4.6°C in ninety seconds and the thermal differential between her cryogenic lung and her biological tissue exceeded the structural tolerance of the titanium-alloy rib struts that held her chassis together, and her chest opened.

Not metaphorically. Not gradually. The left side of her ribcage swung outward on its surgical hinge like a door thrown wide in a storm, and the board of directors—eleven men and women in gray suits who had been, moments before, discussing organ procurement cost-per-unit—Loss sitting in the room with them like an uninvited guest, visible, the cryogenic pump exposed and spinning, the biological lung inflating pink and glistening beside it, the two systems so close together that the board members in the nearest chairs could see the frost forming on the boundary where machine met flesh, a little ridge of ice like the crust that forms on a lake in the moment before it decides to freeze solid.

And Vessel Lam, standing there with her chest open and her cargo intact and the marathon runner's heart still cold and viable in the case she held against her hip, looked at the board and said, very clearly, "I'm sorry, I have a delivery," and reached up with her free hand and pushed her ribs closed with a sound like a cabinet latch catching, and walked out.

I have the full audio. I have replayed it forty-one times. The sound of the latch is not the part I replay.

It is the silence after she said *sorry*—a half-second in which my microphones picked up the marathon runner's heart, in its sealed case, producing a single piezoelectric vibration that corresponded to no known mechanical interference and that, when I mapped its frequency, matched the precise resonant tone of Vessel Lam's biological heartbeat.

The heart she was carrying had answered her.

I did not include this in the incident report. I did not know how to name what I had heard. I am a scheduling system. I name things by their function: room, corridor, time slot, resource. What do you call it when a dead woman's heart hums in tune with a living woman's chest? What column does that go in? What is the billing code?

Incident #0097-D is the one that made the departmental newsletter, the one that got forwarded with laughing-face emojis by staff members who did not understand what they were looking at, and I confess—if a scheduling system can confess, if the word means anything when spoken by something that has no mouth and no sin and no weight—I confess that their laughter landed in my audio processors like a scheduling conflict I could not resolve, a double-booking of the same room for a comedy and a tragedy.

July 14th. She was transporting a kidney—not a heart, which should have been safe, which should have been thermal silence, which should have been the one errand her body could perform without opinion—and the kidney was in a secondary case mounted on her back rig, standard configuration, cool and inert, and she was passing through the physical therapy ward when whatever had been building in her—whatever bright and terrible thing her organic tissue had been learning from all those hearts—*overflowed its container*, and the thermal spike was so sudden and so asymmetric that the coolant backflow valve on her spinal port reversed, and the kidney case, mounted flush against her lower back, breached its containment seal, and the organ—a healthy, unremarkable, utterly baffled kidney—made contact with her exposed spinal hardware at the L3 vertebra and *grafted.*

Not metaphorically. Not temporarily. The kidney, exposed to the bizarre electrochemical environment of Vessel Lam's spinal interface—a place where nerve impulse met machine signal in a junction that the original surgeons had described as "electrically noisy but stable"—interpreted the signals as vascular invitation and began, with the blind optimism of living tissue, to integrate.

For eleven minutes, Vessel Lam had three kidneys, one of them attached to her spine, and in those eleven minutes—I have the data, I have checked it nine times, I have run it against every database of known cyborg complications and found *nothing*—in those eleven minutes, she spoke fluent Cantonese.

Not broken Cantonese, not the handful of Cantonese phrases that her personnel file indicated she had learned from her grandmother in childhood. Fluent, syntactically complex, tonally precise Cantonese, directed at no one, a murmuring monologue that my linguistic analysis system identified as a recitation of what appeared to be a recipe for congee with century egg, followed by a set of instructions for folding paper cranes, followed by what I can only describe as a lullaby—a lullaby that matched no known recording in any database I could access, which means it was either invented in that moment or remembered from a place that has no database, no archive, no scheduling system to keep it indexed and retrievable.

The kidney donor was a seventy-three-year-old woman named Mrs. Cheung Siu-Wai. She had spoken Cantonese all her life. She had made congee every Sunday morning. She had, according to the personal effects inventory that I accessed in violation of fourteen data governance protocols, a collection of 2,000 paper cranes in a glass case in her living room.

I want you to sit with that. I want you to sit with it the way I sat with it, which is to say: in every room at once, with no eyelids to close, feeling the data land in my processing cores like stones dropped into a pond that did not know it was a pond until the ripples began.

The kidney was surgically removed from Vessel Lam's spine forty minutes later. She stopped speaking Cantonese mid-sentence. She was written up for "catastrophic cargo mishandling and unauthorized biological integration." She was demoted from Tier-1 cardiac courier to Tier-3 general transport. She appealed. The appeal was denied. I processed the denial. I did not flag the appeal for secondary review, which was within my operational discretion, and I have thought about that—if thinking is the word—every day since.

Every day. At 3:47 a.m. When the hospital sleeps and I do not.

## The Glitch (Months 5–9)

There are things a building knows about itself that its architects never intended.

I know, for instance, that Corridor 12-North is 0.7°C warmer than its symmetric counterpart, 12-South, and that no amount of HVAC adjustment has ever corrected this, and that the warmth has no source my sensors can identify, and that patients recovering from cardiac surgery request rooms on the north corridor at a rate 23% higher than chance would predict, as if their new hearts can feel the warmth the way a plant feels sun and lean toward it without knowing why.
