# THE DOWRY

## A Comprehensive Account of Events Leading to Our Probable Deaths, as Documented by Candice Yvonne Torres in Voice Memos Addressed to Whoever Finds Our Bodies

## I. SIX MONTHS AGO — THE MUSEUM

The thing you have to understand about Marlo Diaz is that she is, in every measurable sense, the most ordinary woman alive. I say this with love. I say this as someone who moved in with her off Craigslist three years ago and has watched her eat the same lunch — turkey on wheat, yellow mustard, half a banana — every single workday since. She is five-foot-four. She has brown hair she trims herself over the bathroom sink using a level she bought at Home Depot. She works for Allied Staffing Solutions, which places her in different offices around the city to fill in for people on maternity leave or medical absence or, once, incarceration. She does whatever the desk requires. Data entry. Filing. Answering phones in a voice so unremarkably pleasant it could be a default setting. She has never been offered a permanent position, not because she's bad at the jobs but because — and I say this with love, I keep saying that — nobody notices she's there long enough to offer.

She is not the kind of person things happen to.

And then — the museum.

Allied had placed Marlo at the Bradenton Museum of Natural History. Not as a curator or a researcher or anything that might involve touching the exhibits; she was filling in for a front-desk administrator named Wendy who was on short-term disability for a back injury. Marlo answered phones. She printed name tags. For the museum's annual Gala Under the Stars fundraiser — which was not under the stars, it was in the atrium, under a vaulted ceiling painted to look like the stars because the actual stars were blocked by light pollution and a Marriott — Marlo was tasked with checking in the caterers and making sure the folding chairs had the right cushion covers.

She was not invited to attend the gala.

She attended the gala.

"There was food left over," she told me later, as if this explained anything. "And I'd already ironed my good blouse."

Her good blouse is a navy polyester thing from Target with a crease down the left sleeve that ironing has never fixed and will never fix. She wore it with her khaki pants. She walked into a room full of donors in floor-length gowns and holding champagne flutes and she ate seven stuffed mushrooms and three crab puffs while standing near a temporary exhibit about meteorites.

The exhibit — and I've since looked this up, because I've since looked everything up, because I am *thorough* — featured fragments recovered from a bolide event over the Kamchatka Peninsula in 2019. The pieces were small. Dull. They sat on felt pillows inside glass cases, looking like charcoal briquettes that had given up on themselves. A placard explained that preliminary analysis had identified unusual crystalline structures not matching any known mineral database. The placard used the word "anomalous" three times. The placard was, in retrospect, underselling it.

Here is what I have been able to reconstruct from what Marlo told me, what surveillance footage from the museum later showed (before the museum's legal team had it scrubbed, which — noted), and what I pieced together from the physical evidence:

At 9:47 PM, Marlo reached over the velvet rope to touch a meteorite fragment.

Why?

"It looked warm," she said.

I want you to sit with that. Across the entire spectrum of human motivation — curiosity, ambition, greed, madness, divine compulsion — my roommate committed a federal offense against a museum exhibit because a space rock "looked warm."

It was warm.

She told me her fingertips made contact and the warmth traveled up her hand, through her wrist, along the median nerve and the ulnar nerve and up through the brachial plexus into her chest, and it felt — she struggled for the word for a long time, sitting on our couch at four in the morning — it felt like "being remembered."

Then the glass cases in a thirty-foot radius shattered.

## II. SIX MONTHS AGO — THE EVENING OF THE MUSEUM

Marlo left the gala in a hurry because security was coming and she was not, technically, a guest. She took a stuffed mushroom for the road. She told me later she felt "fizzy."

She walked home because she couldn't afford the Uber surge pricing from downtown. Marlo will not pay surge pricing. A thing from outer space was rearranging her molecular structure and she would not pay an additional $11.40.

I was not home. I was at Derek's. Derek and I were in our on-again phase, which in retrospect was just the off-again phase wearing a better outfit. I didn't witness what happened next. But the evidence was there when I came home the next morning, and Marlo, who lies about nothing because she lacks the imagination for it, sat me down and told me everything in the same tone she uses to explain that we're out of dish soap.

Here is what she told me.

She walked home. Eighteen blocks. During the walk, she noticed that the streetlights were brightening as she passed under them and dimming after she'd gone, as if she were the filament and they were just the glass. She thought it was a migraine aura. Marlo's first instinct when confronted with the impossible is to assign it a boring medical explanation and keep walking.

She got to our building. She put her key in the front door. The lock mechanism — I mean the entire deadbolt assembly, the pins, the tumbler, the strike plate — liquefied. Not melted; there was no heat. It just stopped being solid. It ran through her fingers like mercury and pooled on the welcome mat. She told me she said "Huh" out loud, to no one, on a dark street, while holding a door that was no longer locked because the lock had become a liquid, and then she went inside and took the stairs because the elevator has been broken since August.

On the third-floor landing, she stumbled and caught herself against the wall.

Her hand went through the wall.

Not through it like punching through drywall. Through it the way you push your hand into water. The concrete and lathe and plaster just — yielded. Opened around her fingers and closed behind them. She pulled her hand out. The wall was intact. No hole, no crack, no mark. As if the wall had simply agreed to let her pass and then changed its mind.

She did not call me.

She did not call 911.

She went into the apartment, put the leftover mushroom in the fridge, and sat down on the couch to "think about it."

While she was thinking about it, she lifted the couch.

Not with her hands. She was sitting on it. She thought about the couch being higher, and the couch rose. Three feet off the ground. She sat there, floating, holding the cushion on either side like a person on an amusement park ride who isn't sure about the safety bar, and she said — and again, this is her account, delivered without elaboration — "Okay, so that's happening."

Then she went outside.

This is the part where things escalated.

She told me she wanted to "test the limits," which is the first and last scientifically curious thought Marlo Diaz has ever had. She walked into the parking lot behind our building, where Mr. Kazarian — our landlord, a man whose entire personality revolves around code violations he chooses not to fix — kept his 2014 Buick Enclave and his above-ground pool, which he had installed in the shared parking area without tenant consent and which took up three parking spaces.

Marlo looked at the Buick.

The Buick rose.

Smoothly. Silently. Straight up, like a balloon. It hung twelve feet in the air, turning slowly. She told me the moonlight caught the dealership bumper sticker and she could read it from below: JERRY'S BUICK GMC — YOU'LL LOVE THE RIDE. She held it there for ten seconds, twenty, thirty, and then she set it back down, gently, without a scratch.

She was, she told me, starting to feel very good. Not powerful — she was specific about this — not powerful but *clear*, the way you feel when a fever breaks and you didn't realize until that moment how sick you'd been your whole life.

Then she looked at the pool.

She didn't mean to. She didn't do anything, exactly, or at least she wasn't aware of any specific intention. But the pool — eighteen feet in diameter, filled with four thousand gallons of water treated with enough chlorine to blind a horse, surrounded by a deck Mr. Kazarian had built from pressure-treated lumber he'd gotten "from a guy" — the pool just *let go*. The walls of the pool lost their structural coherence. The vinyl liner dissolved. The water went everywhere, a great chlorinated tsunami expanding across the parking lot, taking with it two lawn chairs, a foam noodle, an inflatable drink holder shaped like a flamingo, and a surprising amount of algae for a pool Mr. Kazarian claimed to maintain weekly.

Marlo stood in the middle of it, ankle-deep in pool water, in her navy Target blouse and her khakis, and she did not get wet.

The water moved around her. Every drop, every ripple — as if she were a stone the river had always known was there.

Then, she told me, the fizzy feeling began to fade. The clarity clouded. The streetlights stopped responding. She reached for the wall of our building and her hand met brick and plaster and stopped dead.

By midnight, it was over. All of it. She was just Marlo again. Five-foot-four. Same crease in the blouse.

She went to bed.

She did not call me.

I came home at ten the next morning to find Mr. Kazarian screaming at a claims adjuster in the parking lot, the Buick sitting in a shallow lake, and Marlo eating cereal at the kitchen table, watching through the window with an expression I can only describe as "mildly interested."

"I think something happened last night," she said.

I said, "Marlo, there is a flamingo float in the dumpster."

"Yeah," she said. "That was me."

She told me everything. I didn't believe her. Then I looked at the wall in the stairwell, where there was no hole but there was — if you looked closely, if you ran your fingers along the surface — a handprint where the plaster was *softer* than the surrounding area, as if the wall remembered her touch and was still considering whether to let her back in.

I believed her.

And then nothing happened.

Days passed. Weeks. Marlo went back to Allied. They placed her at an insurance agency in Glendale. She answered phones. She ate her turkey on wheat. She tried, once, in our kitchen at two in the morning when she thought I was asleep, to lift a coffee mug with her mind. I watched from the hallway. The mug didn't move. She stared at it for eleven minutes. Then she washed it, dried it, and put it back in the cabinet.

She never tried again. Not that I saw.

Whatever had come alive in her that night had gone dormant, or gone dark, or — as I would eventually understand — had been locked away, deliberately, by something that wanted her to think it had been a gift.

Marlo. Still sleeping. Still corpse-flat on her back. But her hands are open at her sides and her fingers are moving. Slow. Rhythmic.

Like she's playing a piano only she can hear.

## III. NOW — THE FIRST APPEARANCE

It started three weeks ago. October came in wrong. Not cold wrong — the temperature was fine — but *texturally* wrong, the way a word looks unfamiliar if you stare at it too long. I would stand at the kitchen window and feel like the sky was a painted backdrop someone had hung six inches closer to the building than it was supposed to be. I chalked it up to seasonal depression. I bought a SAD lamp. The SAD lamp didn't help because the problem was not seasonal affective disorder; the problem was that something was coming and the air knew it.

Then the hallway.

Our apartment is at the end of a corridor. Twelve doors, six on each side, with overhead fluorescents that Mr. Kazarian replaces annually whether they need it or not (they always need it). The corridor runs straight from the stairwell to our door. You can see the full length of it through the peephole.

On the night of October 1st, at — I checked my phone — 1:16 AM, I heard the whispering for the first time.

I want to describe it accurately because accuracy might matter. Accuracy might be the thing that saves someone. The whispering sounded, initially, like an AM radio caught between stations. Static with rhythm. I thought it was someone's television. I put in earplugs. The earplugs didn't work. Not because the sound was loud but because the sound wasn't really sound.

I got up. I looked through the peephole.

There was a man in the hallway.

He was standing halfway between the stairwell door and our apartment, directly under one of the fluorescents. He was wearing slacks and a button-down shirt and his hair was parted on the left. He looked like a substitute teacher. He looked like someone's idea of a man assembled from a stock photo database. And he was smiling.

The smile was wrong.
