# The Posture of Receiving

## I.

The taxi left her at the wrong piazza. She knew it was wrong because the driver had been certain it was right, and because certainty, in Saeki's experience, was the posture of error—chin lifted, shoulders locked, the whole body organized around a single brittle point.

"Piazza dei Limoni," he said again, gesturing at the square. There were no lemon trees.

Saeki paid him. She stood with her single suitcase on the cracked flagstones and watched the taxi labor up the road until it vanished behind a wall of limestone the color of old teeth. The town—Pietrascura, her brother's town, which she had never visited and had thought about precisely once a year for eleven years on the anniversary of the phone call—climbed above her in a series of terraces that looked, from this angle, like a face that had been punched repeatedly from the left side.

She consulted the hand-drawn map. Her brother's notary had faxed it: a series of lines and arrows that might have been directions or might have been the cardio-output of a small animal in distress. The notary's handwriting leaned so dramatically to the right that it seemed to be fleeing from itself.

She turned the map sideways. Then upside down. Then she folded it into her coat pocket and chose the alleyway that went uphill, because the dead live above the living—this was something her teacher had said once, and it had turned out to be literally true in most Mediterranean hill towns, where the cemeteries crowned the ridge.

The alleyway was three shoulders wide and smelled of wild fennel and cat urine. Its walls were limestone, the same limestone she would see for the next four weeks in every direction, at every turning, in her sleep—a pale, faintly gold stone that held the day's heat long into the night and gave back nothing. She passed a doorway where an old woman sat shelling fava beans into a plastic basin. The woman looked up.

"Your hips lead your spine," Saeki said, in Italian that was technically correct and emotionally catastrophic. "This is why your hands shake."

The woman stared. Saeki continued uphill.

She found the notary's office forty minutes later, having passed it twice. The notary was a young man named Cataldo who wore his belt too high and tilted his pelvis forward when he sat, which Saeki could see would give him lower back pain within the decade but which she did not mention because she needed something from him.

"Your brother," Cataldo said, unlocking a metal filing cabinet, "was very respected here."

"By whom."

"By everyone." He paused. "Well. By two families in particular. The Ferros and the Basiles."

He said this the way one might say *the cliff and the sea below it*—a paired inevitability, one hard, one deep, and between them a fall.

"They each have asked to host you," he added. "For dinner."

"Both."

"On the same night. Tomorrow." He placed a folder on the desk. The folder was thin. Her brother's estate, it seemed, amounted to: a two-room apartment above a butcher shop, an unpaid water bill from 2011, a wardrobe containing seven white shirts and no pants, and a safe-deposit box at a bank in Catania whose contents were listed only as *documenti personali*.

"There are no pants," Saeki observed.

Cataldo blinked. "Excuse me?"

"In his wardrobe. Seven shirts, no pants."

"Perhaps he was—well. Your brother was an unusual man, Signora Saeki."

"Evidently."

She signed the documents. Cataldo's pen was expensive and his grip was wrong—he held it like a knife, all force and no guidance. She did not comment. She took the keys to the apartment, the address written in Cataldo's fugitive handwriting, and stepped back into the limestone maze.

It took her an hour and fifteen minutes to find the apartment. It was a seven-minute walk.


---

## II.

The apartment smelled of anise and closed windows. She opened both windows, which looked out onto the piazza—the correct piazza, Piazza Ferrante, though it had no sign—and found herself staring down at two cafés positioned on opposite sides of the square like seconds in a duel.

On the left: Caffè Ferro. Green awning, four metal tables, an espresso machine visible through the open door that was older than most democracies. A heavyset man in his sixties sat outside reading a newspaper with the focused hostility of someone looking for his own name.

On the right: Caffè Basile. Blue awning, four metal tables, an identical espresso machine that appeared to have been purchased specifically to match. A thin woman in her fifties stood in the doorway with her arms crossed, watching the heavyset man not look at her.

Between them, the piazza—thirty meters of flagstone, a dry fountain whose basin held a single orange traffic cone, and a cat asleep in the last triangle of sunlight.

Saeki watched for eleven minutes. In that time, three people entered the piazza. The first, a teenage boy on a motorino, veered to the left side, parked, entered Caffè Ferro, and came out with a small paper bag. The second, an old man with a cane, came from the right alleyway, entered Caffè Basile, spoke briefly to the thin woman, and left an envelope on the counter. The third, a young woman with a baby, entered from the upper street, paused in the exact center of the piazza, appeared to weigh something, and then turned left.

The thin woman uncrossed her arms. Crossed them again.

Saeki understood rackets. Not through experience but through structure. A protection racket is a posture—the body says *I will cause the pain I am offering to prevent*. The efficiency of the lie depends on the gap between stance and strike, between the implied threat and the actual capacity for violence. In Kyokushin, one learned to read this gap the way a linguist reads the silence between words. In most people, the gap was all there was.

She closed the windows. She unpacked her suitcase: two changes of clothes, a toiletries bag, three books on Okinawan history she would not read, and a framed photograph of her brother that was twenty-three years old, in which he was twenty, grinning, wearing—she noticed now—a gi she did not remember him owning.

She set the photograph on the windowsill facedown, so it reflected only the ceiling.

Then she tried to light the gas stove.

The gas stove required a match. She did not have matches. The pilot light procedure, as described in faded Italian on a card taped to the wall, involved turning a knob to a symbol that might have been a flame or might have been a ghost, holding the knob for ten seconds, then applying flame to a aperture she could not locate. She tried for six minutes. The apartment filled with the smell of gas but not fire. She turned the knob off, opened the windows again, and went downstairs to find food.

She emerged into the piazza. The heavyset man looked up from his newspaper. The thin woman straightened in her doorway.

Saeki stood between them, on the flagstones, and considered each café with the blank, unhurried attention she had once given to opponents in the ring—which is to say, she did not look at their strengths. She looked at where they were empty.

She went right.


---

## III.

The thin woman was Donna Teresa Basile. She had the posture of someone who had been told to stand up straight so many times in childhood that she had developed a permanent, militant extension of the spine, every vertebra at attention, a life lived at two centimeters taller than she actually was.

"Signora Saeki," she said. "We have been expecting you."

"I got lost."

"Your brother used to say that Pietrascura was designed by a drunk man running from a sober wife. Please—sit. Coffee. Or something to eat? We have arancini, just made. My daughter-in-law's, from the recipe your brother loved."

My brother, Saeki thought, did not cook and did not eat fried food when I knew him. But people change in twenty-three years, or they become people who cannot be contradicted because they are dead, which is easier.

She sat. "Coffee."

The coffee came in a cup the size of a thimble and was strong enough to be a moral position. Donna Teresa sat across from her, and within four minutes, Saeki had learned the following: the Basile family had seven members across three generations; they ran the café, a small construction firm, and "other interests"; they had loved Kenji—her brother—because he had once protected Donna Teresa's son Marco from "a situation" that was not described further; and they considered his death, from a fall in Catania two years ago, to be "a loss to the whole town."

"A fall," Saeki said.

"From a balcony. Third floor. It was very sudden."

"Falls tend to be."

Donna Teresa's eyes did something Saeki recognized—a contraction around the orbicularis oculi, the muscle that tightens when a lie is being maintained under pressure. Not the lie itself. The effort of the lie. Like a stance held too long.

"He spoke of you often," Donna Teresa said.

"What did he say."

"That you were a great warrior. That you had fought in tournaments across Japan. That you had the discipline of—how did he say it—'a sword that has decided not to cut.'"

Saeki drank her coffee. Her brother had seen her fight once, at eighteen, in a regional tournament in Saitama. She had lost in the second round to a woman who outweighed her by fifteen kilos and who hit like a door slamming. She remembered the loss more clearly than any victory because her teacher had said, afterward, *Good. Now you know what your body does when it has no choice*, and she had realized he meant the loss was the lesson. The victory would have taught her only that she was fast enough not to learn.

"He exaggerated," Saeki said.

"All brothers do. More coffee?"

"No." She paused. Across the piazza, the heavyset man had put down his newspaper. He was watching. "Your arancini. Tomorrow I will try them."

She left money on the table. Donna Teresa refused it. Saeki left it anyway, tucked under the saucer, which is a posture in itself—the money that stays says *I am not in your debt*.

She crossed the piazza. She could feel Donna Teresa watching from behind and the heavyset man watching from ahead, and she walked the way she had once been taught to walk in the dojo—as if the ground were already meeting her feet, as if she were not approaching anything but allowing the world to arrive.

She did not go to Caffè Ferro. She walked past it, past the man and his newspaper, and into the alleyway beyond, and she got lost again immediately.


---

## IV.

She found the pharmacy on her third attempt, having first found a church and then a different church. The pharmacist was a young woman with excellent posture and an immediate, transparent anxiety about Saeki's presence that suggested she had already heard of her.

News in a town this size, Saeki understood, did not travel. It was already everywhere, like limestone.

She bought aspirin and a box of matches.

On the way back, she tried to light the stove again. She found the aperture this time—hidden under a cast-iron grate encrusted with something that might have been tomato sauce or might have been older than tomato sauce. The match caught. A blue ring of flame appeared with a sound like a small, satisfied exhalation. She stared at it. She had nothing to cook.

She turned it off.

A knock at the door. She opened it to find a boy of perhaps twelve holding a covered tray.

"From the Ferros," the boy said.

Under the foil: pasta with sardines, a generous portion, still hot. A folded note: *For Signora Saeki, with the compliments of Salvatore Ferro. Your brother ate with us every Thursday. We hope you will do us the same honor. Tomorrow, 8 pm.*

She ate the pasta standing at the window, looking down at the piazza. It was good. The sardines were fresh and the fennel had been cooked just past resistance, the way her brother used to cook—

No. Her brother did not cook. She kept forgetting this, or rather, she kept importing the brother she had known into the brother who had lived here, and they did not fit. The brother she had known was twenty years old, restless, leaving. The brother who had lived here was a man she had never met who owned seven shirts and no pants and who had fallen from a third-floor balcony in Catania with a finality that the Italian police had ruled *accidentale* and that she had not questioned because questioning it would have required her to admit she had not spoken to him in nine years before he died.

She finished the pasta. She washed the plate. She set it by the door to return.

In the piazza below, the cat had moved to the other triangle of sunlight, which was on the Ferro side now. The thin woman—Donna Teresa—was watching the cat.


---

## V.

The next evening, she went to the Ferros first.

Salvatore Ferro was the heavyset man from the newspaper. Up close, he was larger than he had appeared—a man who had been strong and was now merely big, the muscle running to mass, the joints beginning to calcify. She could see it in his shoulders: the left one was frozen at about 140 degrees of flexion, an old injury compensated for so long that the compensation had become the architecture. He moved like a building with a cracked foundation—still standing, but everything slightly off-true.

His house was behind the café, through a courtyard where a lemon tree grew from a crack in the pavement with the desperate optimism of all plants in impossible places. The table was set for eight. Around it: Salvatore's wife Maria, a woman whose posture was so accommodating, so organized around the absence of threat, that Saeki felt a physical urge to tell her to take up more space; their son Enzo, who was thirty and sat like he was waiting for an order; Enzo's wife, whose name Saeki did not catch and who carried plates back and forth from the kitchen with the efficiency of someone who knew the meal was a performance; two cousins or uncles whose relationship to Salvatore operated on the level of gravity rather than speech; and at the end of the table, a very old man who was introduced only as *il Nonno* and who said nothing for the entire meal but watched Saeki the way cats watch the space a bird has just left.

"Your brother," Salvatore said, pouring wine, "saved my life."

"How."

"In 2014. A disagreement with some men from Palermo. They came to the piazza. Your brother—" He shook his head. "He stepped between us. He spoke to them. I don't know what he said. But they left."

"My brother was persuasive."

"He was more than persuasive, Signora. He was—" Salvatore searched for the word. "—*immovabile*. You understand? Like a wall. They could not move him because he did not resist. He just stood there."

Saeki set down her fork. This was the first description of her brother that sounded true—not the tournaments, not the warrior, but the standing still. Her brother had always been able to stand still in a way that made other people's motion look foolish. It was the one gift they shared, though he had used it differently.

"The Basiles," she said mildly, "also speak well of him."

The table went quiet. Not silent—there was still the sound of cutlery, the distant motorino, the old man's breathing—but the quality of the air changed. Salvatore's wife touched her napkin. Enzo looked at his plate. The uncles-or-cousins performed a synchronized study of the middle distance.

"The Basiles," Salvatore said, "did not know your brother."

"They say he protected Marco Basile."

"Marco Basile." Salvatore said the name the way one might say *a cockroach in the soup—*with controlled, precise disgust. "Marco Basile is a thief and a liar, and your brother would not have crossed the street to spit on him."

"And yet."

"And yet what?"

"And yet his mother's arancini are excellent."
