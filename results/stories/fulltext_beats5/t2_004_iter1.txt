# The Hateful Immortals

## A Documentary Account by Dr. Amelia Thornton, Yale Program in Gifted Studies, 2026

---

## I. Permanent Record

The facts are these, and they are not in dispute.

On the fourteenth of October, 1847, at a meeting of the Boston Female Anti-Slavery Society held in the parlor of one Mrs. Caroline Healey Dall, a nineteen-year-old English immigrant named Margaret Greenbriar was introduced to a twenty-two-year-old Chinese-American dockworker named James Chen. The introduction was made by the abolitionist lecturer William Lloyd Garrison, who had published, three months earlier, a pamphlet titled *On the Gifted Among Us: Their Obligation to the Cause of Universal Liberty*, in which he argued that individuals possessing what we now call primary manifestations — heritable, spontaneous, and irreversible alterations to human capacity emerging at or near puberty — bore a special moral duty to the abolition of slavery, as they, more than anyone, understood what it meant to be marked by biology for a life not chosen.

Margaret's gift had manifested at fourteen. Total eidetic recall. Not the parlor trick version — not the ability to memorize a deck of cards or recite a poem after a single reading, though she could do both — but the comprehensive, involuntary, and sensorily complete retention of every moment of conscious experience from the instant of manifestation forward. Every conversation. Every meal. Every face on every street in every city. The taste of the rain the day she arrived in Boston. The particular yellow of the curtains in Mrs. Dall's parlor. The smell of James Chen's coat — damp wool, pipe tobacco, and something briny from the harbor — when Garrison brought him across the room and said, "Miss Greenbriar, I believe you and Mr. Chen share what I might call an uncommon persistence."

James's gift had manifested at sixteen. Biological indestructibility. His bones could not break. His skin could not be permanently pierced. His cells did not degrade. He had discovered this, as the archival record confirms, when he fell from a mast rigging at the Boston shipyard where he worked and struck the dock from a height of forty-seven feet, sustaining no injury beyond a scuffed knee that healed in under a minute, an event witnessed by eleven dockworkers, a foreman, and a horse.

They were married on the sixth of March, 1849, at a small ceremony in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The officiant was a Unitarian minister named Ezekiel Pond, whose notes on the service — preserved in the Harvard Divinity School's collection of ministerial memoranda — include the following observation: "The bride appeared possessed of uncommon composure. The groom appeared possessed of uncommon joy."

They are still married.

This is not a study of superpowers. The literature on primary manifestations is vast, and I have contributed to it elsewhere, and I will not rehearse it here. What I wish to examine is narrower and, I think, stranger. This is a study of a marriage — one specific marriage — and of what that institution becomes when its most fundamental structural assumption is removed.

"Till death do us part."

The phrase is so familiar that we have forgotten it is a *load-bearing wall*. It is the clause that makes the contract of marriage survivable. Not because spouses are always longing for each other's deaths — though the literature suggests this is more common than politeness allows — but because the knowledge that the commitment is finite is what permits the commitment to be total. You can promise everything to someone because "everything" has a boundary. You will die. They will die. The promise will end. This is not a flaw in the institution. It is the institution's *engine*. The marriage vow is an act of courage precisely because it is an act of mortality: you are pledging the *remainder* of a limited resource. To say "I will love you until I die" is to say "I will love you with the only thing I have that is real, which is the fact that I will not be here forever."

Margaret Greenbriar cannot forget.

James Chen cannot die.

The escape clause has been nullified.

I have spent four years with their letters, their legal filings, their rare interviews, the secondhand accounts of friends and acquaintances spanning nine generations. I have read Margaret's 1911 notebook — a document I will discuss at length — in which she attempted, in 437 pages of increasingly anguished handwriting, to catalog every argument they had had to that date, a project she abandoned not because she ran out of arguments but because she ran out of a reason to believe the catalog would help. I have listened to the 2019 audio recordings in which she speaks, in a voice that sounds roughly thirty and carries an accent that has not existed in natural usage since the 1880s, about the specific weight of her husband's hand on her shoulder in a photographer's studio in 1851.

A daguerreotype exists from this session. It is held in the private collection of the couple, who permitted me to view it but not to reproduce it. In the image, Margaret stands in the formal style of the era, unsmiling, her hands clasped at her waist. James stands behind her, slightly to the left, his right hand resting on her right shoulder. His expression, to the extent that the daguerreotype's long exposure and the conventions of the period permit interpretation, might be called *proprietary*. He appears to be a man who cannot believe his luck.

When I asked Margaret, during our third interview, about the photograph, she was quiet for a long time. Then she said: "I can still feel the weight of that hand. I don't mean I remember that I could feel it. I mean I feel it now. The pressure. The warmth. The way his thumb moved, once, just slightly, against my collarbone. It is 1851 in that part of my body. It has never stopped being 1851."

I asked her if this was a comfort.

She said: "Dr. Thornton, I can also feel it on the morning he told me he wished I would learn to let things go, and on the afternoon I threw a plate at his head in Kuala Lumpur, and on the night he walked out of our house in Reykjavik and did not come back for nine months. I feel all of his hands. All of the time. You cannot select which version of a person touches you when the past does not have the decency to become the past."

James, when I relayed this comment to him — with Margaret's permission — said only: "She always had a way with words. That was the first thing. That was the very first thing."

He looked at his hands.

A note on method and a note on the world.

The method: I am a mortal scholar studying immortal subjects. I will die, by actuarial estimate, in approximately forty years. Margaret and James will not. This means I am interpreting, from inside finitude, an experience of infinitude that I cannot access any more than a fish can access the experience of flight. Every observation I make in this account is inflected by the thing I have that they don't: an ending. I am aware of this bias. I cannot correct for it. I can only name it and proceed.

The world: for the reader unfamiliar with the history of primary manifestations, a brief orientation. Gifts, as they are colloquially called, have been documented since at least the twelfth century, though reliable records begin only in the 1600s. They are genetic in origin, unpredictable in expression, and wildly variable in utility. They emerge at puberty, affect roughly one in eleven thousand individuals worldwide, and range from the extraordinary (Margaret's recall, James's indestructibility) to the bafflingly specific (the well-documented case of Edith Morley of Shropshire, whose gift, manifesting in 1793, was the ability to determine the exact number of seeds in any visible sunflower). Gifts are publicly known, socially managed, and, since the Gifted Persons Recognition Act of 1952, legally protected. They are not, despite what the cinema would have you believe, the basis of a secret paramilitary infrastructure. There are no teams. There are no nemeses. There are only people — ordinary people, with ordinary problems — who happen to be *stuck* with capacities that warp the ordinary shape of a life.

Margaret and James are not the most powerful gifted individuals on record. They are not the most famous. A 2018 Gallup poll on gifted-person name recognition placed them forty-third and fifty-first, respectively, behind a man in São Paulo who can photosynthesize and a woman in Lagos who has not needed to sleep since 1987.

They are, however, by a considerable margin, the most *married*.

## II. The Architecture of Accumulation

They went to Paris in the spring of 1867, three months after they had left St. Louis under circumstances that had become, by then, familiar. A neighbor had commented to Margaret, at a church social, that she must share her husband's remarkable constitution, as neither of them appeared to have aged a day in the four years since their arrival. Margaret had smiled, and deflected, and come home to James, and they had begun the quiet, systematic process of severing every tie they had built in four years — canceling subscriptions, settling accounts, writing letters of farewell that explained nothing, packing their belongings into trunks that grew heavier with each relocation because Margaret could not bear to leave behind the objects that anchored her memories to the physical world, even though the memories themselves required no anchoring.

Paris was to be different. Paris, Margaret wrote in a letter to her sister in 1868 — her mortal sister, who would die of typhoid in 1874 and whose death Margaret can still recount in a level of physiological detail that I have chosen not to reproduce here — was "a city that does not ask you to be the same person on Tuesday that you were on Monday." They rented an apartment on the Rue de Sèvres, in the Seventh Arrondissement, four floors up, with a narrow balcony overlooking a courtyard in which an old chestnut tree grew. Their neighbors were artists, radicals, expatriates, and drunks, several of whom were all four simultaneously, and none of whom seemed particularly interested in the ages, origins, or biological peculiarities of the quiet couple on the fourth floor.

They were happy.

I state this plainly because the documentary record supports it and because the story I am telling requires the reader to believe it. Margaret's letters from the Paris decade — I have read all of them, 312 in total, addressed to her sister, to friends in Boston and London, and, in a few cases, to James himself, slipped under his study door when speech had become insufficient — are, by any critical standard, incandescent. She writes about the quality of the bread at the boulangerie on the corner of the Rue de Sèvres and the Rue de Babylone. She writes about the afternoon light in the Luxembourg Gardens, where she and James walked on Sundays with a regularity that she describes, in a letter dated November 1869, as "the only ritual I have ever loved." She writes about a dinner party at which James, who is not by nature a storyteller, told the table about his childhood in a fishing village in Guangdong Province, and the room went quiet, and Margaret watched a French painter weep into his Bordeaux, and she thought: *This is the man I married. This is the man.*

James, whose letters from this period are fewer and more reticent, wrote to a friend in San Francisco: "Margaret likes it here. I like Margaret when she likes things."

They made friends. They attended salons. James, who had taught himself to paint in the flat, idle years after the Civil War — a conflict he had survived in a manner that the 23rd Massachusetts Volunteers found deeply unsettling — began to produce small, precise watercolors of Parisian street life that were, by the assessment of a gallery owner on the Rue de Rivoli, "competent." Margaret, who had begun teaching English to the children of French academics, was known in the neighborhood as the woman who never forgot a name, a birthday, or a promise, which made her beloved by the children and slightly feared by their parents.

They were happy, and they knew — they always knew — that the happiness was on a timer.

It was not the timer of mortality. It was the timer of *legibility*. They did not age. After ten years in a place, this became impossible to conceal. The neighbors who had been charmed by the ageless English-woman and her ageless Chinese-American husband in 1867 would, by 1877, be made uneasy by them, because a decade without visible change in a human face begins to feel less like good health and more like *evidence*. Evidence of what, exactly, was never clear — this was an era before the formal classification of gifted individuals, and superstition filled the taxonomic void — but the effect was the same: after a decade, give or take, the couple would feel the social atmosphere curdle, and they would leave.

The Paris departure was scheduled for March 1878.

I quote Margaret's account with her permission, noting that James, when asked for his recollection of the incident, said: "I don't remember any particular argument about a tree."*

The trunks were packed. The apartment was nearly bare. Margaret had taken the curtains down from the bedroom window — she had sewn them herself, from fabric bought at a market in the Sixth, in a shade of green she describes in the notebook as "the green of the underside of a leaf when the sun is behind it, not in front of it, and the leaf is young" — and she was standing at the bare window, looking into the courtyard, when she saw the chestnut tree.

It was in bud.

Not yet blooming. But close. Perhaps a week away, Margaret estimated, from the full flowering she had watched every spring for ten years, each iteration stored in her memory with the fidelity of a botanical illustration. She had watched the tree bloom in 1868, her first spring in the apartment, when she and James had stood on the balcony together and he had said, "It looks like it's celebrating," and she had thought that was the most wonderful thing anyone had ever said about a tree. She had watched it bloom in 1869. In 1870, during the siege, when they had stayed because James could not be killed and Margaret could not bear to leave. In 1871, 1872, 1873, 1874, 1875, 1876, 1877. Nine blooms. She wanted the tenth.

She told James she wanted one more week.

James said they could not stay one more week. The arrangements were made. The new apartment in Vienna was expecting them. More to the point, Madame Deschamps on the second floor had begun asking pointed questions about Margaret's complexion, and the concierge had made a comment about James's "remarkably preserved" appearance, and the window of safe departure was closing.

Margaret said she understood all of that. She asked for one week.

James said — and here I am quoting Margaret's notebook, which records the statement with the precision of a court stenographer, including the observation that he was standing near the door, that his left hand was resting on the doorframe, and that he was wearing the brown vest she had bought him in 1872: "Margaret, it is a tree. You have seen it bloom nine times. You will see other trees bloom in other cities. This sentimentality is precisely the thing that makes these departures harder than they need to be."

They left the next morning. The tree had not bloomed.

Margaret records: "He is not wrong that I will see other trees. He is wrong that they will be this tree. He is wrong that I will see them for the first time. He is wrong that the hardness of departure is a problem to be solved rather than a grief to be borne. But I could not say any of this, because he would not remember my saying it, and I would remember his not remembering, and the argument would become another entry in a catalog that only one of us is keeping."
