# The Reykjavik Frequency Heist

## Part One: Static

**2019**

The camera finds him in a house that smells of coffee and wool and the particular staleness of rooms where old men sit too long. Magnús Þorsteinsson is eighty-one years old, and his hands have the permanent tremor of a man who spent decades turning dials measured in fractions of a millimeter. He sits in a chair by the window, and beyond him Reykjavik harbor is a grey smear against a greyer sky, and he does not look at the interviewer when he begins to speak.

"You want to know about the signal," he says. "Everyone who comes here wants to know about the signal."

He lifts his coffee. His fingers rattle the cup against the saucer with a sound like a small, tired bell.

"I will tell you what I have told all of them: I was a radio engineer. I built things that listened to the sky. That is all I was, and if you want to make me into something else—some kind of hero, some kind of criminal—then you have misunderstood the story before it has begun."

A pause. The clock behind him ticks. On the wall, framed in black wood, there is a photograph of six people standing in front of a truck loaded with absurd, tangled equipment. The photograph is dated June 1971. One of the figures—tall, angular, her hair a bright defiant mess against the volcanic landscape—is circled faintly in pencil, as if someone had tried to mark her and then thought better of it.

"Elísabet," he says, noticing the interviewer's gaze. "Yes. That's her. That's the woman who ruined my life and saved—"

He stops. Drinks. Sets the cup down.

"I will tell you what happened. But you must understand: I did not believe in any of it. Not at the start."

---

**1971**

The letter arrived on a Tuesday in February, which Magnús would later consider appropriate, because nothing good ever happened on a Tuesday in Reykjavik in February. The sky was the color of a bruise. The wind off Faxaflói bay had been punishing the city for three days straight, and the radiators in his workshop at the Icelandic National Broadcasting Service were producing a sound he could only describe as "tubercular."

The letter was written on University of Iceland stationery, in handwriting so aggressive it appeared to have been produced in conditions of active warfare.

*Magnús Þorsteinsson,*

*I have been told you are the best radio frequency engineer in Iceland. I have also been told you are a coward, a pedant, and an insufferable bore. As two of these qualities are useful to me and the third may be overcome, I would like to meet. Wednesday, 3 pm, Café Hressó. I will be the woman who is not knitting.*

*Dr. Elísabet Jónsdóttir*
*Department of Experimental Physics*

Magnús read the letter twice. He then folded it carefully, placed it in his desk drawer, and resolved to ignore it. He was, at thirty-three, a man who had organized his life to exclude surprises. He maintained his equipment with a devotion that bordered on the liturgical. He ate the same lunch every day—smoked lamb on dark bread, an apple, black coffee—and he was at peace with the knowledge that he would continue to eat this lunch until he died. The National Broadcasting Service paid him adequately to keep Iceland's radio transmitters functioning, and if the work was not glamorous, it was at least comprehensible. Signals went out. Signals were received. The world made sense in the language of watts and wavelengths.

He went to Café Hressó on Wednesday at 3 pm.

He would spend the next forty-eight years asking himself why.

---

She was not what he expected, though he could not have said what he expected. She was perhaps forty, tall and sharp-featured, with a quality of restless intensity that he associated with poorly grounded electrical equipment. She wore a heavy Icelandic sweater over what appeared to be a laboratory coat over what appeared to be another sweater. Her hair—copper-red, wild—seemed to exist in a state of permanent disagreement with gravity.

She was sitting at a corner table with a cup of coffee and a folder thick enough to serve as ballast for a small vessel. She looked up when he entered, assessed him with the efficiency of someone checking a circuit for faults, and apparently found him adequate.

"Sit," she said.

"Dr. Jónsdóttir—"

"Elísabet. Sit."

He sat.

"You work with long-wave radio," she said. "Specifically, you have experience with atmospheric signal propagation in sub-Arctic conditions, you have published a paper on very low frequency reception anomalies in volcanic terrain, and you rebuilt the Gufuskálar VLF navigation transmitter in 1968 after the winter storms. Correct?"

"I—yes."

"Good. I need you to help me steal something."

Magnús blinked. In his experience, physicists were strange, but they were not usually thieves.

"Steal what?"

Elísabet opened the folder. Inside was a mess of charts, spectrograms, handwritten equations, and what appeared to be seismological readings from the Krafla volcanic system in northeast Iceland. She spread them across the table with the enthusiasm of a general laying out battle plans, knocking her coffee cup sideways. Magnús caught it. She did not notice.

"In September last year," she said, "a colleague in the geology department placed a set of geophone arrays around the Krafla caldera. Standard seismic monitoring—magma chamber activity, microearthquakes, the usual. The equipment included a broadband receiver intended to capture electromagnetic emissions from the volcanic system. Magnetotelluric surveys. Nothing exotic."

"I know what magnetotelluric surveys are."

"Of course you do. That's why you're here and not someone interesting." She tapped a spectrogram. "This was recorded on November 14th, 1970, at 02:17 local time. The broadband receiver captured this signal embedded in the electromagnetic noise from the caldera. Look at it."

Magnús looked at it.

He was quiet for a long time.

"That's impossible," he said.

"Yes."

"That's a modulated carrier wave."

"Yes."

"In the very low frequency band. Coming from inside a volcano."

"Keep going."

"The modulation pattern—" He pulled the spectrogram closer, tilting it toward the window light. His training took over, the deep comfort of reading patterns in signals, the language he understood better than Icelandic. "This is amplitude modulation. This is—Elísabet, this looks like a radio broadcast."

"It is a radio broadcast."

"From inside Krafla."

"From inside Krafla."

"That is absurd."

"I agree completely."

Magnús stared at her. She stared back with the patient ferocity of a woman who had been staring at absurd things for months and had come out the other side into a country beyond absurdity, a place where the only rational response was action.

"My colleague captured six more instances of the signal between November and January," she continued. "Each lasting between forty seconds and twelve minutes. Each on approximately the same frequency—16.4 kilohertz—though with slight drift. Each exhibiting the same modulation characteristics. And I have spent the last two months analyzing these recordings, Magnús, and I can tell you exactly what they contain."

She reached into the folder and produced a cassette tape. She placed it on the table between them with the care of someone handling an explosive device.

"I demodulated the signal and cleaned it as best I could with the equipment available to me at the university. The signal-to-noise ratio is appalling. The recordings are fragmentary. But what I recovered—"

She pressed her lips together. For the first time, Magnús noticed something in her expression that was not confidence or impatience. It was fear.

"What you recovered," he prompted.

"News broadcasts," she said. "Radio news broadcasts. In Icelandic. Describing events that have not happened yet."

---

**2019**

"I know what you're thinking," Magnús says. "You're thinking: this old man has lost his mind. Or: this old man is telling a story. A fantasy."

He adjusts his glasses. Behind them, his eyes are pale and clear and entirely serious.

"I thought the same thing when she told me. I thought: this woman is brilliant, clearly, but she is also insane. There are, after all, logical explanations. The signal could have been a reflection—a skip propagation artifact bouncing a real broadcast from some distant station into the Krafla monitoring equipment. The 'future' content could have been coincidence, or misinterpretation, or deliberate fraud."

He holds up a finger.

"But I listened to the tape. And on the tape—buried in static, broken, barely audible—a man's voice was reading the news. A news bulletin. And the news was dated. The voice said: 'This is Ríkisútvarpið, the Icelandic National Broadcasting Service, the date is March the seventh, 1971.' Remember—this was recorded in November 1970. Elísabet played it for me in February 1971. March the seventh was three weeks away."

He pauses.

"The bulletin described a fishing trawler, the *Jón Sigurðsson*, running aground near Vestmannaeyjar in heavy seas. No casualties. A minor story. A nothing story. And I sat in that café and I thought: this is an elaborate joke.

"On March the seventh, 1971, the *Jón Sigurðsson* ran aground near Vestmannaeyjar in heavy seas. No casualties."

The clock ticks.

"I stopped laughing," Magnús says.

## Part Two: Carrier Wave

**1971**

In the weeks that followed, Magnús learned several things about Elísabet Jónsdóttir.

First: she had been denied tenure twice, not because her physics was poor but because her physics was so heterodox that no committee could evaluate it without suffering what she cheerfully described as "an institutional seizure." Her work concerned the electromagnetic properties of magma under extreme pressure, and specifically the theoretical possibility that certain geological formations could function as natural waveguides—channels that could trap and propagate electromagnetic signals across distances and, she argued, across boundary conditions that conventional physics considered inviolable.

"You mean time," Magnús said, during their third meeting, which took place in her university office, a room so catastrophically cluttered that he suspected it was being used as a storage facility by several departments simultaneously.

"I mean time," she confirmed, without embarrassment.

"Time is not a boundary condition."

"Magnús, you have a very narrow conception of what time is."

"I have a *sane* conception of what time is."

"And that is precisely why I need you. Your sanity will keep us grounded while we do the insane thing."

The insane thing, as Elísabet described it, was this: the fragmentary recordings captured by the university's monitoring equipment were tantalizing but insufficient. The signal appeared at irregular intervals, lasted unpredictable amounts of time, and was captured by equipment not designed for the purpose. What she wanted—what she needed—was a dedicated reception operation. Purpose-built antenna arrays. Proper receivers tuned to the exact frequency. Recording equipment capable of capturing every second of every transmission.

She wanted, in short, to build a listening station at the edge of the Krafla caldera and steal every signal the volcano would give them.

"Why do you keep saying 'steal'?" Magnús asked. "If the signal is naturally occurring—"

"Because it isn't ours," Elísabet said. "Think about it. These are broadcasts from our own national radio service. Dated in the future. Someone, or something, or some *process*, is sending them backward through the volcanic system. If we intercept them, we are taking something that was not intended for us. We are, in the most literal sense, stealing tomorrow."

"You are romanticizing what might be a mundane atmospheric phenomenon."

"And you are hiding behind caution because the alternative terrifies you. Which is fine. I need a terrified engineer. Terrified engineers check their work."

Second thing he learned about Elísabet: she was almost always right about people, and she wielded this knowledge without mercy.

---

The team assembled over the course of April. There were six of them in total, and Magnús privately considered it the least impressive collection of conspirators in the history of conspiracy.

There was Elísabet herself—visionary, tyrant, impossible.

There was Magnús—skeptic, engineer, apparently incapable of saying no to a woman who insulted him.

There was Guðmundur Árnason, "Gummi," a fisherman from Akureyri who was also, improbably, the best antenna rigger in the north of Iceland, having spent twenty years maintaining the long-wave radio equipment on herring trawlers. He was sixty, nut-brown, taciturn, and possessed of the deep calm of a man who had survived six North Atlantic winters on vessels that should not have floated. He had been recruited, Elísabet explained, because he could build an antenna array from scrap metal and cursing.

There was Sigríður Helgadóttir, a postgraduate student in Elísabet's department, who appeared to be approximately nineteen years old and who vibrated with the particular anxious energy of someone who worshipped her thesis advisor and was terrified of disappointing her. She would handle the recording equipment.

There was Pétur Magnússon, a radio hobbyist and conspiracy theorist from Kópavogur who owned more receiving equipment than the Icelandic military and who had been, in Elísabet's words, "the only person in the country who believed me without requiring proof, which tells you everything about both his character and his epistemology." Pétur was small, nervous, and prone to monologues about government surveillance. He would provide the receivers.

And there was Friðrik Hallgrímsson, who was never satisfactorily explained. He appeared to be in his fifties, spoke with the precise diction of a career diplomat, and possessed an extraordinary ability to obtain things—permits, equipment, fuel, information—through channels that Magnús strongly suspected were not legal. When Magnús asked Elísabet where she had found him, she said only, "Friðrik finds people. Not the other way around."

They met for the first time as a group in Elísabet's office on April 12th. Rain hammered the windows. Elísabet stood at a chalkboard on which she had drawn a map of the Krafla region, the caldera marked with a circle that looked, Magnús thought, disturbingly like a target.

"The signal appears to originate from a point approximately four kilometers east of the main caldera," she said. "There is a secondary fissure system there, largely inactive, where the electromagnetic emissions are strongest. We will establish our listening station at this location." She tapped the map. "We will arrive in early June, when road conditions permit access. We will have, at most, three weeks before the geology department's summer field season begins and the area becomes cluttered with scientists who will ask questions we cannot answer."

"Why can't we answer them?" Pétur asked. "This is the most important discovery in—"
