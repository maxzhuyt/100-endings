# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo

## As Compiled, Annotated, and Published by Enomoto Ryōichi
### Translated from the Japanese by Watanabe Chiyoko

---

## Publisher's Note

Enomoto Ryōichi, former All-Japan Kendo Champion (1978, 1980, 1982) and runner-up in the 1983 Tenka-Ichi Invitational Tournament, delivered this manuscript to our offices on the fourteenth of September. He was seventy-nine years old and suffering from advanced pancreatic cancer. He gave precise instructions regarding the typesetting, the paper stock, and the order of his annotations. He asked that nothing be altered. He died on October the first, eleven days before the publication date he had selected.

We have honored all requests save one: Enomoto-sensei asked that the final page be printed in a deliberately faded ink, so that readers could not be certain whether they were reading the text or imagining it. Our printers were unable to replicate the effect reliably. We apologize for this failure. We believe he anticipated it.

—Saitō Press, Kyoto

## Compiler's Preface

I will be brief because the letters are not.

Kuroda Hideo and I met seventeen times in competition between 1971 and 1983. I won six of those matches. He won ten. One—the last—he forfeited. That forfeiture made me champion. This is a matter of public record.

What is not a matter of public record is that six weeks after the Tenka-Ichi final, I received a letter postmarked from Eiheiji monastery, in Fukui Prefecture, composed in a hand I did not recognize but whose cadence I knew instantly: the same rhythm as his swordsmanship, the stroke arriving not when expected but just before, so that you felt it had been waiting for you rather than approaching.

I replied. He asked me not to. I did not reply again. He continued writing. Over forty years I received thirty-seven letters. I kept each one in a cedarwood box my wife gave me for our twentieth anniversary. When she died, I moved them into her box. When I received my diagnosis, I opened them again.

I have added notes where I felt they were necessary. I ask the reader to understand that the notes are not explanations. They are my half of the match. I was not permitted to send them. Now I have nowhere else to place them.

I am not publishing this to resolve anything. I am publishing this because silence is not the same as peace, and because the story of two men requires, at minimum, two voices, even if one of them speaks only in the margins.

—Enomoto Ryōichi, September 2023

## LETTER THE FIRST

*Received October 29, 1983. Written on monastery stationery. The ink is standard sumi, the brushwork controlled but hurried, as though composed in a period of enforced activity—perhaps the twenty minutes between evening zazen and lights-out.*

---

Enomoto,

You will think this letter is an apology. It is not. An apology requires a wrong, and I have not determined yet whether what I did was wrong. Perhaps by writing to you I will discover the shape of the thing.

At Tenka-Ichi, in the final, we assumed our positions. You entered *jōdan no kamae*. I entered *chūdan*. The referee called *hajime*. And in the first half-second—less—I saw something in your stance that I had never seen in twelve years of facing you. A thing so small I might have imagined it. But I did not imagine it.

I will not tell you what it was. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. I need to understand first why knowing a thing obliges a man to act on it, or whether it obliges him at all. I have forfeited and come to the mountain. The monks here wake at half past three. Perhaps if I wake enough times before the sun, the question will exhaust itself.

Do not write back. I have nothing yet that deserves an answer.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *He says "the first half-second." I have watched the NHK footage of that match many hundreds of times. In the first half-second, Hideo's eyes change. I always attributed this to focus. I now believe it was recognition. In the second before the referee's call, he was my opponent. By the first half-second, he was my keeper. I did not know this for forty years. I wonder now how the match would have looked to me if I had known—if I would have seen what he saw, or whether the flaw, like a mole on one's own back, is visible only from a position one cannot occupy alone.*

## LETTER THE THIRD

*Received March 3, 1984. Brushwork more settled. Paper of higher quality—Hideo had apparently requested materials from outside the monastery. A small breach of discipline for a novice. I note it because discipline is the hinge on which every subsequent letter turns.*

---

Enomoto,

Spring cleaning at the monastery today. The abbot says we are to sweep not only the dust but the reason we notice it. I find this unhelpful.

I have been reading the Hagakure again. Yamamoto Tsunetomo says: "The Way of the Warrior is found in death." This is the line everyone remembers. But he also says: "There is something to be learned from a rainstorm. When meeting with a sudden shower, you try not to get wet and run quickly along the road. By doing such things as passing under the eaves of houses, you still get wet. When you are resolved from the beginning, you will not be perplexed, though you still get the same soaking."

I forfeited the match. This was my rainstorm. But I cannot determine whether I ran beneath the eaves or stood in the open. The resolved man would have—what? Struck? Not struck? The resolved man would not have *discovered*, because discovery implies surprise, and surprise implies one has built a house of expectations that can be damaged. The resolved man lives in the open already.

But this is theoretical. I am not theoretical. I am a man who stood across from his oldest rival, saw the place where his body had betrayed him, and could not strike because the strike would not have been a victory. It would have been something else. I do not know the word for it yet in any of the languages I have.

The snow is melting here.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *When Hideo writes "the place where his body had betrayed him," I cannot help reading this with my own body in the room. I am seventy-nine. My body is in a state of comprehensive betrayal. Was it always? Was there always a doorway standing open in my stance through which a sufficiently perceptive man could send ruin? I have asked three of my most senior students to examine footage of my jōdan guard from the 1983 final. Two found nothing. The third, Miyashita, said she observed a slight forward displacement of my left heel, perhaps two centimeters, that would have fractionally delayed my retreat from a committed downward diagonal. I asked her what the consequence would have been if an opponent had targeted that delay with a full-speed strike. She was quiet for a long time. Then she said: "At full competition speed, with a shinai, a severe concussion and possibly a cervical fracture. With a real blade—" and stopped herself. I told her to finish. She said: "He would have killed you, Sensei." I said: "Thank you." She said: "Did someone see this?" I changed the subject.*

*Two centimeters. A left heel. Forty years.*

## LETTER THE FIFTH

*Received January 11, 1985. The calligraphy in this letter is notably more refined—Hideo was receiving instruction.*

---

Enomoto,

I have been given calligraphy practice by the abbot, who believes my mind moves in shapes too irregular for zazen alone and requires another discipline to "wear smooth the stone." He is right, though not for the reasons he believes. Zazen asks you to sit with everything. Calligraphy asks you to express one thing at a time, and to mean it, and to accept that the stroke cannot be retracted. I find this closer to combat than sitting is.

There is a character—*katsujinken*—the life-giving sword. You know it. We both learned it from Tanaka-sensei in our third year, when he told us that the highest purpose of the sword is not to kill but to preserve life. At the time I thought this was beautiful but abstract, a thing masters said to students to elevate butchery into philosophy.

Now I think Tanaka was being precise.

What would you call a strike that cannot be delivered because it would end something that must not be ended? Not mercy, because mercy implies power held over another. Not cowardice, because the strike was certain. Not love—

No. I crossed that out and wrote it again. Not love. Something else.

I wake at half past three and the mountain is always the same mountain.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *He crossed it out and wrote it again. The reader should understand: in Japanese calligraphy, a crossed-out word is a visible wound on the page. It is not done. The discipline forbids it. To cross out and rewrite the same word—"not love"—is either a failure of technique or a piece of swordsmanship executed on paper. I have spent forty years deciding which.*

## LETTER THE EIGHTH

*Received November 2, 1987. A significant gap. The longest silence between letters to this point—eleven months. When I received this letter, my daughter had just been born. I mention this because Hideo could not have known, and yet the letter concerns children.*

---

Enomoto,

I apologize for the silence. I was ill for most of the winter and spent the spring relearning how to sit, which is harder than it sounds when the body has forgotten how to be trusted. A digression: illness is the closest thing to combat I have experienced since leaving competition. Both involve a period in which you do not control the encounter. Both reveal what you have practiced and what you have merely memorized.

A village boy asked me today if I was a samurai. I told him no. He asked what I was. I said I was a monk. He said monks were boring. I agreed.

He asked if I had ever fought anyone. I said yes. He asked if I had won. I said sometimes. He asked why I stopped. I told him I found something I couldn't use and couldn't put down, and so I went somewhere I wouldn't need to do either. He looked at me as children do when an adult has said something true but irrelevant.

I think about what would have happened if I had struck. Not the physical consequence—I am not speaking of your body, Enomoto, though God knows I think about your body in that stance often enough that the abbot would question my meditation if he could read its contents—but the ontological one. If I had delivered that strike, what would I have become? Not a champion. A champion is a man who defeats a rival. I would have become a man who destroyed one. And the instrument of destruction would not have been skill or courage but knowledge that arrived by accident, a flaw I stumbled on like a stone in the dark.

Is it honorable to win by accident of perception?

The boy came back the next day with a stick and asked me to teach him to fight. I taught him how to sweep.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *My daughter is now thirty-six. She has never held a shinai. This is not relevant, but I have no one left to tell it to except the margins of this book.*

## LETTER THE ELEVENTH

*Received June 19, 1990. By this point I had begun to detect the pattern. Each letter approaches a center and withdraws. A spiral. Not a tightening one—the radius seems to widen and narrow by turns, as though the confession is a living thing that breathes.*

---

Enomoto,

Let me try it this way.

Two men stand facing each other. One holds a sword. The other holds a sword. In the space between them exists every possible outcome: death, defeat, victory, stalemate, the referee's whistle, the spectators' silence, the long walk afterward. Every possible outcome but one. There is no outcome in which one man may give the other a gift the other does not know he needs without transforming the gift into a wound.

This is my difficulty.

If I had struck you—

If I had told you—

You see. Even on paper. Even after seven years. Even with no audience but the mountain and a man I will not see again. I cannot complete either sentence, because each completion annihilates a thing I need to preserve. To strike would have been to take your life (or the use of your body, which for men like us is the same). To tell would have been to take your mastery (or the belief in it, which for men like us is the same). And to do neither—

I have chosen the only path that preserves everything except myself.

Do not mistake this for nobility. There is nothing noble about refusing to act. The noble man acts and accepts the consequence. I have hidden from consequence on a mountain, and the mountain has not asked me to leave, which I take as evidence either of its compassion or its indifference.

The rice was poor this year. I think about the 1979 semifinal often. You were very good that day.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *He says "to tell would have been to take your mastery." I have considered this sentence more carefully than any I have encountered in a life spent reading. He is saying: if he told me the flaw, I would have corrected it. And in correcting it, I would have known that my entire career—every defense, every successful retreat from jōdan, every match I won from that guard—had been conducted through an open door that no one happened to walk through. He withheld the knowledge not to preserve his advantage but to preserve my past. I did not ask him to. I will never know if I would have wanted him to.*

*He was also very good in 1979. The match lasted nine seconds. Neither of us scored. We were both reprimanded for excessive caution. I have never been more present in my life than I was during those nine seconds.*

## LETTER THE FOURTEENTH

*Received August 8, 1993. First mention of what I will call the central paradox.*

---

Enomoto,

The abbot died this morning. He was ninety-one and he died as he had lived: slowly, meticulously, and without asking anyone's opinion. In his final days he told me a story I had heard before—the koan of Nansen's cat—but he told it differently than I had learned it. In the version I knew, Nansen kills the cat to make a philosophical point. In the abbot's version, Nansen holds the knife above the cat for a very long time and says nothing, and the cat, which is a cat and not a philosopher, goes to sleep. The abbot said this was the real lesson: the cat has no idea it is in a koan.

I tell you this because I wonder whether you are in a koan. Whether you have any idea that there is a knife, and a silence, and that both concern you. Perhaps you have gone on with your life entirely unaware. Perhaps you coach students, drink beer, watch baseball, grow old without the faintest sense that a man on a mountain has spent ten years trying to find the right sentence to explain why he left you standing in an arena in 1983.

Or perhaps you know everything and have chosen your own silence, in which case we are not two men but one animal, divided down the center, each half unable to speak because it requires the other to form the mouth.

The monastery will select a new abbot. I have been asked and have declined. I decline everything. It is what I do best.

Hideo

---

**Compiler's Note:** *He asks whether I know. I have spent the remaining thirty years of this correspondence waiting for him to ask directly, and he never does. Here, then, is what I knew and when I knew it.*
