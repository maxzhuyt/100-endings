# The Commissar's Confession

---

The cart wheels need grease.

That is what I am thinking—that the cart wheels need grease—as we pass the Rue Saint-Honoré and the crowd opens for us like a wound opens for a blade, without wanting to, simply because the metal is there and the metal is moving and what can flesh do but part. The left rear wheel screams against its axle and I think: someone should see to that. Someone should fill a requisition form. Someone should speak to the municipal cartwright, if the municipal cartwright has not already been arrested, and I believe he was arrested in Ventôse for the crime of having once shod a horse belonging to a ci-devant marquis, which is not actually a crime but which becomes one when your neighbor wants your shop, and so the cartwright is probably dead and the wheel will go on screaming.

I am thinking about the wheel because I cannot think about the five people sitting in the cart behind me.

No. That is a lie. I am thinking about the wheel *and* the five people because this is what my mind has become—a mechanism that runs on parallel tracks, every thought twinned with its shadow, every observation a door that opens onto the room I am trying not to enter. Three years of living as two men will do this. Three years of writing reports for the Committee in a hand that my schoolmaster would have admired, careful and rectilinear and devoid of flourish, while simultaneously composing messages in lemon juice between the lines of love letters that I passed to a dead man who was not yet dead when I began passing them, and who believed, as I believed, as we all believed in those first burning months—

The wheel screams.

Citizeness Morin is looking at me. I can feel her looking at me without turning around because I have spent nine months memorizing the quality of her attention, the particular weight of her gaze, which is not heavy like a stone but heavy like a hand placed on your chest when you are lying down, firm and warm and full of a knowledge that has nothing to do with information. She is forty-three years old. Her husband printed pamphlets until a ball of Prussian lead passed through his throat at Valmy, and after that she printed them herself, setting the type with fingers that smelled of ink and turpentine, and she never once printed a word she did not believe, which is how I know she is braver than I have ever been.

She taught me—

But I cannot think about what she taught me. Not yet. Not with the wheel screaming, not with the Place de la Révolution still two streets away, not with the crowd pressing in and the faces blurring into that single composite face that haunts me, the face of the Revolution, which is all faces and no face, which is ecstasy and hunger and rage and boredom, such terrible boredom, because even the guillotine becomes routine, even blood becomes tedious, and the crowd comes now not for the spectacle but for the habit, the way one goes to mass not because one believes but because the bell is ringing and one's legs know the way.

My name is Étienne Lessard. I am thirty-one years old. I hold the rank of Inspector of Police attached to the Bureau of General Security, seconded by special order to the Committee of Public Safety, which means I answer to Robespierre, which means I answer to a man who sleeps in a cabinet-maker's house and powders his wig while France devours itself, a man whose spectacles have never been spotted with blood because he has people like me to stand between his principles and their consequences.

I was recruited in the autumn of 1791 by a man named Dufresne.

Not recruited by the Committee. Recruited by Dufresne. There is a distinction, though it no longer matters, because Dufresne is in a mass grave outside the Madeleine cemetery with lime eating through his cheeks and his fine English coat rotting off his shoulders, and the distinction between being recruited by a man and being recruited by an institution is the distinction between a cause and a machine, and Dufresne was a cause, Dufresne was the cause made flesh, Dufresne sitting across from me in a café on the Île de la Cité with his coffee going cold, saying—

*The revolution is a river, Étienne. Our task is not to stop it. Our task is to keep it in its banks.*

He had a way of making metaphors feel like mathematics. Precise. Inarguable. The revolution was a river and the banks were the law and if the river overflowed its banks it would not water the fields but drown them, and so we needed men inside the apparatus, men who could feed information to the moderates, men who could warn the Girondins when the Montagnards were sharpening their knives, men who could—

Men who could what, Dufresne? What exactly did we accomplish?

The Girondins are dead. I warned them, and they are dead. The moderates I fed information to used that information to moderate nothing. They used it to prepare their defenses, and their defenses failed, and then they used it to prepare their escapes, and some of them escaped and some of them didn't, and the ones who escaped went to Bordeaux and Caen and raised armies against Paris and became the very thing we said we were preventing—tyrants, counter-revolutionaries, men who would drown the republic in royalist blood instead of republican blood, as though the blood were different, as though blood has a political color—

The wheel screams. We turn onto the Rue de la Révolution. The crowd thickens.

I received my orders this morning at seven o'clock. Saint-Just delivered them personally, which is how I knew they were serious, because Saint-Just does not visit a mere inspector unless the inspector is about to become either very important or very dead, and the two conditions are not mutually exclusive. He stood in my doorway with his cravat immaculate and his eyes like stones at the bottom of a frozen lake and he said:

*Citizen Inspector, you are to take personal charge of the condemned printers. Five in number. Transport, processing, execution. The Committee requires a trusted hand.*

A trusted hand. My hand. The hand that wrote reports in careful rectilinear script *and* love letters in lemon juice. The hand that signed arrest warrants by day and slipped warnings under doors by night. The trusted hand.

Did he know? Does Robespierre know? Is this a test, a trap, a coincidence so cruel that only a universe without God could produce it?

It does not matter. This is what I have learned in three years of double life: the question of whether they know is less important than the question of what you do while not knowing whether they know. You behave as the person they believe you to be. You become the mask. And my mask is a man named Étienne Lessard who is efficient, who is dependable, who processes the condemned with the flat affect of a butcher counting carcasses, who has never once flinched at the blade's descent because flinching would be noted, flinching would be written down, flinching would become a line in someone's report, and reports are the circulatory system of the Terror—the blood flows through them, by them, because of them.

Citizeness Morin is still looking at me.

I know this because I have turned around. Just for a moment. Just a glance over my shoulder at the cart, a perfunctory inspection of the prisoners, the kind of glance that a diligent inspector would perform, and my eyes meet hers and—

She knows.

Not that I am her ally. Not that I am the shadow who slipped notes under her door warning her to move the press, to burn the subscriber lists, to hide the Brissot correspondence in the false bottom of the flour bin. She does not know that. She never knew that. Dufresne was my only contact and Dufresne is dead and the chain between me and the network was always mediated by dead drops and pseudonyms and the elaborate architecture of deniability that keeps an infiltrator alive, which means that Citizeness Morin, who is riding in a cart toward a blade that will separate her head from her body in approximately eleven minutes, believes that I am exactly what I appear to be: one of the Terror's functionaries, a man with ink on his fingers and death in his ledger, a clerk of annihilation.

And yet she knows something. I can see it in her eyes—not knowledge but recognition, the recognition of one human being looking at another and seeing, beneath the mask, beneath the tricolor cockade and the official sash and the set jaw and the flat affect, something that is struggling.

Or perhaps I am flattering myself. Perhaps she is simply looking at me the way the condemned look at their executioners—with the unbearable intimacy of the final transaction, the way a lamb might look at the knife if the lamb had eyes that understood.

I turn back to face the street.

Dufresne. Think about Dufresne. Think about the beginning. The beginning is clean. The beginning makes sense.

*We are not conspirators, Étienne. We are surgeons. The body politic is sick and we must cut carefully, not with the butcher's cleaver but with the lancet.*

September 1791. The constitution ratified. The king a constitutional monarch. The revolution, we believed, was over—completed, achieved, its work done. Liberty, equality, fraternity. The words meant something then. They were not yet carved into the lintels of buildings where people were tortured. They were not yet shouted by men whose boots were wet with the blood of prisoners massacred in their cells. They meant—they meant what Citizeness Morin taught me they meant, later, in the back room of her print shop with the press standing silent and the ink drying on the tray, when she—

No.

I cannot. Not yet. The Place is ahead. I can see the platform. I can see the blade.

Think about the mechanics. Think about the procedure. This is what the double life teaches you: when the emotional mind fails, the procedural mind continues. There are steps. There are protocols. The condemned are unloaded from the cart. Their hands are bound—already bound, bound since the Conciergerie, bound with rope that I requisitioned from the naval stores because the Committee's budget for rope is—

Listen to yourself. You are thinking about rope budgets while walking five people to their deaths.

This is what you have become.

This is what the revolution makes of its servants: men who can think about rope budgets and human lives in the same breath, men whose minds have been partitioned like the departments of France, each compartment sealed from the others, so that the part of you that loves can coexist with the part of you that kills, and neither part need acknowledge the other, and the whole machine runs smoothly, efficiently, on parallel tracks, the wheel screaming, always the wheel screaming—

I had a calculation to make this morning. Saint-Just left and I sat at my desk and I made a calculation. This is the calculation:

There are five condemned. Citizeness Morin. The typesetter Giraud. The boy apprentice—God, the boy, the boy is seventeen—named Luc Paret. The bookseller Fontaine. The ink merchant Delacour.

Of these five, three are members of the network. Morin, Giraud, and Fontaine. They received and distributed information. They printed and circulated the moderate pamphlets. They are guilty of the crimes for which they are condemned, if those crimes are crimes, which they are under the Law of Suspects, which makes everything a crime, which makes breathing a crime if you breathe in the direction of a ci-devant, which makes—

But the boy and Delacour are not part of the network. The boy swept the floors. Delacour sold ink. They are guilty only of proximity, and proximity is indeed a crime under the Law of Suspects, but it was not always a crime, and there was a time when standing near the wrong person did not sign your death warrant, and that time was—

Three years ago. Only three years ago. My God.

The calculation:

There are three other members of the network still hidden. I will not write their names even in the margin of my mind because names are dangerous, names are contagious, names spread from thought to speech to paper to arrest warrant with the speed of plague. Call them A, B, and C. They are alive. They are hidden. They do not know that I know them—they were Dufresne's contacts, not mine—but I have deduced their identities from the pattern of the dead drops, from the geography of the warnings, from the structure of the network as I reconstructed it in the weeks after Dufresne's arrest, sitting alone in my room with a candle and a map and the cold certainty that everything was coming apart.

If I save one of the five—if I engineer an escape, a diversion, a bureaucratic error that mislays one prisoner—the investigation will intensify. They will interrogate the remaining four. The remaining four will be tortured, and under torture, connections will surface, threads will be pulled, and A, B, and C will be exposed.

If I save none—if I walk all five to the blade with the flat affect of the dutiful inspector—A, B, and C remain hidden. The network, what remains of it, survives. For what purpose, I no longer know. But it survives.

This is the mathematics of the Terror. This is the arithmetic that Dufresne never taught me because Dufresne believed, with the optimism of the early revolution, that we would never be called upon to choose between people, only between principles. *We are surgeons, not butchers.* But the surgery has become butchery and the lancet has become a cleaver and the calculus is no longer which principle to serve but which human being to sacrifice, and the answer, the terrible answer, the answer that sat in my chest all morning like a stone—

The answer is all of them.

I sacrifice all five. I walk them to the blade. I let the machine do its work. And A, B, and C go on living in their hidden rooms, doing their hidden work, serving a cause that I am no longer certain exists.

This is what Dufresne would have wanted. I think. I think this is what Dufresne would have wanted because Dufresne was a man of systems/doctrine, a man who understood that the network was more important than any individual node, that the revolution—the *true* revolution, the moderate republic of laws and rights and rational governance—required sacrifice, required the subordination of the personal to the political, required—

Required me to watch Citizeness Morin die.

We have arrived at the Place.

The crowd roars. They always roar. I used to think the roar was bloodlust but now I think it is something worse—it is participation. The crowd roars because roaring makes them part of the spectacle, and being part of the spectacle makes them safe, because the spectacle only consumes those who stand apart from it, those who are silent, those who do not roar, and so the roar is not enthusiasm but survival, each throat opening not in celebration but in the desperate performance of revolutionary virtue, and I understand this because I have been performing revolutionary virtue for three years, my entire life is a roar, a sustained scream of pretended conviction—

The guards are pulling the condemned from the cart. Giraud stumbles. The boy—Luc—the boy does not stumble. The boy walks with a steadiness that makes me want to vomit because I remember being seventeen and I remember believing that I was immortal and I wonder if the boy still believes that, if some merciful delusion has descended upon him, or if he knows, as I know, as we all know, that the blade is real and the death is real and there is no reprieve coming from the Convention, from the Committee, from God, from anyone—

Citizeness Morin is helped down from the cart by a guard who holds her elbow with surprising gentleness, and for a moment the guard is not a guard but a human being helping an older woman descend from a vehicle, and the normalcy of the gesture is so obscene that I have to look away.

She taught me what justice meant.

I will say it now because the blade is there and the time is short and the procedural mind cannot hold the emotional mind at bay forever, the compartments are breaking down, the partitions are crumbling, and so I will say it:

She taught me in the back room of her print shop on a Tuesday evening in Brumaire—no, it was still October then, the old calendar, the real calendar, the calendar that counted years from the birth of Christ rather than from the birth of the Republic, as though the Republic were a new Christ, a new salvation—October, a Tuesday, the press silent, the ink drying, and I had come to deliver a warning, a dead drop had been compromised, she needed to move the Brissot correspondence, and I did not know her then, Dufresne had given me only an address and a password, and she let me in and I delivered the warning and I should have left but—
