# The Last Watch of the Mara Celeste

---

I should tell you about the tea first.

It was oolong. She kept a tin of it in the chart room, behind the sextant case her grandfather had bolted to the shelf in 1974. The tin had a painting of a crane on it, the bird kind, white with a red crown, and the lid never sat right so you had to press it down with the heel of your hand and twist. She was particular about the steeping. Three minutes, no more. She said longer than that and you were just drinking bitterness for the sake of it, and there was enough of that going around.

I brought her a cup at 0400 on the first morning of the storm and she didn't drink it. It sat on the compass housing and went cold and I watched the surface of it tremble in the cup, and then slosh, and then by 0600 I picked it up and the cup was half-empty from the pitching and what remained was the temperature of the rain coming through the wheelhouse seal.

That was Tuesday. I think it was Tuesday.

---

The *Mara Celeste* was a forty-eight-foot ketch, wooden-hulled, teak on oak frames. Built in Mystic in 1971 by a man named Dao Wen, who was not from Mystic, who had come to Connecticut from Kaohsiung with a set of tools and a stubbornness his granddaughter would inherit like a disease. She was wide in the beam and heavy in the water and when Wen — the captain, I mean, not the grandfather, though their names were the same, Lin Wen, both of them, the grandfather insisted — when the captain talked about the boat, she used words like *honest* and *forgiving* and I didn't understand until I'd sailed on her a while that those were the highest things you could say about a hull.

The boat was not fast. The boat was not modern. The engine was a Perkins 4-108 that Wen rebuilt every winter with her own hands, her fingers black to the wrist with diesel and gasket sealant, and she would not talk to anyone during those weeks. She would play the radio in the engine compartment and I would hear her in there singing in Mandarin, songs I didn't recognize, and the sound of ratchets.

---

Let me tell you what I know about the letters.

The first one came in March. She read it standing at the dock in St. George's and I saw her face do something I'd never seen it do before, which was nothing. Her face went vacant, like a house where someone had switched off all the lights. She folded the letter back into its envelope and its envelope back into her jacket and she said, *Clara, do we have limes,* and I said we did, and she said, *Good, I want fish tonight,* and that was all.

There were more after that. The bank. The insurance company. An attorney in Hamilton whose stationery smelled like cedar, which I know because I found one of the letters in the trash and I am not above reading another person's mail when that person has stopped sleeping and started staring at bulkheads like they owe her money.

Which, in a way, they did.

The grandfather built the boat. The father sailed the boat. The father died and the boat came to Wen with a lien she hadn't known about, a second mortgage her father had taken against the hull to pay for treatments at a hospital in Boston that did not, in the end, treat anything at all. She told me this once. Just once. We were anchored in a cove off Eleuthera and it was one of those evenings when the water goes flat and pink and the sky forgets to be a sky and becomes something closer to a held breath, and she said, *He mortgaged the boat to die slowly instead of quickly, and now they want me to pay for the privilege of his pain.*

Then she asked if there was more rice and I said there was.

---

I was the cook. I should be clear about that. I was not the first mate. I was not the navigator. I was not anyone whose opinion on matters of seamanship carried weight. I was a woman of fifty-three years who could make a pork shoulder last four days and knew how to keep dried beans from going mealy, and Wen had hired me off a dock in Christiansted because her last cook had quit and I was sitting on a crate reading a novel and she said, *Can you make soup,* and I said, *What kind,* and she said, *Any kind,* and I said, *Yes,* and that was my interview.

That was two years before the storm. Two years of soup and rice and fish and the mornings we played cards.

---

Gin rummy. Every morning, 0700, if the weather allowed and sometimes when it didn't. She kept a running score in a small notebook, the kind with the marbled cover, and she tallied our points in pencil in columns so neat they looked printed. I was ahead by eleven thousand points over two years. She said this was because I cheated. I did not cheat. She was simply a terrible card player, which I found endearing, because she was not terrible at anything else.

She played cards the way she read charts — with an absolute faith in the system's internal logic, not understanding that gin rummy is a game of feeling, of guessing what the other person wants and not giving it to them. She could read a tide table like breathing but she could not read a face. I mean that as a kindness.

On the second morning of the storm, Wednesday — I'm almost certain it was Wednesday — the table was at an angle that sent the cards sliding into my lap every time the boat rolled to port, and Wen gathered them and held them and played from her hand without laying anything down, and I did the same, and we sat there in the saloon with our hands full of cards like we were waiting for a bus, not saying anything, the hull making sounds I had never heard a hull make. Deep sounds. Structural sounds. The boat was talking to itself in a language I didn't want to learn.

Wen laid down three queens and said, *Gin.*

She hadn't had gin in weeks. I looked at her hand. It was correct. I wrote the score in the notebook because she let me keep the notebook during storms, since my handwriting was better when the world was tilting.

---

The *Mara Celeste* was running deliveries between the islands, had been for a decade. Provisions, mail, small cargo — cylinder heads, water pumps, boxes of textbooks for the school on Salt Cay. It was not glamorous. It did not pay well. But it paid, or it had, until the engine needed an overhaul she couldn't afford, and then the insurance went up, and then the letters, and then a man in a blue blazer came to the dock in Hamilton and put his hand on the gunwale of the boat while he talked to Wen about restructuring her payments and I watched her look at his hand on the wood the way you'd look at a wasp on your child's arm.

She told him she would make the payment. She told me later she could not make the payment. She said this while peeling a potato, and she peeled it all, the whole thing, into one continuous ribbon of skin without looking down, and she said, *They'll take her in Bermuda. There's a man from the company waiting in Bermuda.*

I said, *Then don't go to Bermuda.*

She said, *Where else is there.*

---

The typhoon was called Miriam, which is a name for a person, not for a thing that wants to kill you, but that is how they do it. They came on the weather radio two days before and Wen listened with her head tilted the way she tilted it when the engine made a new sound, cataloguing, and she plotted the storm's track on the chart with small x's in pencil, and she saw what I saw, which was that the storm was going to pass between us and Bermuda like a door swinging shut.

Any other captain would have run north. Any other captain would have made for harbor and waited it out and arrived in Bermuda five days late with a story about the weather and the insurance man would still be there, standing on the dock with his briefcase, patient as a headstone.

Wen looked at the chart for a long time. She looked at the x's. She looked at the penciled line of our course, which went directly through the storm's meridian.

She said, *We'll take the direct route.*

I said, *Through it?*

She said, *Through it,* and she didn't explain and I didn't ask, because there are moments when you understand what a person is saying underneath what they're saying, and what Wen was saying underneath was: *If the sea wants her, the sea can have her, but that man in Bermuda cannot.*

---

I should tell you about her hands.

They were small. You wouldn't think it to look at her — she was five-eight, broad-shouldered, carried herself like a woman who'd been lifting things her whole life — but her hands were small and precise and scarred across the knuckles in a pattern I eventually realized was from reaching into the engine compartment at bad angles. Two fingers on her left hand didn't close all the way. She set her own jib in a Force 7 once and when she came back to the cockpit her palms were bleeding from the lines and she wiped them on her shorts and said, *The halyard's fraying. Remind me.*

I reminded her. She didn't replace it. There wasn't money.

Those hands on the wheel. That's what I keep seeing. Those hands.

---

Tuesday the wind was thirty knots and the sea was ugly but manageable. The *Mara Celeste* shouldered through it the way she shouldered through everything — low and steady and without complaint. I made rice and beans and fried plantains and Wen ate everything, which was unusual. She usually picked at her food, not because it was bad but because she forgot she was eating, her mind somewhere in the rigging or the chart or the long arithmetic of debt.

But Tuesday she ate everything and asked for more, and I gave her more, and she sat in the wheelhouse with the plate in her lap and the rain streaming down the glass and she said, *My grandfather ate like this before a big passage. He said you should eat like you won't see land again.*

I said, *Will we see land again?*

She looked at me with those dark steady eyes that never lied and never quite told the truth either. She said, *I hope so, Clara. Eat your rice.*

---

Wednesday the wind was fifty knots and the pumps started.

The *Mara Celeste* had always taken water. That was not new. She was an old wooden boat and old wooden boats breathe and sweat and weep through their seams — Wen's word, *weep*, and she said it with tenderness, the way you'd describe a child with a fever. The bilge pumps ran twice a day in good weather, four times in bad, and the electric pump was backed by a manual one that I'd learned to operate because Wen believed everyone on board should know how to do everything, in case.

In case.

Wednesday at noon the electric pump burned out. I heard it — a whine that became a groan that became silence, which is worse than any noise. Wen went below and came back up and said, *We'll use the manual pump in shifts.* Her face was the face from the first letter. All the lights off.

By Wednesday evening the manual pump's diaphragm split and the water was over the floorboards in the saloon and my shoes were wet and the cards were wet and Wen and I played gin rummy with damp cards on a table that was tilting thirty degrees and the water moved back and forth beneath us like something alive, like the sea had sent an ambassador into the hull to negotiate terms.

---

I said, *We should put out a call.*

She said, *No.*

I said, *Wen.*

She said, *If I call, they'll send someone, and they'll take us to Bermuda, and the man will be there.*

I said, *You'll lose the boat either way.*

She put down a seven of clubs. She said, *There's ways of losing.*

And I understood then. There are ways of losing. There is losing where they take the thing from your hands and you watch it go and you live with the empty hands. And there is losing where you hold the thing so tightly it goes down with you and nobody gets it, not the bank, not the insurance man, not the sea, because the sea doesn't get it either, the sea just gets *you*, and the boat goes on being yours at the bottom, yours in the dark, yours forever with the fishes swimming through the portholes and the coral growing on the wheel your grandfather's hands built.

I picked up the seven of clubs. I didn't need it. But she'd put it down like she was giving something away, and I wanted to keep it.

---

Wednesday night I made tea three times. She drank none of it. The wind was a sound beyond sound — it wasn't screaming, it was something lower and bigger than screaming, a pressure, as if the air itself had clenched. The waves were not waves anymore. They were architecture. They were cathedrals of black water assembling themselves around us and collapsing, and each time the *Mara Celeste* climbed one I could feel the old hull flexing like a ribcage taking a breath and I thought: *she's tired*. The boat was tired. Forty-seven years of water and wind and the hands of three generations of Wens and she was tired.

I stood behind the captain in the wheelhouse and held the grab rail and watched her steer. You could not steer through waves like that. You could only suggest. You could only have a conversation with the water about where you might go, and sometimes the water agreed and sometimes it didn't, and Wen stood there having this conversation with her small scarred hands on the wheel and her feet apart and her jaw set, and the compass light made her face green from below and she looked like something from a painting, something from a church — a saint, if saints wore oil-stained jackets and hadn't slept in two days.

---

Thursday.

On Thursday the mast cracked.

Not broke. Cracked. A sound like a rifle shot and then a long splintering groan, and the mainmast — which her grandfather had stepped himself, Douglas fir, sixty feet, chosen from a yard in Oregon and shipped across the country because Dao Wen did not trust eastern lumber — the mainmast opened a split from the partners to six feet above the deck and the whole rig shifted to leeward and the boat lurched and I fell and hit my shoulder on the chart table and later I found a bruise the color of an eggplant, the size of my hand, and I thought: *this is my souvenir. This is what I'll bring home from this passage.*

Wen went on deck.

---

I should not have followed her but I followed her.

The rain was horizontal. That description is not a metaphor. The rain was moving sideways through the air as if gravity had been renegotiated when no one was looking. I couldn't see the bow. I couldn't see the water. I could see Wen, three feet ahead of me, because she'd put on her yellow foul-weather jacket and it was the brightest thing in the world, the only color, everything else cancelled to grey and black and the white of breaking seas.

She had the rope. She'd gotten it from the lazarette — I don't know when, I didn't see her get it — and it was the heavy dock line, the one we used for tying up in surge, three-quarter-inch nylon, and she wrapped it around herself and around the mast, under the split, where the wood was still whole, and she tied herself there.

I stood in the cockpit with the water around my knees — there was water everywhere now, the water had won its negotiation with the hull — and I shouted her name.
