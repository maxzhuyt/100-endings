# The Informant Test

## I.

The helicopter set down on the pad at 14:07, which was already dusk. In three days it would be full dark and would remain so for sixty-one days. The pilot kept the rotors turning while the six of them climbed out, hunched against the downdraft, carrying nothing that couldn't be shouldered. The pilot had not spoken to them during the flight from Tromsø. He did not speak now. When the last brother's boots hit the concrete, the helicopter lifted, banked east, and shrank to a sound, then to nothing.

The station was called Sverdrup-11 in the briefing materials, though no sign marked it. It was a former Norwegian meteorological outpost, decommissioned in 2014 and purchased through a Cypriot shell company whose beneficial ownership led, through seven layers of incorporation, to their father. It sat on a granite headland on the northern coast of Spitsbergen, eighty kilometers from the nearest settlement. The sea to the north was already freezing. The land to the south was permafrost and scree and then mountains, and beyond the mountains more permafrost and scree.

The station consisted of a main building — a flat-roofed concrete rectangle, sixty meters by twenty, with double-insulated walls and triple-glazed windows that looked north over the ice — and two outbuildings. The first outbuilding housed a diesel generator and fuel supply. The second was an equipment shed containing snowmobile parts, ice axes, rope, and nothing else of interest. The main building contained eight sleeping quarters (small, each with a cot and a lockable door), a kitchen with a commercial gas range and provisions for ninety days, a common room with bolted-down furniture, a communications room (locked, the key not among those left on the hook by the entrance), a storage room, two lavatories, and a medical cabinet stocked with field-trauma supplies, antibiotics, and morphine.

There were no firearms in the station. There were knives in the kitchen. There were tools in the equipment shed.

The six brothers stood in the common room and took inventory of each other. Marten, the eldest, was forty-three. He had their father's jaw and their father's economy of movement. Emil, forty-one, stood at the window with his arms crossed and said nothing, as was his custom. Falk, thirty-nine, clapped Nikolaj on the shoulder and said it was good to see him, that it had been too long, that they should not let the old man's errands keep them from being brothers. Nikolaj, thirty-seven, let himself be clapped and said yes. Søren, thirty-five, sat on the edge of a table and looked at the ceiling. Viktor, the youngest at thirty-two, tried the door to the communications room, found it locked, and reported this to no one in particular.

On the table in the common room was an envelope. Heavy stock, cream-colored, unsealed. Inside was a single typed page. No letterhead, no signature, no date. Marten read it aloud because Marten always read things aloud when their father was involved — it was his role, his inheritance of proximity to the source.

The letter said:

*One of you has been providing information to Europol's Organized Crime Unit for a period of no less than fourteen months. The identity of this individual has been confirmed through independent channels but will not be disclosed. You are to determine, among yourselves, which of you is the informant. You are to deal with this matter conclusively before extraction, which will occur on the first of February. Those who demonstrate their loyalty and judgment in this matter will be received into the full confidence of the family's operations. Those who fail this test, through inability or unwillingness, will not.*

That was all. Beneath the letter was a satellite phone, powered off. A sticky note on its screen read: *For extraction confirmation only. One call. February 1.*

Falk picked up the satellite phone and turned it over in his hands. "One call," he said.

"He means it," Marten said.

"Of course he means it," Emil said from the window, without turning around.

Viktor tried the communications room door again. It did not open. Søren, still sitting on the table's edge, said: "Or we wait. We sit here for sixty days, we eat his food, and on February first we call and say we've decided this is insane, and we'd like to go home."

Five brothers looked at Søren. The quality of their looking was the first data point any of them recorded, though none of them said so.

"That's an option," Nikolaj said carefully.

"It's not," Marten said. He folded the letter and placed it back in the envelope. "You read what it says. Those who fail this test. He's not offering a choice between playing and not playing. Not playing is a result. It has consequences. You all know what his consequences look like."

The generator hummed beneath them. Outside, the wind was picking up, and with it a fine, dry snow that would not stop for months. The windows were already dark. At this latitude, in this season, the sun did not set so much as fail to appear — a slow abandonment, the light retreating south like an army that has decided the position is not worth holding.

Viktor found the heating controls and set them to sixty-eight degrees. The station's pipes knocked and ticked as they warmed. It was the most domestic sound any of them would hear for a long time.

They chose their rooms. Marten took the one nearest the common room, which was also nearest the kitchen and its knives, though no one mentioned this aloud. Emil took the room at the far end of the corridor, maximizing the distance between himself and everyone else. Falk and Viktor took adjacent rooms, as they always had when they were young and the family moved between safe houses in Antwerp and Rotterdam. Nikolaj took a middle room. Søren took the room across from Nikolaj and left his door open, the only one who did.

That night they ate together — canned stew, bread from the freezer, coffee that Falk made too strong because Falk always made coffee too strong. They talked about the food and the cold and the generator's fuel consumption and whether the snow would reach the windows by January. They did not talk about the letter. They were, for one more evening, six men who shared a father and a childhood of shared concealments, of learning early that a family could be both a shelter and an operation, that love and logistics were conducted through the same channels.

After dinner, Viktor washed the dishes. Falk dried. It was an old arrangement between them, dating to the apartment in Antwerp where the two youngest had been given the chores because the older brothers were given the work. Viktor washed carefully, slowly, holding each plate under the water longer than necessary, and Falk dried with a towel slung over his shoulder and talked about a woman in Oslo who had been, he said, the single most unreasonable person he had ever met, and he included their father in the comparison. Viktor laughed. It was a real laugh — sudden, uncalculated, the kind that escapes before strategy can intervene.

In his room, Nikolaj heard the laugh through the wall and noted it. He did not write it down. He would not begin writing things down until the third day. But he noted it — the ease of it, the timing, the fact that Viktor and Falk could still produce that sound in this place — and he filed it in the part of his mind that was already, despite his efforts, beginning to sort his brothers into categories.

He locked his door. He heard, down the corridor, four other locks engage in sequence.

The wind found a seam in the window frame and made a sound like a sustained, toneless note on a broken instrument. It would not stop making this sound. After the first week, none of them would hear it anymore. After the third week, the silence would be worse.

## II.

On the second morning, Marten gathered them in the common room and proposed a structure. He stood at the head of the table — their father's position, and everyone understood the citation — and said that they should approach this rationally. They should talk. Each man should say where he'd been, what he'd been doing, what access he'd had to the information Europol might want. "We're not animals," Marten said. "We can reason through this."

"And then what?" Emil said. He was sitting with his chair pushed back from the table, one ankle crossed over the other knee, his hands still. Of the six, Emil was the most physically contained. He had been quiet as a child — had not spoken until age four, which their father had taken as either deficiency or strategy, and the ambiguity of that judgment had never been resolved.

"Then we reach a conclusion," Marten said.

"You mean we reach a verdict."

"I mean we determine who it is, and we deal with it."

"Deal with it." Emil uncrossed his ankle. "You can say the word, Marten. He wants us to kill someone. You can say the word."

Marten said the word. He said it flatly, without drama, the way their father said things — as though the content of a statement were unrelated to its emotional register, as though language were a delivery system and the payload was someone else's problem.

They went around the table. Marten reported his movements for the past eighteen months: Hamburg, Antwerp, three weeks in Istanbul, the logistics corridor through Lithuania that was his primary responsibility. He named dates, routes, associates. He offered this information as though filling out a customs form — thorough, dull, verifiable.

Falk went next, and his account was the opposite: expansive, anecdotal, full of characters and incident. Falk managed the family's interests in the Norwegian port system — a network of bribed harbor officials and container-routing arrangements that had been, he said, running beautifully until the Fredrikstad seizure eight months ago, which was when, presumably, someone's information had started leaking. He said this and then looked around the table to see who flinched.

No one flinched. But Nikolaj saw Falk looking, and he noted the looking. And Søren saw Nikolaj noting, and he thought: *Nikolaj is watching who watches. Nikolaj is building a second-order model.* And Søren filed this thought, and Nikolaj saw Søren filing it, and for a moment the two of them held each other's gaze across the table with the perfect mutual awareness of two people who had both just recognized that observation, in this room, was indistinguishable from suspicion.

Viktor's account was brief. He had been running the family's clean-side businesses — the real estate portfolio, the restaurant group in Bergen — and his contact with the operational side had been, he said, minimal. "I barely know what's worth informing about," he said, and smiled, and the smile was either self-deprecating honesty or the single most elegant deflection anyone had offered so far.

Søren spoke last. He had been asked to speak and had initially declined, which required Marten to ask again, which required the table to sit in a silence that lengthened past the point of comfort. When Søren finally spoke, he said: "I was in Copenhagen. I was doing what I was told. I'm not the informant, but I'm aware that saying so is meaningless, because the informant would also say so, and with the same face I'm making now. So I don't know what this exercise is supposed to produce."

"It produces information," Marten said.

"It produces performance," Søren said. "Each of us just performed innocence for twenty minutes. We're very good at it. We were raised to be good at it."

Emil, who had said nothing during his own turn beyond the bare facts of his whereabouts — dates and cities, no texture, no affect — now spoke from his position against the wall. "Søren is right that declaration is worthless. The question isn't what someone says about himself. It's what he does when he thinks no one is watching."

"And you'd know about that," Falk said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means you've been sitting in the corner of rooms for as long as I can remember, Emil. Watching. Saying nothing. Hoarding."

"Hoarding what?"

"Whatever it is quiet people hoard."

This was the first open accusation, and the room registered it the way a seismograph registers a tremor too small to feel — not with alarm but with calibration. Something had been measured. A baseline had shifted.

That afternoon, Nikolaj began writing things down. He used a small notebook he'd brought in his bag — an Amatøren-brand pocket notebook, the kind sold at gas stations, with a red cover and lined pages. He wrote in a personal shorthand he'd developed as a teenager, when their father had taught all six sons basic cryptographic hygiene. The shorthand was not sophisticated — letter substitution, some abbreviation — but it was fast, and it was private.

He wrote:

*M. — leading, performing leadership, needs structure because structure is his authority. Vulnerable if structure breaks down. Not behaving like a man with a secret. Behaving like a man who needs to be seen managing the secret of others.*

*E. — watching. Baseline behavior: always watching. Impossible to distinguish guilty surveillance from habitual surveillance. This is his advantage and his risk. If he is the informant, his silence is cover. If he is not, his silence will convict him anyway.*

*F. — talking too much. Performing fraternity, warmth, normalcy. The Fredrikstad comment was a test — he threw information into the room to see what it disturbed. Smart. But the performance is too visible. An informant embedded for fourteen months would not need to test the room. He would already know it.*

*S. — refusing to play. This is either integrity or the deepest form of strategy. If S. is the informant, his refusal to engage in the identification process is optimal: it positions him as the principled objector, the one who tried to stop the violence, which is exactly the narrative Europol would want their asset to maintain. But it is also exactly what a man who is not the informant and who finds this entire exercise obscene would do.*

*V. — easy. Too easy? The youngest, the least operational, the one with the least access. The obvious non-suspect. But access can be indirect — V. manages the clean-side businesses, and clean-side is where the money surfaces, which is where Europol's financial investigators would focus. V. has the books. V. has every number.*

Nikolaj looked at what he'd written. Every entry was a closed loop — every piece of evidence pointed both toward and away from guilt, every behavior was legible as both innocence and cover. He recognized this as a problem. He did not yet recognize it as the answer.

He tore the page out, folded it, and put it in his pocket. Then he reconsidered. He unfolded it, tore it into strips, and burned the strips one by one over the gas burner in the kitchen, holding each strip with tongs until the ash fell into the sink.

From the doorway, Viktor watched him do this. Nikolaj did not see Viktor. Viktor saw the burning and the care with which it was done, and he thought: *Nikolaj has written something he doesn't want anyone to find.* And because Viktor was the youngest and had spent his life constructing explanations for the behavior of older brothers — translating their silences, interpreting their moods, building models of their motivations the way a child builds models of weather, not to control it but to survive it — he immediately produced three explanations: (1) Nikolaj was keeping analytical notes and burning them for security, which was rational; (2) Nikolaj had written something operationally sensitive, perhaps a communication he'd drafted for someone outside the station, which was suspicious; (3) Nikolaj was performing the act of burning for the benefit of anyone who might see, creating the impression of a man who has secrets that require destroying, which was either very guilty or very clever.

Viktor backed out of the doorway without speaking. He went to Falk's room and knocked on the door, and when Falk opened it, Viktor stepped inside and said: "I think we should talk about Nikolaj."
