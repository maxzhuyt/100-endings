# The Deep Ice

---

## I.

---

**Voss, M. — Field Notes, Station Ísfjall**
**Core Sample Series 77, Deep Stratum**
**Day 184 of Overwintering Rotation**

The Larsen bit reached 1,220 meters at 03:47 UTC. We had expected to transition from Holocene-era compacted firn into the Wisconsin glacial layer by 1,100 meters. Instead, the stratigraphic record showed anomalous banding beginning at approximately 1,140 meters—alternating light and dark laminations inconsistent with any seasonal deposition model I have encountered in twelve years of polar glaciology. The drill motor drew 18% more current through this section. The ice was denser than it should have been. Harder. Reyes said it felt like the bit was cutting through something that did not want to be cut.

That is not what she said, exactly. What she said was, "It's fighting us." I noted it because Reyes does not anthropomorphize. She is a mechanical engineer. She speaks in torque specifications and thermal conductivity values. When Reyes says the ice is fighting, something about the ice has deviated from every parameter she has internalized across four drilling seasons.

I was the one who pulled the core from the barrel. Standard procedure: the cylinder comes up in its housing, you rack it in the cradle under the fluorescent banks, you begin the visual survey before it goes into the cold room. The station hummed around me in its usual frequencies—the ventilation system's low B-flat, the generator's shudder through the floor plates, the irregular crack-and-settle of the ice shelf adjusting beneath us. Ísfjall is never silent. It breathes and complains and shifts in its sleep, and after six months you stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own pulse. It becomes the sound of being alive in a place that would prefer you weren't.

The core was beautiful. I want to record that before I record anything else. It was a beautiful piece of ice.

The upper sections were milky, granular, shot through with air bubbles—atmosphere from centuries before industrialization, trapped in tiny glass-clear spheres. Climate data. The history of what we have burned and what we have breathed, written in bubbles smaller than the head of a pin. Below that, the ice clarified, as it always does at depth: pressure squeezing out the air, compressing the crystal structure until it approached the transparency of optical glass. You could see through it the way you see through a window in winter—not perfectly, but enough. Enough to know something was on the other side.

At 1,187 meters equivalent depth, embedded in ice that predated the oldest known human settlement in the Arctic by a factor I am not yet prepared to state in writing, I observed structures.

Biological structures.

I will describe what I saw without interpretation, because interpretation is where credibility dies and I intend to keep mine.

Suspended in the matrix of the deep core: tissue. Laminar, striated, showing differentiated cell layers visible to the unaided eye under strong transmitted light. A branching network consistent with vascularization. A density gradient suggesting the interface between two tissue types—possibly dermal and subdermal, possibly muscular and connective, I could not determine without sectioning. The tissue was intact. Not mineralized, not fragmentary, not the compressed residue of ancient biology that you find in sedimentary cores and must reconstruct through inference and electron microscopy. Intact. Pliable-looking, even, as though the crystalline matrix had not merely preserved it but maintained it—held it the way a hand cups water.

The color was wrong for any animal tissue I know. Too dark at the center, shading to an amber translucence at the margins, as if something had stained it from within. Or as if the cells themselves contained a pigment I had no reference for.

I called Reyes over. Then Lindgren. Then Okafor. I showed them. I pointed. I held a flashlight behind the core and let them see.

I want to record their faces: the way Reyes went still, the way Lindgren put his hand out and then pulled it back as if the core were hot rather than the coldest thing in the room. The way Okafor said, very quietly, "Maren, that's a person." And I said, "No." And he said, "That's human tissue." And I said, "I know."

It was not a person. It was a piece of a person. Or a piece of something that had arrived at the same biological conclusions a person arrives at—the same solutions to the same structural problems—but had done so in ice that was older than persons. Older than anything that should be a person.

I bagged it, logged it, stored it in the secondary cold room at minus thirty. I filled out the chain-of-custody form. I noted the time, the depth, the GPS coordinates of the borehole, the names of every individual present. I did everything correctly. I have always done everything correctly. I want that established now.

The station settled into its nighttime frequencies around me as I finished the paperwork—the ventilation cycling down, the exterior wind picking up to what sounded like forty knots, the building swaying on its pilings with a motion that was almost maritime, almost gentle, as if we were not on ice but on a sea that had simply forgotten to move. I could hear the deep stratum groaning far below. It does that. All ice does that. The differential pressures, the slow plastic deformation under its own weight, the tide-driven flexion of the shelf—these produce infrasonic vibrations and audible cracking that propagate through the structure of any station built on or into the ice. Everyone learns to sleep through it by day forty.

But that night I heard something beneath the groaning. Or within it. A regularity, a rhythm, a pattern of stress and release that repeated at intervals I found myself counting without deciding to count. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Eleven seconds. Then a pause that lasted exactly as long as three intervals, thirty-three seconds, and then the pattern resumed.

I noted this in the margin of my field book and then crossed it out, because ice does not have rhythm. Ice has physics. And I was tired, and it had been a long day, and the mind finds patterns when the mind is tired. That is the first thing they teach you about polar work: your pattern-recognition software is overclocked by the sensory deprivation, and it will find faces in static and music in wind if you let it.

I did not let it. I went to bed.

But I dreamed the counting continued.

The station is small enough to become the world. That is the first thing to understand about Ísfjall, and the last thing anyone outside the ice will ever fully believe.

Twenty-three people. A main habitation module connected by enclosed walkways to the drilling bay, the laboratory wing, the communications room, the generator housing, and the supply depot. The walkways are unheated and the temperature inside them hovers at minus fifteen on calm days and plunges to minus forty when the wind finds the gaps in the thermal jacketing, which it always does. You move through them quickly, hunched into your layers, and arriving in any heated space feels less like walking through a door than like being born—the shock of warmth, the explosive condensation on your goggles, the sense that the space you've entered is alive and the space you've left is not.

Beyond the station: the ice shelf, the polar plateau, the horizon in every direction. In summer there is light for twenty-two hours and the world is white and blue and the distances are impossible to judge—a pressure ridge that looks a hundred meters away turns out to be three kilometers off and forty meters high. In winter, which it has been for sixty-one days now, there is no light at all except what we make. The darkness outside the windows is not the darkness of a room with the lights off. It is the darkness of a world that has turned its face away from the sun. It is absolute. It has texture and weight. You open an exterior door and it pushes in like water.

We will not leave here until the supply traverse arrives in October. That is one hundred and nine days from now. There is no airstrip. Helicopter range from the nearest base is marginal in summer and impossible in winter darkness. We have a satellite uplink that functions for two hours and forty minutes per day when the orbital window aligns, and it carries voice, text, and low-bandwidth data. I have used my allotted personal minutes to call my sister in Bergen twice this month. Both times the signal fractured her voice into something that sounded less like speech and more like ice cracking, and I found myself holding the handset after the call ended, listening to the static, waiting for it to resolve into something I recognized.

The crew is a good crew. I chose some of them myself, back when choosing was something I did—back when the project was mine, when the drilling program was designed to my specifications, when Ísfjall was a research station doing the work I had spent four years securing funding for. Reyes, who keeps the drill running and can diagnose an electrical fault by the sound it makes. Lindgren, who maintains our weather instruments with a devotion that borders on the spiritual. Okafor, who processes core samples with the careful hands of a man who once restored medieval manuscripts and still, sometimes, handles ancient ice with the same tenderness. Yuen, who manages our food stores and somehow produces meals that taste like more than survival, and who will sit with you in the galley at 2 AM without requiring that you speak. These are the people I chose.

Harlan, I did not choose.

Harlan Corwick came with the station the way the emergency generator came with the station—mandated by the oversight committee, installed before I arrived, non-negotiable. Station Security and Operations Lead. His title was longer than his job description, which was, as far as I could determine: ensure that the personnel and assets of Ísfjall Station remain secure, compliant, and operational. What this meant in practice was that Harlan controlled the communications schedule, managed the supply inventory, enforced the safety protocols, and served as the station director's right hand in all matters that were not strictly scientific. The station director was Brennan, who had a doctorate in geophysics and a temperament suited to administration, which meant he deferred to expertise in technical matters and to Harlan in everything else.

I understood Harlan's function. Every remote installation has one—the person whose job is to keep human beings from doing the things human beings do when they are isolated, confined, and slowly being disassembled by the dark. He monitored morale. He mediated conflicts. He tracked alcohol consumption with a spreadsheet that I once glimpsed on his laptop and that contained more data points than some of my research datasets. He was good at his job. He had a voice that sounded like a door being held open for you, and eyes that were already logging what you carried through it.

I had no reason to fear him. I had no reason to think about him more than I thought about the backup water filtration system or the emergency egress routes—things that existed, that had purpose, that you noted and then forgot because they were infrastructure, not company.

That changed the morning I brought the specimens to the all-hands meeting.

## II.

---

The meeting was in the galley because the galley was the only space that could hold everyone. Twenty-three chairs around tables that had been pushed into a configuration resembling a horseshoe, the open end facing the whiteboard where I had taped my preliminary photographs. The room smelled like coffee and thermal underlayers and the particular staleness that buildings develop when the same air has been cycled through the same filters for six months. Outside, the wind was doing something in the low fifties, knots, and the station's seaward wall flexed microscopically with each gust—not enough to see, but enough to feel in your inner ear, a subtle wrongness in the vertical that made you grip your coffee cup slightly tighter without knowing why.

I presented the findings cleanly. I had spent the previous night organizing the data into a sequence that I believed was airtight: stratigraphic context, visual documentation, preliminary spectroscopic analysis suggesting organic composition, anomalous depth relative to established human presence in the region. I used cautious language. "Consistent with." "Suggestive of." "Warrants further investigation." I did not say *human tissue preserved in ice that predates humanity.* I said *biological structures exhibiting morphological similarities to mammalian tissue observed in a stratum requiring independent dating confirmation.* I said it in the voice I have been trained to use—measured, precise, aware that every claim creates an obligation of proof.

The room was very quiet when I finished. Not the quiet of awe. The quiet of people calculating.

Reyes spoke first. She confirmed the anomalous drilling resistance. Okafor confirmed the core extraction and visual observation. I felt, for a moment, the architecture of corroboration assembling itself—three professionals, consistent accounts, documented evidence. This is how science is supposed to work. You see a thing. Others see the thing. You proceed together toward understanding.

Then Harlan spoke.

He did not stand. He remained in his chair at the back of the horseshoe, legs crossed at the ankle, hands wrapped around a thermos that he held against his sternum like a man warming himself by a fire. He smiled. The smile was the first wrong thing—not because it was hostile, but because it was sympathetic. It was the smile you give someone who has just told you something that reflects well on their passion but poorly on their judgment.

"I want to make sure I understand the chain of custody on this," he said.

His voice was level, warm, curious. He asked me when the core had been extracted. I told him. He asked when I had first observed the anomalous structures. I told him. He asked how long the core had been in my unsupervised possession before I called others to observe it.

"Unsupervised," I repeated.

"I just mean unwitnessed," he said, and the correction sounded like an apology that was actually an escalation. "Standard protocol for anomalous findings requires concurrent observation during extraction. I'm sure it's just a documentation gap."

"I documented—" I began.

"You documented beautifully." Another smile. "I'm thinking about what an external review board would need. That's all. Dotting i's."

He asked three more questions. Each one was reasonable. Each one, taken individually, reflected nothing more than procedural diligence. Taken together, they formed a shape. The shape was: Maren Voss was alone with the ice core before anyone else saw it. Maren Voss has a professional interest in producing dramatic findings. Maren Voss is the source.

He never said this. He did not have to. The questions said it for him, and by the time he was finished asking them, I could see the calculation changing behind the eyes of three or four crew members who had, minutes earlier, been looking at my photographs with something like wonder. Now they were looking at me.

Brennan closed the meeting by thanking me for a "thorough preliminary report" and suggesting that the specimens be secured in the main cold storage under dual-access protocol—two keycards required for entry—pending "a more rigorous assessment of the stratigraphic data." Harlan would manage the access log. This was, Brennan noted, simply good practice. He said "simply good practice" the way you say "it's nothing personal," which is to say: it was entirely personal.
