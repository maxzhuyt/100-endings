# The Only Donor

## I.

The antibody profile rendered on the screen at 11:47 p.m. like a sentence in a language only a few hundred people in the world could read fluently, and Nora Kaplan was one of them. She had been one of them for twenty-two years. Tonight, in the hematology lab on the seventh floor of Massachusetts General, where the overhead lights were off and the only illumination came from the monitor and the blue standby glow of a centrifuge she'd run four hours ago, that fluency felt less like a credential than a cruelty.

The profile was specific. That was the problem. That had always been the problem.

Lily's crisis had been cascading for nine days—autoimmune hemolytic anemia complicated by an alloantibody constellation that made conventional transfusion a game of Russian roulette with five loaded chambers. The first crossmatch had been incompatible. The second. The third. Nora had stood in this lab and watched the agglutination reactions bloom under the microscope, her daughter's borrowed red cells clumping and lysing against every unit the blood bank could source, and she had understood with the particular clarity of her training that the standard options were closing, one by one, like doors in a corridor.

She had submitted the extended phenotype to the national registry herself. She had specified the parameters: AB-negative, high-titer, with anti-Kpa and anti-Jsa in a combination that occurred in fewer than one in four hundred thousand donors. The registry's algorithm had searched its 11.2 million entries. It had returned one match.

Nora stared at the name.

Her hand, which had been resting on the keyboard, withdrew. She placed it in her lap. She looked at the name for a long time.

**MAREN CROSS**

Status: Inactive. Last contact: 2021. Last known address: Seattle, WA. Phone: disconnected.

Nora's pulse did something she could have described clinically—a premature atrial contraction, the vagal response to sudden psychic stress—but what she experienced was simpler and older than any clinical description. It was the feeling of a door she had sealed shut ten years ago being kicked open from the other side.

She pressed print.

The printer in the corner of the lab activated with a mechanical stutter. In the silence of the seventh floor, it sounded enormous. The single page slid into the tray. Nora did not get up to retrieve it immediately. She sat in the blue light and performed, with rigorous internal discipline, the calculation she already knew the answer to.

There was no second match. There was no workaround. The therapeutic window for plasma exchange was narrowing at a rate determined by Lily's own immune system, which was destroying her red blood cells with the same methodical efficiency with which it had once fought off ear infections and stomach bugs—the body's defense apparatus turned executioner, unable to distinguish self from enemy. At current hemolysis rates, Lily had perhaps eight days before organ damage became irreversible. The plasma they needed had to come from a living donor with that exact antibody profile, and the national registry—the most comprehensive in the world, the one Nora herself had helped expand during her years as a researcher—contained exactly one such person.

Maren Cross. Who had stood behind a podium at the American Society of Hematology conference in December 2014 and told a room of four hundred people that Nora Kaplan had fabricated data in three published studies. Who had presented doctored audit trails with Nora's electronic signature. Who had sat on the review panel that recommended retraction. Who had subsequently been awarded the NIH R01 grant—$2.3 million over five years—that had been Nora's, built on research that had been Nora's, for work on alloantibody desensitization protocols that Nora had designed in a notebook Maren had access to because they shared a lab, shared coffee, shared eleven years of friendship that ended in a conference room with institutional lawyers and the words *research misconduct*.

Nora had not fabricated anything. The investigation, grinding on for fourteen months, had ultimately been inconclusive—not exoneration but a procedural shrug, a determination that the evidence was "insufficient to sustain the charge," which in academic medicine meant: we can't prove you did it, but the stain is permanent. By then the grants were gone, the collaborators had quietly distanced themselves, the lab had been reassigned. Nora had rebuilt—had clawed her way into clinical practice, had found she was good at the diagnostic puzzles of the blood bank even if the research world had expelled her—but the rebuilding had cost her a marriage, six years of her daughter's childhood spent in the numb haze of professional survival, and something less quantifiable but more important: the version of herself that had once believed expertise and rigor could protect you from anything.

She picked up the printout. She folded it once and put it in the breast pocket of her lab coat.

In the elevator going down to the lobby, she caught her reflection in the polished steel doors. She looked exactly like what she was: a forty-six-year-old woman carrying a piece of paper that contained both the cure and the cost.

## II.

Lily's room in the pediatric ICU was 7-South-14, a corner unit with a window that looked out over the ambulance bay. The curtains were printed with hot-air balloons. There was a crayon drawing taped to the IV pole—a yellow house, a purple dog, the sky a single thick stripe of blue pressed hard enough to gouge the paper—and beneath it a small whiteboard on which the night nurse had written *Lily K., 7 y/o, Day 11* and a smiley face that was missing an eye where the dry-erase marker had skipped.

It was 6:20 in the morning. Nora had not slept. She sat in the vinyl recliner beside the bed, holding a juice box with the straw bent at the angle Lily preferred—forty-five degrees, no more, a specificity Lily enforced with the solemnity of case law—and watched her daughter sleep.

The petechiae had spread overnight. They covered Lily's forearms in a stippled rash of pinpoint hemorrhages, each one a tiny capillary that had bled into the skin because there were not enough platelets to seal it. They looked, in the gray early light, like a field of red freckles, almost decorative, and this was the thing about the disease that Nora found most obscene: it aestheticized its own damage. Her daughter was decorated with her dying.

Lily's nose was packed with hemostatic gauze. The nosebleed had started at 2 a.m. and lasted forty minutes. The nurse had paged Nora—she'd been in the on-call room, not sleeping, staring at the ceiling—and Nora had come and held the gauze in place herself, pressing with exactly the right pressure, angling Lily's head forward, doing everything correctly while her daughter breathed through her mouth and looked up at her with eyes that were calm and brown and enormous.

"Mom," Lily had said, nasally, through the packing. "My platelets are being a-holes."

Lily had heard the word from a resident. She deployed it with precision and relish.

"They are," Nora had said.

Now Lily was asleep, and Dr. Amari was standing at the foot of the bed with a tablet, speaking to Nora in the voice. She knew the voice. She had used the voice herself—the measured, warm, carefully paced register that physicians adopt when they are speaking to the families of very sick patients, when they are translating catastrophe into language that can be received. It was the voice of calibrated compassion. She had never expected to be on the receiving end.

"Her hemoglobin dropped to 5.8 overnight," Amari said. "We transfused two units of the least-incompatible packed cells we had, but her antibody screen—"

"Positive on all panels," Nora said. "I ran them."

Amari paused. He was a good doctor—she had chosen him for Lily specifically because he was thorough and careful and did not ego-manage upward—but he struggled, sometimes, with the fact that Lily's mother was a hematologist who already knew everything he was going to say before he said it. He was trying to deliver information to a woman for whom the information was not information but confirmation.

"The direct antiglobulin test is strongly positive," he said, more slowly. "The eluate shows warm auto-anti-e overlapping with allo-anti-Kpa and anti-Jsa. We can't find compatible units. The last three crossmatches—"

"Incompatible at AHG phase. I know."

A silence.

"Nora," Amari said. He put the tablet down. He was not looking at the screen anymore. He was looking at her. "If we don't find compatible plasma for exchange within the next six to eight days, we're looking at progressive hemolysis, renal compromise, and—"

"I know what we're looking at."

He nodded. He did not finish the sentence. He didn't need to. They both knew the word he had not said. They both knew the sequence: hemolysis → hemoglobinuria → acute tubular necrosis → multi-organ failure. It was a cascade with its own grammar, its own inevitability, once the early clauses were uttered.

"I found a donor," Nora said.

Amari's expression changed. Relief—but cautious, hedged, the relief of a doctor who knows that *found* is not the same as *secured*.

"In the registry?"

"The national donor registry returned one match. AB-negative, anti-Kpa and anti-Jsa, high-titer. One person."

"One."

"One."

"And you've contacted—"

"Not yet. There are complications."

She did not elaborate. Amari waited, but she offered nothing further, and after a moment he picked up his tablet and said he would check back in two hours and that she should try to rest.

After he left, Nora sat with the juice box still in her hand, the straw still bent to forty-five degrees, and watched her daughter breathe. Lily's chest rose and fell. The cardiac monitor beeped. On the muted television above the bed, a cartoon octopus was teaching smaller cartoon fish to count in Spanish.

Lily's stuffed bear, Dr. Paws—named by Lily at age four, retained with fierce loyalty through three years of hospitalizations—sat propped against the pillow. Lily had, at some point during the night, wrapped a length of medical tape around Dr. Paws's arm and inserted the cap of a pen into the crook of the bear's elbow, fashioning a miniature IV line. She had written, in her careful second-grade printing, on a scrap of paper taped to the bear's chest: *PLATLETS LOW. NO FOODS PAST MIDNITE.*

Nora read this note and did not allow herself to make a sound. She set the juice box on the tray table. She smoothed the blanket over Lily's legs, very gently, so as not to press on the bruises she could see blooming along the shins, fresh since yesterday, purple-black and shapeless.

Then she took her phone into the hallway and began making calls.

## III.

The digital trail for Maren Cross ended in July 2021.

Before that, there were the expected artifacts of an academic career in managed decline: a final co-authored paper in *Transfusion* in 2019, listed fourth among six authors, a position that spoke of diminished standing. A university directory listing at Oregon Health & Science University, then removed. A LinkedIn profile with a headshot from 2016—Maren's face sharp and angular, dark hair pulled back, the half-smile of institutional portraiture—last updated two years before the profile went dormant. A forwarding address in Portland, then Seattle, then nothing.

Nora sat at her kitchen table at 10 p.m., two days after the registry result, her laptop open, a legal pad filling with dead ends. She had called OHSU's hematology department and been told Dr. Cross was no longer affiliated. She had called the Seattle address, which was an apartment complex; the manager said the tenant had left eighteen months ago, no forwarding. She had tried three phone numbers, all disconnected. She had emailed the last known academic address and received an automated bounce: *This mailbox is no longer monitored*.

Maren Cross had done what people do when they want to stop being found. She had allowed her connections to the findable world to atrophy, one by one, like capillaries closing.

But Nora was a diagnostician. She worked backward from what she had.

The 2019 paper listed a co-author named David Kerrigan, a immunohematology researcher at the University of Washington. Nora called him at his office line, got voicemail, left a message that was carefully vague. He called back the next morning. He remembered Maren. He'd heard she'd left academics. No, he didn't know where she'd gone. He hesitated—a beat too long—and then said, "You might try her cousin. Brenda something. Brenda Hallvik. She was at a conference dinner once. I think she lives in Juneau."

Brenda Hallvik's number was listed. She answered on the fourth ring, and when Nora said Maren's name, the silence on the other end was long enough that Nora could hear a television in the background, the rise and fall of a studio audience laughing.

"Who is this?" Brenda said.

Nora gave her name. Another silence.

"I know who you are," Brenda said. Her voice was flat, unrevealing. "Why are you looking for her?"

"It's a medical matter. I'm not able to share details, but it's urgent."

"She doesn't want to be found."

"I understand that. But this is—it's life or death. I'm not being dramatic. It is literally a question of whether someone lives or dies."

The television audience laughed again. Brenda said nothing for so long that Nora thought she'd hung up. Then: "I'm not going to give you her address. But I'll pass along your number. If she wants to call, she'll call."

Maren did not call. Three days passed. Lily's hemoglobin dropped to 5.2. A new petechial rash appeared on her chest, spreading across her sternum like a spray of red ink.

On the fourth day, Nora called Brenda back. This time Brenda did not answer. Nora called again that evening. No answer. She called a third time at 10 p.m. Alaska time, knowing it was rude, knowing she did not care.

Brenda answered with the clipped tone of a person who is being asked to serve as a conduit for a conflict that is not hers.

"She didn't call," Nora said.

"I passed your message along."

"Brenda, I need you to hear me. I'm a hematologist. My daughter is seven years old and she has a blood disorder that will kill her without a specific kind of plasma donation. Your cousin is the only compatible donor in the national registry. I am not exaggerating. I am not speaking in metaphor. One person. One match. Your cousin."

The longest silence yet. Then Brenda exhaled hard and said: "She's in Tenakee Springs. It's on Chichagof Island. You can get there by floatplane from Juneau or the state ferry from—"

"I'll find it."

"She goes by Maren Eld now. She works at—there's a small operation, they do smoked fish, tourist stuff. She's there most days." A pause. "She's not the same person she was."

"I don't need her to be the same person. I need her blood."

Brenda said nothing to this. The call ended.

Nora arrived in Juneau on a Wednesday in early October. She had arranged for Amari and a hematology fellow to manage Lily in her absence. She had kissed her daughter's forehead and told her she was going to get the medicine, the special medicine that was far away, and she would be back soon.

"How far away?" Lily had asked.

"Alaska."

Lily had considered this with the grave thoughtfulness she applied to all geographical claims. "Is Alaska past Connecticut?"

"Way past Connecticut."

"Okay." Then: "Bring Dr. Paws a fish."
