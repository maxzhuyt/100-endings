# Perdition Gulch

---

## I.

The town lay in the crease of two hills like a letter someone had started and never finished. From above — and this story begins from above, because that is where you must stand to see a thing whole before it comes apart — Perdition Gulch was a single unpaved street, thirty-one structures of varying honesty, a church without a steeple, and a well.

The well bore mentioning.

It had been dug in 1869 by a man named Farraday who was looking for water at forty feet and found it at one hundred and sixty, which should have told him something about the land's willingness to give. He kept digging past the water, or so the story went. His wife said he would lower himself down on the rope at dawn and she would feel the slack run out at a depth that could not be right, that exceeded the length of rope she had watched him coil. Farraday never explained what he was looking for past the water table. He sold the claim and moved to California and was struck by a cable car on his second day in San Francisco, which was the kind of fate that befell people who left Perdition Gulch, though no one said so.

The drought had entered its seventh month. The creek bed that cut behind the livery stable carried dust in the shape of water, a perfect fossil of a current, every ripple preserved in clay as if the earth had decided to remember the river even after the river had forgotten itself. The hills were the color of an old bruise — yellow at their crests, purple in their hollows where the sage still clung to something deep and private. Rain was discussed the way the dead are discussed: fondly, without expectation of return.

And yet the town functioned. This is the fact that should have alarmed a careful observer and did not.

Land disputes were settled. Wounds closed. Fugitives were found. Town meetings achieved quorum. These are ordinary civic achievements, not worth mentioning in a place with ordinary resources, but Perdition Gulch had no marshal, no lawyer, no physician, and a population of eighty-three souls spread across forty miles of territory that would have been called godforsaken if anyone still invoked God with conviction. The town should not have worked. It worked anyway, in the way a house built on a questionable foundation works: so long as you do not go into the cellar, everything appears sound.

The reasons for this — and they were five — could be observed on any given day by anyone who chose to look, which no one did, because looking was the one thing Perdition Gulch had agreed, collectively and without discussion, never to do.

---

On a Tuesday in September of 1887, Abilene Cask stood in the packed-dirt yard between the Howell and Prester properties and settled an argument that had been running, by her count, for eleven years, four months, and six days.

"The terms," she said, "were as follows." She was a narrow woman, tall, with a face that had been made for stillness — broad cheekbones, a mouth that rested in a line neither kind nor unkind, eyes the color of creek water when there had been creek water. She wore her hair pulled back so tightly it seemed to taper her skull, and she stood with her hands clasped behind her, and when she spoke, her voice carried the quality of something being read from a ledger rather than conjured from memory, because for Abilene, there was no difference.

"On the fourteenth of May, 1876, Jacob Howell and Desmond Prester stood at the eastern fence post — the one with the notch, Desmond, the one you later replaced, though the replacement post is three inches west of the original, which is part of the problem — and shook hands on the following: that the grazing rights east of the gully extended to Prester cattle from April through September, and to Howell cattle from October through March, and that neither party would move the fence, and that the terms would hold until one or both parties sold the land or died. Jacob, you said, and I am quoting, 'I suppose that's fair enough, though I still think the gully ought to be mine outright.' And Desmond, you said, 'It ought to be, but it isn't, and that's what a handshake is for.' You then spat, Jacob. Desmond did not."

The two men — both older now, both carrying the leathered grievance of ranchers who have argued over the same line of dirt for so long it has become a kind of marriage — stood with their hats in their hands and said nothing for a moment. There was nothing to say. The woman had been present the day of that handshake. Everyone knew this. She had been present for every handshake, every verbal contract, every deathbed instruction, every whispered promise in Perdition Gulch since her arrival fourteen years ago, and she remembered each one with a fidelity that was not merely impressive but total — not the gist, not the drift, but the *exact words in the exact order with the exact weather and the exact angle of the light*, and this was not discussed, just as one does not discuss the depth of a well or the speed at which a wound closes or any of the other things that kept the town upright.

"The post moved three inches west," Abilene said. "Move it back. The terms hold."

Desmond Prester nodded. Jacob Howell put his hat on and walked to his horse and rode home to move the post, and that was the end of it, as it always was.

---

On the same day, in the back room of what had once been a barber shop and was now the closest thing Perdition Gulch had to a surgery, a man named Morrow held his hand over a wound in a rancher's side and did not move.

The bullet had entered below the eighth rib and lodged against something that, in a larger town, would have required a surgeon with instruments and ether and a thirteen-percent chance of killing the patient on the table. Morrow had neither instruments nor ether. What he had were his hands — large, weather-cracked, belonging to a man of perhaps forty-five who carried himself with the careful slowness of someone aware that he was stronger than the situation typically required. His hair was black and had not grayed, and his eyes were set deep beneath a brow that gave him a look of perpetual consideration, as though he were working a calculation no one had asked him to perform.

He had removed the bullet with forceps — he owned forceps; let no one say he relied entirely on the other thing — and now he pressed his right palm against the wound and held it there, and the rancher, a young man named Polk, gripped the edges of the table and breathed through his teeth and watched Morrow's face for a sign that the ordinary medical process was underway. It was not. What was underway was something else, and Polk could feel it — a heat that did not originate from the hand, an ebbing of pain that occurred on the wrong schedule, too soon, too completely, as though the body were being *reminded* of its undamaged state and was simply agreeing to return there.

"Keep the bandage on for a week," Morrow said, lifting his hand. Beneath it, the wound had closed to the degree that a wound of this severity might close after a month of clean healing. The skin was pink, new, slightly raised, as if time had been folded and the weeks of recovery compressed into the space beneath Morrow's palm, and this was not discussed.

"A week," Polk said.

"And don't ride for two."

"I rode here with a bullet in me."

"And you'll ride home without one. Two weeks."

Polk looked at the wound. He looked at Morrow. There was a question that lived in the space between them, and it had lived there for every patient Morrow had ever treated, and it was never asked, because asking it would require a kind of courage that had nothing to do with bullets and everything to do with the social contract of a town that preferred its miracles to remain unmarked.

"I thank you," Polk said. He reached for his shirt.

"Leave two dollars with the post if you think of it," Morrow said, and turned to wash his hands in a basin, and the water ran faintly pink, and the light in the room was the color of old gold, and outside the window the dirt street baked in the heat of a sun that had not been interrupted by a cloud in thirty-one days.

---

On the same day, a boy of approximately ten — his age was approximate because no one knew where he had come from, only that he had been in Perdition Gulch for as long as anyone could remember, which, given Abilene's presence, meant he had arrived in the autumn of 1873, walking out of the eastern hills barefoot and unspeaking and staying — a boy called Sutton stood at the edge of the Garrett property, his hand on the top rail of a fence, his head tilted at an angle that might have been curiosity and might have been listening.

The sheriff — Perdition Gulch had a sheriff in the way it had a church, nominally, a man named Breck who wore the star because no one else would and who enforced the law by consensus and exhaustion — the sheriff had been riding the perimeter for three days looking for a man who had stolen four horses from the Olmstead ranch and vanished into the draws and coulees east of town as if the land had swallowed him.

Sutton was standing at the fence when Breck rode past.

"He's in the Carrizo barn," Sutton said.

Breck reined up. The boy did not look at him. He was looking east, toward the barn in question, which sat a mile distant in a property that had been abandoned two years prior when the Carrizo family moved to Denver.

"How do you know that?"

Sutton appeared to consider this question with the seriousness it did not quite deserve. "Just a feeling," he said.

The sheriff looked at the boy. The boy looked east. The feeling — if that is what it was — had a quality of certainty that Breck had learned not to question, because in seven years, the boy had never been wrong, not once, not about the location of lost cattle or the intentions of strangers or the weather, and this was not discussed.

Breck found the man in the Carrizo barn, asleep in the hayloft with the four horses picketed in the corral outside and a pistol he'd been too tired to keep in his hand.

---

On the same day, in the building that served as the town hall — a grandiose title for a structure that was, in truth, a repurposed feed store with benches — a meeting was held to address the water table's decline. Seventeen residents attended. The discussion concerned whether to deepen the well or dig a new one, and the cost of either, and who would bear it, and whether the town could survive another three months of drought if neither was done — the sort of discussion that, in a town of eighty-three people and no tax base, could spiral into acrimony and paralysis and the kind of slow civic death that had claimed a hundred frontier settlements before this one and would claim a hundred after.

The twins spoke.

They were identical in the way that unnerved people who had not grown up around them — the same narrow jaw, the same sand-colored hair cut to the same length, the same economy of gesture, as though they had been allocated a single body's worth of motion and divided it precisely between them. Lark sat in the front row. Essie sat in the back, near the door, ostensibly because she had arrived late. They had not conferred. They did not need to.

"The issue is not the well," Lark said.

"The issue is whether we trust the aquifer," Essie said from the back of the room, her sentence beginning before Lark's had finished, not interrupting but *continuing*, as though both mouths drew from the same lung. Seventeen heads did not turn. They had learned not to turn.

"If we dig deeper, we bet on depth."

"If we dig new, we bet on location."

"Either way, we bet."

"So the question is what we're willing to lose," Essie said.

"And that's not a water question," Lark said.

"That's a trust question," they said, at the same time, from opposite ends of the room, in the same cadence, at the same volume, with the same pause before and after, and the silence that followed was not shock — it had long since stopped being shock — but the settled, practiced silence of people who lived in a town where impossible things happened at the register of town governance and who had decided, collectively, without discussion, that this was simply how things worked in Perdition Gulch.

The vote was taken. The well would be deepened. The cost would be shared. The meeting adjourned.

---

These five — the woman who could not forget, the man who closed wounds by touching them, the boy who heard what others thought, the twins who shared a mind across any distance — were not a council. No one had elected them. No charter named them. No authority had been formally granted, because formal authority would have required formal acknowledgment, and formal acknowledgment would have required the town to say aloud what it had spent fourteen years agreeing not to say, which was that Perdition Gulch was kept alive by five people who could do things that people could not do, and that the ordinary mechanisms of survival — law, medicine, memory, judgment — were not ordinary here, had never been ordinary, and that every resident who remained did so in a state of voluntary and mutual blindness, a compact of denial so complete that it had taken on the character of faith.

The narrator of this account has seen other compacts. The narrator has watched other towns in other territories build their peace on things they chose not to name. The narrator has infinite patience and no stake in the outcome of this particular arrangement, which is not cruelty but simply the condition of witnessing: you see the weight on the beam, and you see the crack in the beam, and you do not reach out, because you are not that kind of story.

The crack had already appeared.

None of the five had seen it yet.

## II.

Here is an earlier day.

The smoke rose from the basin in a column so straight it appeared architectural — a pillar, a monument, the kind of structure that implies intention — and then it caught the wind at a height of perhaps eighty feet and folded east, dispersing as it went but not before it had stained the sky with a color that was not the color of wood smoke or grass fire but of burning canvas and burning wool and burning hair, which are three different shades of gray tending toward brown, and a woman on a ridge a quarter mile to the north catalogued each shade without wanting to, because she could not stop herself from cataloguing, because she had never once in her life seen a thing and failed to retain it in its full and unabridged detail, and this was not a gift but a condition, and on this particular afternoon in the autumn of 1873, it became a sentence.

Abilene Cask was twenty-two years old. She had been traveling alone for six months, moving west from a place she did not name, then or ever, carrying what she owned in a canvas pack and wearing men's trousers and a hat that shadowed her face sufficiently to discourage conversation. She had stopped on the ridge to eat. From this elevation, the basin below was legible: sixteen wagons arranged in an inward-facing crescent, the standard configuration of a migrant train at rest. Horses on a picket line. A cookfire, still smoking from the midday meal. Children — she could see children, four or five of them, running between the wagons in the purposeless joy of children who have been told to stay close but have not yet been told why.

The men came from the east draw. She counted them as they appeared — nine, then twelve, then fifteen — mounted, moving at a pace that was not a charge but not a casual approach, the pace of men who had done this before and knew that speed mattered less than angle of approach, and she understood with the instantaneous clarity of a mind that did not process information so much as *receive it whole* what was about to happen, and she did not move.

She did not move.

She would remember this — she would always remember this, would never have the mercy of forgetting, would carry the next forty minutes in a fidelity that no human memory was designed to sustain — and the first gunshot crossed the dry air at a speed that arrived as silence and then as sound, a flat crack that flushed birds from a stand of juniper to her left, and then there were more, overlapping, and then the screaming began.
