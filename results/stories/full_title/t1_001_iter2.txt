# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

*Edited and annotated by Kurata Yasunari*

---

## Editor's Preface

The documents collected here comprise the complete surviving correspondence of the man known, during his years of public activity, as Ōnishi Hideo, and known thereafter — for reasons this preface will attempt to address with what objectivity the compiler can muster — as the monk Ikkyū Hideo of the Eigen-ji temple in Ōmi Province. These thirty-seven letters, spanning a period of roughly thirty-eight years, were sent to a single recipient, and have remained, until now, in that recipient's private possession.

A word on provenance is necessary. The letters arrived without regularity — sometimes two in a season, sometimes none for more than a year. They were written on a variety of papers: the earliest on high-quality *torinoko* of the kind stocked by temple scriptoriums, the later on whatever was evidently at hand, including, on two occasions, paper so coarse it has resisted preservation. The ink is consistent in the earlier letters — a dense, well-ground *sumi* applied with exceptional control — and markedly less so in the later, where the brushwork, while never losing its fundamental character, shows evidence of cold fingers, poor light, or the kind of tremor that attends certain illnesses of age. I note these material details because they are the sort of thing a responsible editor notes. The letters are presented in chronological order to the extent this can be reliably established. Where dates are absent from the letters themselves, I have relied on postmarks, internal references to datable events, or, in three cases, my own records of receipt.

The facts of the matter are broadly known and, I expect, the reason any reader has taken up this volume. In the autumn of the twenty-third year of Meiji, Ōnishi Hideo stood as the undefeated champion of the Dai-Nippon Butoku Kai invitational tournament, a title he had held for four consecutive years. He was twenty-nine years of age. His opponent in the final match was a man seven years his senior, a practitioner of some reputation, representing the Hokushin Ittō-ryū tradition. The match was expected to be closely contested. It was not. At the moment of engagement — or, more precisely, at the moment immediately preceding engagement — Ōnishi lowered his shinai, stepped backward, and offered a formal bow of concession. No strike had been attempted by either party. The referee confirmed the forfeit. The tournament concluded. Ōnishi Hideo was not seen in any public hall or dōjō again. Within six months, it was reported that he had taken vows at Eigen-ji. Within a year, the matter had been largely forgotten by all but the most dedicated chroniclers of tournament history, and the man who had stood opposite him that afternoon went on to a long career of teaching, modest competition, and — it must be said — a species of reputation that attached to him not through any exertion of his own but through the simple, stubborn arithmetic of the record book: he was the man who had ended Ōnishi Hideo's unbeaten run, even if the manner of ending was not one either party would have chosen to boast of.

I have chosen to publish these letters now because my health is such that the question of their disposition has become practical rather than theoretical. I have no children. My senior students are fine swordsmen but indifferent custodians of paper. The alternative to publication is destruction, and while I have considered that course — I have considered it, I will say, with more seriousness than this mild sentence conveys — I find I cannot execute it. The letters have scholarly interest. They illuminate the thinking of a practitioner whose competitive record remains, despite the brevity of his career, among the most remarkable of the modern era. They contain observations on technique, philosophy, and the ethics of martial practice that deserve wider attention. These are my reasons.

I should note, finally, that Letter Fourteen bears a water stain across its lower third that has rendered several characters illegible. I have not attempted reconstruction. The stain is old — decades old, at minimum — and its source is not rainwater, as the paper shows no evidence of having been exposed to weather. I mention this only for completeness.

K.Y.
Shōwa 4, Third Month
Tokyo

## I.

## Letter One

*Ninth Month, Twenty-Third Year of Meiji*
*Eigen-ji, Ōmi Province*

Kurata-sensei —

Forgive the intrusion of a letter where silence might be more appropriate. I am uncertain of the etiquette. Is there a convention for correspondence between men who have stood opposite one another only once, and briefly? I suspect not. I suspect we are composing the etiquette as I write this, which is a situation that would have amused my old teacher Murakami — who, as you may know, held that convention exists primarily to spare us the terror of having to mean what we say.

I am at Eigen-ji. This is perhaps already known to you, or perhaps not; I have no sense of what news circulates and what does not. The temple is old and small and very cold in the mornings. The abbot is a man of great intelligence who employs the Rinzai method, which is to say he asks impossible questions and then strikes one with a stick for answering them. I find this less different from my previous training than I expected.

I write to you for a reason I cannot fully articulate, which is an unpromising beginning to any correspondence but an honest one. I wanted to say: I hope you are well. I hope the school is well. I hope the winter is gentle where you are, though I suspect Tokyo does not traffic in gentleness of any season.

I am learning to copy sutras. My brushwork is adequate. The older monks are patient with me, which I find more difficult to bear than criticism.

With respect,
Hideo

**[Editor's note:** *Received Ninth Month, twenty-second day. This is the earliest of the letters and arrived approximately six months after the events of the tournament. I responded within the week. The protocol for such a reply was, as he correctly observed, nonexistent. I wrote three lines expressing a wish for his health and thanking him for thinking to write. Whether this was the correct thing to do, I cannot say. It was the thing I did.*
**— K.Y.]**

## Letter Two

*First Month, Twenty-Fourth Year of Meiji*

Kurata-sensei —

Thank you for your reply, which I read several times — not because the meaning was unclear, but because the handwriting was interesting. You write with more pressure on the downstroke than I expected of a man whose technique favors lateral movement. Perhaps this is an impertinent observation. I find monastic life has not cured me of the habit of reading bodies in everything.

The new year at Eigen-ji begins with a ceremony of bells that lasts from the deepest hour of night until dawn. One hundred and eight strikes, for the one hundred and eight worldly desires. I was assigned the forty-third through the fifty-first and acquitted myself, I think, respectably. The sound is extraordinary. Each strike erases the one before it. By the end, the air itself feels scoured. The abbot says the point is not to ring the bell but to become unnecessary to the bell's ringing. I am still thinking about what he means by this.

The cold here is very pure. It arrives without malice. I have learned to sit in it without resistance, which is not the same as sitting without discomfort but which the older monks assure me is the important thing. I believe them and do not believe them in alternating minutes.

I hope the new year finds your students sharp and your dōjō warm.

With respect,
Hideo

**[Editor's note:** *Received First Month, twenty-eighth day. I wrote back within ten days.*
**— K.Y.]**

## Letter Three

*Eighth Month, Twenty-Fourth Year of Meiji*

Kurata-sensei —

Summer in Ōmi. The cicadas are relentless — they begin at the first light and do not stop until the dark has fully established itself, and even then some few insist on continuing, as if contractually obligated to fill every silence. I have come to think of them as the most committed practitioners in the temple. They do not question their purpose. They do not vary their form. They do not stop to consider whether the sound they are making is beautiful or necessary. They simply produce it, with total commitment, until they die.

I wonder whether you have had the chance to see the new intake at the Butoku Kai this year. I heard a rumor — carried by a visiting priest who had it from a merchant who had it, I imagine, from the air itself — that the standard has fallen. I am skeptical. The standard always falls in the telling; this is a feature of memory, not of history. I suspect the young men are as fierce and earnest as we were, and that they will look up one day and find that a decade has passed and some new young man is being described as the end of a long decline.

There is an apprentice monk newly arrived — sixteen or seventeen years old, from Kyoto. He copies sutras in the afternoons. His brushwork is technically precise but cautious in a way that produces a particular kind of error: the characters are correct but lifeless, each stroke arriving at its expected destination by the expected route, so that the whole page has the look of a thing completed rather than a thing made. The senior monks correct his grip, his posture, his ink preparation. None of them says what I see, which is that the boy is afraid of the space between intention and contact — that narrow interval when the brush is moving and the outcome is not yet fixed. He controls what should be released. The result is characters that are technically unimpeachable and spiritually inert. I have not mentioned this to him. I am not his teacher. It is not my observation to give.

I hope you are keeping well in the heat. The summers in Tokyo were always impossible.

With respect,
Hideo

**[Editor's note:** *Received Ninth Month, third day. He wrote this fourteen months after the forfeit. I read it seven times.*
**— K.Y.]**

## Letter Four

*Third Month, Twenty-Fifth Year of Meiji*

Kurata-sensei —

Your account of the school's renovation is wonderful — I can picture the chaos perfectly. Murakami-sensei always said a dōjō reveals its character during construction, when the students must practice among sawdust and confusion. That you have apparently been training your students on the temple grounds down the road, among the gravestones, strikes me as either deeply philosophical or deeply improper. I choose to believe it is both.

Spring here. The plum tree in the eastern courtyard has produced an extravagant display — the blossoms appeared almost overnight, so that one morning the branch was bare and the next it was heavy with white, the petals so dense they seemed to weigh more than wood. I was sweeping the walkway when I saw it and stopped for a long time. The abbot walked past and said nothing, simply detoured around me, which is his form of approval.

I have been thinking about precision. Not martial precision — or not only that — but the broader question of what precision costs. To be precise is to exclude. The precise calligrapher commits to this stroke and not that one; the precise swordsman to this angle and not that one. Each choice closes a universe of alternatives. I wonder sometimes whether the deepest practitioners in any art are those who have found a way to be precise without being narrow — who can commit fully to a single line while remaining somehow open to the lines they have declined. I do not know if this is possible. I suspect it is the kind of thought that sounds profound in a letter and dissolves under scrutiny. Feel free to discard it.

With respect,
Hideo

**[Editor's note:** *Received Fourth Month, tenth day.*
**— K.Y.]**

## Letter Five

*Eleventh Month, Twenty-Fifth Year of Meiji*

Kurata-sensei —

Thank you for the news about Shimada. I did not know he had opened a school in Osaka. He was always the most cheerful of us — do you remember the year he arrived at the invitational with his shinai wrapped in festival ribbons? The judges were appalled. Shimada was entirely unrepentant. I think he understood something the rest of us were too serious to see: that the forms exist to serve the spirit, not the reverse, and that a man who cannot laugh in a dōjō has confused discipline with rigor mortis.

The first snow came early this year. I watched it from the engawa — that particular silence that snowfall brings, not an absence of sound but a muffling, as though the world has put a finger to its lips. The younger monks went out to shovel the paths. I was not asked to help, which I take as either a commentary on my age (I am thirty) or my shoveling (poor).

I read your thoughts on *zanshin* with great interest. You describe it as the persistence of awareness after the cut — the mind that does not withdraw when the body does. This is the standard understanding and, I think, the correct one for a teacher of your students' level. But I find myself wondering about its inverse: the awareness that persists *before* the cut — the mind that has already arrived at the outcome while the body is still in motion. Is that also zanshin? Or is it something else — something without a name, or something with a name I have forgotten?

I realize this is becoming one of those letters that sounds like a lecture, which was not my intention. I will stop.

The snow is still falling.

With respect,
Hideo

**[Editor's note:** *Received Twelfth Month, second day. For context: the passage on zanshin to which he refers appeared in a letter I sent him in the Ninth Month, responding to what I took as an implicit invitation, in Letter Four, to exchange views on martial philosophy. It was my longest letter to him at that point — perhaps four pages. I did not keep copies of my own letters, a failure of archival practice I regret.*
**— K.Y.]**

## Letter Seven

*Fifth Month, Twenty-Sixth Year of Meiji*
*(Excerpt)*

... the garden here operates on a principle I have come to think of as *structured neglect*. The head gardener — a monk of perhaps seventy who has never, to my knowledge, spoken in complete sentences — maintains certain beds with severe precision and allows others to grow as they will. The effect, viewed from the western gallery, is of order and wildness held in a kind of tension, each needing the other to be legible. Remove the precise beds and the wild ones become mere chaos. Remove the wild ones and the precise beds become merely sterile. The garden requires both, and the relationship between them is the garden's real subject.

I have been thinking about form in swordsmanship — not the content of specific forms, but the architecture of *kata* itself, the question of why we preserve sequences of movement that no longer correspond to the conditions of actual combat. The standard answer is that *kata* trains the body to respond without deliberation. This is true but incomplete.
