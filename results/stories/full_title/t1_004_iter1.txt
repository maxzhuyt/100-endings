# The Divided Loop

## I.

The mop finds the seam where tile meets tile and drags a thin tongue of gray water along it. Third floor, east corridor, 9:47 PM. The seam has been grouted twice in the years his hands have known it and both times the grout was the wrong shade — a slightly warmer white against the original cold — so the line persists like a scar the building keeps showing him. He works the mop head into the join, wrings, lifts, moves forward. Behind him, the floor holds a brief, dark mirror of the overhead fluorescents and then dries to nothing.

His name is on a badge clipped to his shirt pocket, but nobody reads it and it faces inward half the time anyway. Since the stroke took his speech nineteen months ago, the badge has become the only external assertion that he is someone in particular. He does not think about this. He thinks about the mop, which needs a new head. He thinks about the supply closet on the second floor, where the replacement heads are stored behind a case of nitrile gloves that someone keeps restacking in front of them. He thinks about room 311's door, which has begun to stick at the latch plate and which he has WD-40'd three times this month and which will need the latch plate itself re-shimmed, a job he can do with a screwdriver and a folded square of cardboard but which he has not yet done because the patient in 311 sleeps lightly and wakes afraid.

The building is this. Not a floorplan. Not a name — Mercy House, the sign out front says, green letters on cream board, one mounting bolt rusted to a permanent orange tear-streak down the H. The building is a sequence of tasks that refresh themselves nightly, a loop that begins when he pushes through the service entrance at 8:30 PM and ends when he pushes back out at 5:00 AM, and in between there is the route, which he can walk without sight, without hearing, probably without waking. East corridor third floor. West corridor third floor. Stairwell C to second floor. East corridor second floor. The biohazard bin outside the supply room, which fills at a rate he can predict by the day of the week because certain patients receive certain treatments on certain days and those treatments produce certain waste. He lifts the red bag. He ties its throat. He fits a new bag into the bin, tucking the edges under the rim the way you tuck a fitted sheet, which is a comparison he has never made because he does not make comparisons. He works.

At 10:15 PM the shift changes. For eleven minutes the nurses' station on the second floor is unmanned. He knows this the way he knows the grout seam — by repetition, by the accumulation of nights into knowledge that has no language and needs none. During those eleven minutes the call lights sometimes blink in the empty hallway, small red pulses above doorframes, and sometimes a ventilator alarm sounds its three-note chime into a corridor where no one hears it but him. He is not a nurse. He cannot answer the call lights. He cannot silence the alarms. He can stand in the hallway and watch the red light blink, and he does, sometimes, mop in hand, the water cooling in the bucket beside him, until the night nurse arrives and the light goes out and the hallway is just a hallway again.

He has outlasted three administrators. The first was a woman who tried to learn his name and then stopped. The second was a man who called him "buddy" and signed the checks. The third is new, a woman who communicates by laminated memos taped to the break room wall, and he reads them because he can read — the stroke took his speech, not his literacy, not his mind, though he suspects the staff conflates these — and then he cleans the break room and the memos curl at their edges under the tape and yellow.

The patients are sounds. A cough in 307 that has changed in pitch over the past week, loosening, deepening, the way a hinge changes sound when it is about to give way. The low moan in 214 that begins at midnight exactly and lasts for an hour and then stops, not because anyone tends to it but because the body that produces it exhausts itself. The whir and click of the compression boots in 210, inflating and deflating around legs that no longer walk, and the fainter sound beneath them — breathing, shallow, measured, the automatic kind that a body does when the person inside it has retreated from the task of living but has not yet been released from the machinery of it.

He knows when someone is going to die. Not mystically. Practically. There are signs that have nothing to do with charts or vitals. The smell changes. Not the dramatic smell — not the one the nurses spray air freshener to mask — but a subtler shift in the room's ecology, something in the sheets, something in the air itself, a closeness, a density. The visitors change: they come more often or stop coming, and both are signals. The patient's relationship to the bed changes — they stop shifting, stop fighting the mattress, stop trying to find comfort because they have either found it or abandoned the concept. He has seen this dozens of times. Maybe more. He does not count. When it happens, he waits. After the body is taken, after the family leaves or doesn't, after the nurses strip the bed and wipe the rails, he enters alone with his mop and his bucket and his spray bottles and he cleans. He cleans the floor. He cleans the bathroom. He cleans the windowsill where someone always leaves something — a cup, a tissue, a prayer card — and he puts these things in a small plastic bag and leaves the bag at the nurses' station and nobody asks where it came from.

His hands. Look at his hands. They are large and the knuckles are swollen and the skin across them has the texture of something that has been wet and dried and wet and dried ten thousand times. There are cracks along the sides of his thumbnails that never heal. His right hand has a tremor — the stroke's souvenir — that manifests only when his hand is still, which means it manifests only when he stops working, which means it almost never manifests. When he grips the mop handle, the tremor disappears. When he wrings the mop head — both hands, a twist, the water sluicing brown and gray into the bucket — the hands are as steady as tools, because that is what they are. They are the part of him that still speaks fluently. They know the torque required to loosen the drain cap on the utility sink. They know the pressure required to strip old wax from the floor in the lobby, a job he performs every six weeks on Thursday nights. They know the exactly adequate tightness of a trash bag knot. They are his argument for his own existence. Every surface he touches becomes — for a few hours, until the building's entropy reclaims it — clean.

At 11:00 PM he is in the basement, loading the industrial washer. He sorts by protocol: linens from biohazard, lights from darks, though the hospice's linens are all the same pale blue that has been washed to near-white, a color that is not a color but the memory of one. The washer starts. It will run for forty-seven minutes. He sets no timer. His body is the timer. He goes upstairs and begins the west corridor of the first floor, and by the time he reaches the east corridor the washer will be ready and he will go back down and transfer the load, and this is how the night passes — not in hours but in tasks, each one leading to the next like vertebrae in a spine, and the spine holds the body upright, and the body moves through the building, and the building is cleaned, and the night ends, and he leaves, and the building begins to get dirty again, and he will return.

The fluorescent lights in the east corridor of the second floor have a cycle. Three of the twelve tubes are dying. They do not flicker randomly; they flicker on a period he has timed, roughly, by his footsteps — nine steps of full light, a stutter, a half-second of dark, then full light again. He has reported this twice via written notes left in the maintenance request box. The tubes have not been replaced. He works under them without looking up. He knows where the stutter will fall. He mops through the half-second of darkness the way a musician plays through a rest, and the corridor is a score he has memorized, and no one is the audience, and the performance is every night, and it is the same.

## II.

He saw it first from the far end of the third-floor east corridor on a Tuesday. It was already there. Already moving. A figure in the hallway that his body registered as wrong before his eyes confirmed it — wrong because it was in his corridor at his hour and yet produced no sound he recognized. No soft-soled nurse's shoe. No shuffle of a patient escaped from bed. A different quality of motion altogether: even, unvarying, a thing moving through space without the micro-hesitations that attend human movement in an institution at night — the pause at a doorway, the adjustment to darkness, the faint deference of a body sharing a narrow hallway with another body.

He stopped mopping. The mop head rested on the tile, pooling a small lake of gray water around its fringe, and he watched.

It was pushing a supply cart. The cart was standard — the same beige plastic, the same three shelves, the same squeaking left-rear caster that he could hear now, faintly, as the figure moved down the corridor toward room 308. The thing pushing it was not standard. It was humanoid — two arms, two legs, a head — but assembled with a proportionality that missed in some way he could not immediately identify. The torso was perhaps a centimeter too long relative to the legs. Or the shoulders were too symmetrical, lacking the slight imbalance of a human skeleton shaped by handedness, habit, injury, sleep. It wore scrubs. Light blue, the same shade as the linens. Its hands on the cart's push bar were steady, articulate, the fingers wrapped with mechanical precision around the bar's tubular steel, and even from forty feet away he could see that the grip did not shift. A human being pushing a cart adjusts constantly — a finger lifts, a palm re-seats — because a human grip is a living negotiation between intention and fatigue. This grip was a decision made once.

It entered 308. He heard the door open — the particular sound of 308, which had a slightly loose hinge pin that produced a faint tonal note, an E-flat, though he did not know this in musical terms, only that 308's door sounded different from 307's and 309's. The door closed. A pause. The door opened again and the figure emerged, cart first, and moved to 307, and entered, and emerged, and moved to 306.

He resumed mopping.

Over the next three nights, he assembled an understanding from fragments. The nurses talked freely near him, as they always had, because a mute janitor is a piece of furniture — he had understood this for nineteen months and understood it without bitterness because furniture has the advantage of presence: it is always in the room.

"—donated, refurbished, some kind of tax thing—"

"—CareCompanion model, the ones they pulled from St. Luke's after the refit—"

"—better than nothing with staffing the way it is. You want to keep pulling doubles?"

"—still creeps me out. Did you see its hands?"

"—works fine. Logged vitals faster than Jennifer ever did."

"—Jennifer quit, so—"

"—exactly, so—"

He learned its schedule by observing it the way he observed everything: through the lens of his own route and the disruptions it introduced. The cart's squeaky caster left marks on freshly mopped tile — a faint rubber whisper he could see in the right angle of fluorescent light, a track that ran down the center of every corridor it traveled. He began mopping *after* it passed through a hallway, adjusting his sequence by fifteen minutes. This was neither accommodation nor kindness. This was labor efficiency. You do not mop a floor before a cart travels over it. You mop after. His route shifted. His body registered the shift as a minor recalibration, the kind he had made a hundred times for a hundred variables: a spill, a late delivery of linen, a death that required early room cleaning.

He watched it move. In the quality of his watching there was nothing he would have called curiosity. It was the assessment one body makes of another body operating in shared space. *Will this one stay?* Three orderlies had been hired and quit in the past year. Two night nurses had transferred. A physical therapist had walked out mid-shift in October, and he had mopped the therapy room that night the way he always did, neither more nor less carefully, because the room's floor was the room's floor regardless of who had abandoned it. *Will this one last? Will this one leave things I have to clean up? Will this one get in my way?*

It didn't get in his way. Their paths traced a kind of double helix through the building — two figures moving through the same corridors on slightly different arcs, one mopping, one tending, neither pausing when their paths nearly crossed. One night he was emptying the biohazard bin on the second floor and it came around the corner with its cart at a distance of five feet and they occupied the same stretch of hallway for exactly the time it took him to tie the bag and it to pass, and neither entity adjusted course or speed for the other. They shared the fluorescent light. They shared the three dying tubes and their nine-step flicker. They shared the corridor the way two currents share a river — the same water, the same channel, no mingling.

Its footfalls. He noticed them because his body noticed all sounds in the building, filing each one against the known catalog, flagging deviations. Its footfalls had no deviation. The same pressure, the same interval, the same faint composite-on-tile contact sound, step after step after step, down the full length of the third-floor east corridor without a single variation in tempo or weight. A human being walking that distance will — unconsciously, inevitably — adjust pace near a corner, shift weight approaching a door, slow fractionally when passing a room where a sound issues. This figure walked the way a metronome counts: each beat identical, each beat indifferent to the music it measures.

He noted this. He filed it. He mopped.

It had no face he could read, though it had a face — features molded from a composite material approximating skin tone, eyes that tracked and focused, a mouth that formed words when it spoke to the night nurses at the station, a voice he had heard once from a distance and found unremarkable, a mid-register human voice shaped with a care that almost obscured its origin in engineered vocal folds and patterned breath. But the gap was there. Not in any single feature. In the ensemble. A face is a system of micro-movements — a cheek twitches when the eye squints, the jaw shifts when the brow furrows, and these cascades happen simultaneously, linked by musculature and nerve and the history of a face's use, the decades of expressions that carve their pathways. This face moved feature by feature, sequentially, a quarter-second lag between the eyes adjusting and the mouth following, which meant that for a quarter of a second, in every expression, the face disagreed with itself.

He saw this and did not think *uncanny valley* because he did not have the phrase. He thought nothing about it in words. His impression, if it could have been rendered into language, was simpler and more specific: *It looks like a new hire still learning the building.* Not yet fluent. Not yet integrated. Still operating from the manual rather than from the body's accumulated memory. He had seen that look on every new orderly, every new nurse, every new administrator — the slight awkwardness of a human being who has not yet worn a groove into a place. The android had that look. It would have it forever.

Two caretakers. One of them mopped the building. One of them tended the dying. Both followed routes. Both repeated. Both moved under the same fluorescent light that illuminated everything and revealed nothing, that cast no shadows because it came from everywhere, that flattened the hallways into planes of even, institutional brightness in which nothing could hide and nothing stood out and the only way to see clearly was to already know what you were looking at.

He already knew what he was looking at.

He was looking at the new coworker. He would see how long it lasted.
