# The Last Watch of the Mara Celeste

---

The way Wen took her tea was with the bag left in. I know that's not what you're asking me about. I know you want the coordinates, the sea state, the last radio transmission and who said what into the receiver and at what hour. I'll get to that. But I need to start where I can start, and where I can start is the tea.

She took it with the bag left in, which is a thing that bothered me for the first two years and then stopped bothering me and then became the way tea was supposed to be. Wen said her grandfather did it that way. She said a lot of things were her grandfather's way. The way you check the fuel filters. The way you coil a line so it won't kink when it runs. The way you never, ever let another man's bank tell you what your boat is worth.

I was the cook on the *Mara Celeste* for six years. My name doesn't matter to you and it didn't matter much to the maritime board and it won't matter to the insurance people when they get to the bottom of whatever it is they're looking for at the bottom of all this. I was the cook. I made food and I brought it to people and I cleaned up after and I did it again. That is the whole of what I was. Wen was the captain and the owner and the closest thing to a remaining heir of Amos Celeste, who built the boat in 1974 in a yard outside Xiamen with money he earned running freight through the Strait when running freight through the Strait could still make a Chinese man wealthy, before the companies swallowed everything and the men with the money became men with signatures on paper and nothing else.

The galley had a cabinet above the stove that wouldn't latch right. You had to press the lower left corner and then push up and then slide the latch, and if you did it in the wrong order it would swing open going through any kind of sea at all. In that cabinet I kept the star anise and the white pepper and the dried shrimp, because those were the things I reached for most and I am not tall. Six years of pressing that corner and pushing up and sliding the latch. My thumb wore a smooth place on the wood. I think about that smooth place. You wouldn't think that's the kind of thing that would stay with a person, but I need you to understand that when I say the *Mara Celeste*, I don't mean a sixty-eight-foot steel-hulled cargo vessel with a gross tonnage of whatever your records say. I mean the smooth place on the cabinet where my thumb went.

I mean Wen's hands on the wheel, which were square and brown and nicked all over the knuckles from engine work. I mean the way the hull sounded at night when we were making way through calm water, which was a low hum you felt through the soles of your feet before you heard it in your ears. I mean the particular list to port she always carried, two and a half degrees, which Wen's grandfather had apparently tolerated and Wen tolerated and which I had compensated for in the way I stacked everything in the galley, so that my whole kitchen was built on the assumption of those two and a half degrees and would've looked wrong on level ground.

I want to say that I know what you need to hear, and I am going to tell you. But the way I know how to tell a thing is to start with what I know, and what I know is a kitchen.

---

The letters I found by accident. This would have been—I'm going to say eight months before. Maybe nine. We were in Kaohsiung and Wen had gone ashore for diesel and I needed the can opener, which had migrated the way things migrate on boats, and I pulled open the drawer beneath the chart table because it was a wide flat drawer where things ended up.

There were no charts in it. There were envelopes. I don't know how many. Enough to fill a wide flat drawer. White envelopes and brown envelopes and a few of the kind with the little window where you can see the name and address printed on the letter inside, so there's no question who they're coming for. Red printing on some of them. FINAL NOTICE in English and characters I didn't stop to read beneath that. Some were opened and some were not, and the unopened ones were the more recent ones, and I understood from that single detail more than Wen had ever intended me to understand.

I closed the drawer. I found the can opener in a mug by the stove, where I should have looked first.

I didn't ask her about it. That was not our way. Our way was that I cooked and she captained and we played cards in the morning and if something needed saying it arrived in its own time or it didn't arrive at all. But I noticed things after that. I noticed how she held the mail when the port agent brought it, like it was something that might cut her. I noticed the jaw. You could see the whole history of what they were doing to her in the jaw—the way it set and held when she read certain pages, the way the muscle just below her ear would flex and flex.

I noticed she stopped calling the shipping brokers for return cargo. She'd wait for them to call her. And when they didn't call, she'd take whatever was offered at whatever rate, and I watched the fuel calculations get tighter and the provisions budget get shorter and I adjusted without being asked. You can make rice stretch. You can do a lot with the bones of a fish if you have time and ginger and don't mind the work.

I noticed the photograph in the wheelhouse. It had always been there—old Amos Celeste standing on the deck of the *Mara Celeste* the day she was launched, 1974, skinny and young and proud in that way that embarrasses descendants because no one feels that way about anything anymore. Wen had always kept it above the VHF set, just a snapshot in a brass frame gone green. But I noticed she'd started touching the frame when she entered the wheelhouse. Not looking at the photograph. Just touching the frame the way you'd touch a wall to steady yourself in a corridor. Like it was a fixed point.

I learned the rest in pieces. The grandfather died in 2005. Wen's mother had no interest in the boat and less in the sea and signed her share to Wen along with, it turned out, a lien she either didn't understand or didn't care to explain. The bank in Taipei held paper on the *Mara Celeste* going back a decade. Wen had been making payments. Then she'd been making partial payments. Then she'd been making promises. The promises had a certain shape—just one more run, just one more contract—and the shape was getting thinner.

I know about the repo men in Bermuda because Lin, our engineer, told me. He'd heard it on the radio or from another crew in Colón—I don't remember which. He said there were men waiting in Hamilton with papers to seize the vessel. He said it like he was telling me the weather. Lin was that kind of person. He said, "When we get to Bermuda, they're going to take the boat."

I said, "Does she know?"

He looked at me for a long time. "She's the one they told," he said.

So that was the shape of things when the typhoon came. I need you to understand the shape of things. The newspapers said she sailed into a typhoon. The maritime board said she failed to divert to a safe port. The insurance company said whatever insurance companies say, which is whatever saves them from writing a check. But what I'm telling you is that the storm was in front of us and Bermuda was on the other side of it and Bermuda was the place where men in clean shirts were going to take Amos Celeste's boat away from his granddaughter and sell it, probably for scrap, probably for a number that wouldn't cover what was owed, and the bank would write it off and everyone would go home and there would be no more *Mara Celeste* and no more photograph in the wheelhouse and no more anything.

I'm not saying she sailed into the storm on purpose. I'm saying that when the weather routing service said divert south, the place to the south was Bermuda. And when they said hold position and wait, holding position cost fuel we didn't have. And when they said run north, the north was open Atlantic in November and we were loaded heavy with container freight for Hamilton and the *Mara Celeste* was not a young ship and had never been a fast one.

I'm saying that every safe option led to the same place, and the place was loss.

So we sailed east, into the throat of it, because east was the only direction that still belonged to her.

---

The first thing the storm took from me was the broth.

I had a pot of stock going—pork bones and the dried shrimp and some cabbage ends I was stretching into something for the crew. The *Mara Celeste* had a gimbaled stove, which is a stove that swings to stay level when the ship rolls, and for six years that stove had stayed level through weather that would empty a bathtub. But there is a limit to what a gimbal can compensate for, and the typhoon found that limit on the first night.

The pot went. Not a dramatic crash—that came later, with other things. The pot simply tilted past the rail and the broth went across the stove and onto the floor in a long spreading sheet that moved with the next roll and came back the other way and I stood there watching six hours of simmering slide back and forth across my galley like it was looking for someplace to settle.

I cleaned it up. The ship rolled and I cleaned it on my hands and knees with a towel braced between the stove and the bulkhead, and the water came back through the towel and I wrung it into the sink and did it again. That is what you do. You do the thing you know how to do, even when doing it has become the same as not doing it.

The second thing the storm took was the flame. The propane stove needed a steady flow and something in the supply line—a valve or a fitting, I never found out which—began to stutter with the ship's motion. The flame would go and I'd relight it and it would hold for ten minutes or twenty and then die again. By the morning of the second day I couldn't keep it lit long enough to boil water.

I stood in my galley and I could not boil water. I am asking you to understand what that means for someone like me. Not the practical consequences, which were bad enough—a crew that needed hot food in bad weather not getting it. I mean what it means for a person whose one purpose on a ship is to transform raw things into nourishment and who suddenly cannot perform the most basic act of that transformation. I was not a sailor. I could not help with the pumps or the lines or the navigation. I was a cook who could not cook, and the storm had made me that.

The jars went next, one by one. The star anise. A jar of fermented bean curd I'd bought in Kaohsiung. Something hit the hull—a wave, a piece of debris, I never knew—and the impact traveled through the steel and up through the shelving brackets and the cabinet with the bad latch sprang open, which of course it did, and the jars came down. Not all at once. That first impact sent two of them to the floor. Then the ship rolled and a third came out. Then a lull, a pause, the kind of silence that is just the storm inhaling, and then a wave that must have been very large because the whole galley shifted and the rest came down and I stood in broken glass and fermented bean curd and star anise and the smell was enormous—sweet and sharp and fermented all at once—and in that smell was every meal I had ever cooked aboard the *Mara Celeste* and I covered my face with my hands because the smell was worse than the glass.

I will tell you about the three days but I cannot tell them to you as three days because that is not how I lived them. I lived them as cycles. There was the part where I tried to make the stove work. There was the part where I gave up and assembled something cold—crackers and tinned fish and the peanuts I kept in a sealed bucket. There was the part where I walked the food to whoever I could find—Lin in the engine room, soaked to the chest and swearing at the bilge pump in Hokkien; Tomas, the deckhand, lashing things that had come loose and then lashing them again when the lashings failed. There was the part where I tried to sleep and could not because the angle of the ship was wrong—not the rolling, which you can sleep through if you've done enough sea time, but a new and deeply incorrect angle, a lean that was not the *Mara Celeste*'s familiar two and a half degrees but something greater, something that meant water was in a place water should not be.

There was the part where I lay in my bunk and listened to the hull.

I am not an engineer. I don't know the language for what a steel hull does in a typhoon. But I can tell you what it sounded like from inside, which is that the ship was speaking. Low, sustained tones, almost musical, the kind of sound you feel in your molars before you recognize it as sound. And sharper sounds above that—pops and cracks, rivets or welds or bolted plates adjusting under stresses they'd been holding for fifty years. And beneath it the water, always the water, not the waves striking the hull, which was a separate and more violent conversation, but the water *inside*—in the bilge, in the lower compartments—sloshing with a patience that I found worse than the storm itself, because the storm was attacking and would eventually pass, but the water inside was accumulating. It was not going to pass. It was going to win.

By the second day I could not walk the corridor without bracing against both walls. By the second night I could not tell whether it was night. The portholes showed the same thing at every hour—gray water, black water, white spray, the absence of horizon. Time became a thing I measured by how many times I tried and failed to feed people. That's five meals I couldn't cook. That's how long we've been in it. That's all I know.

---

On what I believe was the morning of the third day, I made tea.

The stove wouldn't hold a flame but I had a camp burner—a small butane cartridge with a metal frame, the kind you take on a hiking trip, barely enough to heat a single pot. I'd been saving it. I don't mean saving it the way you save a tool for when you need it. I mean saving it the way you save a thing that represents your last ability to perform the act that defines you. While the camp burner still had fuel, I could still boil water. I could still be the cook.

I filled the kettle from the fresh water tank—the tank was low, I noticed, but I did not investigate why—and I set it on the camp burner and I lit the flame and I stood over it with both hands on the counter and my feet braced wide and I watched the water heat. The ship groaned beneath me. Something structural, something deep in the keel or the frames, let out a sound like a man turning over in sleep, and the kettle trembled on the burner, and I held it there with one hand and shielded the flame with the other and I waited.

The water boiled. I set the bag in the mug—her mug, the enamel one with the blue rim, chipped on the left side—and I poured the water and I let it steep and I did not remove the bag because that was how Wen took it.

I carried it up. This was a journey I had made perhaps two thousand times—out of the galley, down the corridor, up the companionway stairs, along the upper passage, into the wheelhouse. I could do it in the dark. I had done it in the dark. But on this morning the corridor was ankle-deep in water that had come from somewhere I didn't want to think about, and the companionway stairs were slick with spray and oil, and I climbed them one-handed, the mug in my left hand held against my chest, my right hand on the rail, and the ship told me with every step that the angle was worse, that the lean had deepened overnight.

The wheelhouse was gray. Gray light through the windows, gray water beyond, gray sky that was indistinguishable from the sea. Wen was at the helm. She had been at the helm for a period of time I could not accurately reckon—hours certainly, possibly since the previous afternoon. She was in her weather gear, her hair plastered to her skull, her hands on the wheel, and she was watching something ahead and above, something in the sea or the sky that I couldn't see from where I stood.

I said, "Tea."
