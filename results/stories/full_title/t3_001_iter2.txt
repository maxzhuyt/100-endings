# The Infinite Playoff

---

## I.

My name is Lyle Keetch, and I have spent the better part of my adult life in the company of numbers that belong to other men.

This is not a complaint. I want to be clear about that. I have a master's degree in applied mathematics from the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee, or rather I have the coursework and the residency hours for a master's degree; the thesis — "Bayesian Approaches to Small-Sample Run Expectancy in Short-Season Leagues" — was accepted at the committee level but never formally defended, owing to a scheduling conflict with my adviser's sabbatical that no one, including me, ever troubled to resolve. It is an undefended thesis. I have always liked that phrase. It sits in a filing cabinet in Milwaukee like a city with no walls.

For a period of five months in 2004, I held a consultancy with the Helena Brewers, the Pioneer League affiliate of Milwaukee, analyzing pitch-sequence tendencies for opposing hitters. The engagement was not renewed. I was told the organization was "moving in a different analytical direction," which is what you say to a man when the direction you are moving is away from him. I have no grievance with the Brewers. Helena is a fine city. The press box at Kindrick Legion Field had a particular quality of light in August, the sun cutting low across the valley and turning the scoreboard amber, that I have never encountered anywhere else and do not expect to encounter again.

After Helena, I did contract statistical work. Reconciliation, mostly — auditing minor league records, cross-referencing box scores across multiple reporting systems, correcting the small errors that accumulate in any bureaucratic archive. The teams I worked for read like a casualty list of the minors' long contraction: the Pulaski Blue Jays, the Oneonta Tigers, the Jamestown Jammers, the Vermont Expos. Franchises folded into other franchises the way a losing bet folds into the body — absorbed, metabolized, converted into a generalized heaviness you carry without being able to name its source. I was not employed by these teams in any salaried sense. I invoiced them. They paid me, sometimes promptly, sometimes on a schedule that suggested the invoice had been misfiled or used to level a desk. The work was seasonal. The compensation was marginal. But the work was real, and it was mine, and I did it well.

My apartment, since you are forming a picture and I would rather supply it than have you invent one, is a one-bedroom on the second floor of a house in Johnson City, Tennessee, a city that has had professional baseball since 1911 and currently has it in the form of a franchise that has changed names and affiliations so many times the locals simply call them "the team." The apartment is arranged around the work. The desk is by the window. The desk lamp has a green glass shade that cost twenty-two dollars at a Goodwill in Kingsport and is, I believe, the single most aesthetically considered object I have ever purchased. Around the desk, on the walls, taped and re-taped and taped over, are printouts — box scores, game logs, reconciliation worksheets, statistical summaries I have generated for projects both commissioned and uncommissioned. They layer. When I correct a record, I do not always remove the old printout; I tape the new one over it, so that on the walls of my apartment there are strata, geological layers of corrected data, earlier versions buried beneath later ones but still, if you peeled carefully, recoverable.

I mention the layering because it seems relevant now, though at the time it was simply how I worked.

The uncommissioned projects — my "active projects," as I called them, always with the slight self-conscious irony of a man who knows no one is waiting for his results — were statistical reconciliations I undertook on my own initiative, without client or deadline. Records I suspected were flawed. Games whose box scores didn't balance. Plate appearances that resolved differently depending on which source you consulted. These small fractures in the historical record — invisible to anyone who wasn't looking, meaningless to anyone who was — constituted, for me, a kind of moral crisis in miniature. A box score that doesn't add up is a lie about what happened. Someone stood in a batter's box and the result of his standing there was recorded incorrectly and he went home and lived the rest of his life and the record persisted, wrong, like a name misspelled on a headstone. I wanted to correct these. I have always wanted to correct these. Not out of brilliance — I am not brilliant and have never claimed to be — but out of an inability to leave a data set alone. Some men cannot leave a scab unworked. They do not pick at it because they think they can heal it. They pick at it because the boundary between the wound and the intact skin is a border their fingers cannot stop finding.

This is who I was when the committee contacted me. This is who I have always been. A man whose competence marginally exceeds his station, whose attention to the historical record is both his vocation and his compulsion, who lives alone in a small apartment in the foothills of the Appalachian Mountains surrounded by the statistics of games that no longer matter, if they ever did, played by men who have since moved on to selling insurance or coaching high school or standing in their own kitchens at midnight wondering what happened to the feeling they had when they were nineteen and the ball came off the bat and the world briefly organized itself around a single, legible event.

You will have to trust me. That is the nature of testimony. I can provide the evidence, but you will have to trust the man who gathered it.

## II.

I should tell you what a box score is, not because you don't know — you likely know — but because you probably don't know the way I know, and the difference matters.

A box score is the complete statistical record of a baseball game. It is not a narrative. It does not tell you how the game *felt* — who was nervous, who was triumphant, whether the crowd was thin and bitter in the early-April cold or thick and loud in the long light of July. It tells you what happened, tabulated. Every plate appearance resolving into a finite set of outcomes: single, double, triple, home run, walk, strikeout, hit-by-pitch, sacrifice, fielder's choice, error, the various subcategories of each. Every pitch to every batter in every inning, recorded or reconstructable. The box score is a closed system. Unlike a life, it adds up. The runs in the linescore equal the runs in the individual columns. The outs recorded by the pitchers equal the outs required to end the innings pitched. The at-bats plus walks plus hit-by-pitches plus sacrifices equal the total plate appearances. If they don't, something is wrong — not existentially wrong, not philosophically wrong, but *wrong*, the way a misbalanced equation is wrong: locatably, correctably, with the error discoverable by anyone willing to work through the figures.

I have spent evenings — I will not say how many — doing nothing but reconciling a single minor league game log across three sources, looking for the discrepancy that prevents the at-bats from summing correctly. I want to be truthful about what this feels like. It is difficult to describe without sounding pathological, and I am aware that the line between dedication and pathology is, in my case, a subject that others have opinions about. But when the numbers resolve — when you find the missing plate appearance, the sacrifice fly coded as a fielder's choice, the walk attributed to the wrong pitcher — there is a sensation that I can only call *peace*. Not happiness. Peace. The sensation that the world, for a brief and verifiable moment, is accountable. That every event has been recorded, and every record can be checked, and the system, while not moral, is at least *coherent*.

I believe this is what other people get from prayer. I have never said that aloud before. I say it now because I have come to feel that the time for protecting how I sound has passed.

At the league level — I did periodic contract work for the Appalachian League office, small tasks, the work distributed to me by a coordinator named Phyllis Brogan who addressed me in emails the way you address a freelancer whose invoices you process but whose face you have never learned — my colleagues regarded me with what I would characterize as polite disengagement. I was the man who came in to fix the data. Not the data that generated revenue or influenced player development. The old data. The retroactive corrections. The auditing of records that had already been filed, certified, forgotten. There was an administrative assistant named Dale who once asked me, while I was photocopying scoresheets in the staff room, whether I had "ever thought about coaching." The question was delivered kindly. It was also the kindest possible way of saying: *Why are you doing this?*

I told Dale that I found the work satisfying. He nodded in the way people nod when they have received an answer they do not intend to examine.

I often brought the reconciliation work home. You understand — it was not that the work required my apartment. It was that the apartment required the work. Evenings without a data set to reconcile were evenings in which the apartment became merely a room, and I became merely a man in it, and the silence was not the productive silence of concentration but the other kind, the kind that has texture and weight and that settles over a person like something geological.

I want to tell you about one specific evening, because I believe it is where this begins, or where I now believe it begins — the distinction, I have learned, is not always available to me.

It was October 2016. I was reconciling game logs from the Appalachian League season that had ended six weeks prior. Routine work. I was cross-referencing the official records filed by the home team's scorekeeper against the road team's scorer and, where available, the league's real-time data entry, which in the Appalachian League that year was handled by interns using a system that was, to put it charitably, not always manned at full attention. Discrepancies between these sources were common. Most were clerical: a strikeout swinging coded as a strikeout looking, a single to center recorded as a single to left. Minor. Correctible. Matters of no consequence to anyone but me.

On this particular evening, working through a late-June game between Bluefield and Burlington, I found a plate appearance in the bottom of the fifth inning — the number-eight hitter, a seventeen-year-old Dominican outfielder whose name is in my files and which I will not include here — that appeared in the home scorer's book as a strikeout and in the road scorer's book as a walk. Not a mere coding difference. A categorical contradiction. Strikeout or walk. Out or on base. These are not different descriptions of the same event. They are descriptions of different events.

I noted it. I checked the third source, the league data-entry record. That source listed the plate appearance as — and I wrote this down, it is in my notes — "K," a strikeout. So two of three sources agreed. I entered the correction. Strikeout. I moved on.

Except I want to be precise here, because precision is the only thing I have left to offer and I have come to distrust even that.

I *believe* I corrected it to a strikeout. Two of three sources supported the strikeout. The standard reconciliation protocol — which is not formally codified but which I have followed consistently for years, or believe I have followed consistently for years — resolves contradictions in favor of the majority source unless there is specific evidence to override. Two of three said strikeout. I would have corrected it to a strikeout.

But sitting here now, writing this down, I cannot tell you with certainty which version I corrected *to*. I did not note my resolution in the margin. I normally note my resolutions in the margin. I did not, in this case. I remember the discrepancy. I remember finding it. I do not remember which way I resolved it.

This is a minor matter. One plate appearance in one game in the Appalachian League in June 2016, involving a teenage outfielder who batted .187 for the season and was released in the spring of the following year. It is a matter of absolute inconsequence. I mention it only in the interest of completeness, and I move on from it now.

## III.

In February 2018, I was contacted by the Office of Statistical Integrity and Competitive Standards, Minor League Baseball—Eastern Division, a body whose full name I render here in full because its self-serious bureaucratic grandeur deserves to be preserved as precisely as the sentence in which it arrived: printed on letterhead that featured a logo I had never seen before, addressed to me by my full name (Lyle Edward Keetch, the middle name a detail I do not make generally available), and signed by three people, none of whose names I recognized, all bearing the title "Associate Director."

The letter — and there was a letter, physical, mailed, which felt archaic even then and which I regarded, I will admit, with a kind of reverence — invited me to undertake a paid investigation into "statistical anomalies consistent with undisclosed external influences on competitive outcomes" in a recent series of minor league games. The language was bureaucratic and cautious. What it meant, stripped of its institutional euphemism, was point-shaving. Someone, the committee believed, was manipulating the outcomes of minor league baseball games.

The franchise in question was the Tri-City ValleyCats, a New York–Penn League short-season affiliate that played its games at Joseph L. Bruno Stadium in Troy, New York. I had never personally worked with the ValleyCats' organization, but I knew the New York–Penn League's statistical infrastructure well — I had done reconciliation work for three of its other franchises — and I knew the particular fragilities of short-season record-keeping: the turnover of scorekeepers, the compressed schedules, the organizational inattention that settles over a franchise whose players will spend, on average, less than one full season in its uniform.

The committee enclosed a dossier.

I want to describe this carefully, because the dossier was, for a time, the most important object in my life, and because a man should be permitted to describe the thing that destroyed him with the same attentiveness he would give to a thing that saved him.

It was forty-seven pages. Printed, bound with a single brass fastener, the cover page bearing only the committee's name, a case number (OSI-2017-0043), and the word CONFIDENTIAL in a font that suggested the word was performing its own significance. Inside, it contained a list of fourteen games from the 2017 New York–Penn League season flagged as statistically anomalous. For each game, the dossier provided a summary of the anomaly: improbable scoring patterns, run distributions that deviated significantly from expected models, late-inning outcomes that correlated with known betting-line movements. The analysis was sophisticated. Whoever had compiled it understood run-expectancy matrices, understood leverage indices, understood the statistical signatures that distinguish organic improbability from manufactured improbability. The methodology was careful, rigorous, and — I noted this at the time, with a flicker of professional pleasure that now makes me ill — elegant.

It used Bayesian conditional probability to weight the anomalies. It employed a small-sample correction factor I recognized. It organized the games not chronologically but by deviation magnitude, listing them in descending order of statistical improbability so that the most suspicious games appeared first, generating a momentum of persuasion that was structural rather than rhetorical.

I recognized the correction factor because I had developed it. I had described it in my undefended thesis. It was not published. It was not in use by anyone. It was, as far as I knew, mine alone — an idiosyncratic adjustment for the run-environment instability of short-season leagues, designed to prevent small-sample variance from triggering false positives in anomaly detection. It was the kind of methodological quirk that its inventor considers a fingerprint and the world considers trivial, because the world does not live inside the architecture of its own analytical frameworks the way I do.

I noticed this. I want to be clear: I noticed it. I read the dossier's methodology and I felt the flicker of recognition you feel when you hear your own speech patterns in another person's mouth. But I did not pursue the recognition. I set it aside. I told myself that Bayesian methods are not uncommon, that small-sample corrections are a known problem with known solutions, that the application of familiar tools by an unfamiliar hand does not require explanation. This was plausible. It is still plausible. I ask you to consider whether plausibility, when applied by a sufficiently motivated mind, is distinguishable from fabrication.

The committee offered a fee. The fee was four thousand dollars, payable upon delivery of a final report. It was more money than I had earned in the preceding four months combined. I accepted the same day.
