# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

*Edited, with annotations and commentary, by the winner of the Forty-Second All-Japan Invitational Martial Arts Tournament (Kendo Division, Open Class). Published by arrangement with the Enryaku-ji Monastic Archives and the estate of Nakamura Hideo, 1922–2007.*

---

## Editor's Preface

I have delayed this publication for reasons I will not dramatize. The letters speak, or rather circle the act of speaking, well enough on their own. My role here is custodial: to provide dates where Hideo omitted them, to gloss references that may be unfamiliar to readers outside the traditions of classical swordsmanship, and to arrange in proper sequence what arrived over the course of four decades in no particular order of argument, though in faithful order of time.

The facts are simple and have been recounted elsewhere, usually incorrectly. In October of 1967, at the Forty-Second All-Japan Invitational, Nakamura Hideo — then twenty-one years of age, undefeated in regional and national competition since the age of sixteen, widely regarded as the most gifted kendōka of his generation and perhaps several generations prior — faced me in the final bout. I was twenty-three. I was ranked second. I expected to lose. Every informed observer expected me to lose. What happened instead has been discussed at sufficient length in other venues that I will not rehearse it here, except to note that the public record is both voluminous and useless, as public records of private acts tend to be. The relevant details are these: Hideo forfeited. I was awarded the championship. Hideo entered monastic life at Enryaku-ji within the week. Seven months later, I received the first of thirty-seven letters, hand-brushed on monastery stationery, addressed to me by name but with no return address beyond the temple's.

I never replied. This is a fact I state rather than defend.

Why publish now, when both of us are — as of this writing — still alive, though only in the technical sense the word retains past the age of eighty? I have a debt to the record. What began in a tournament hall in Kyoto in 1967 was never completed there. A bout that ends in forfeit is not a bout that ends. The judges' decision stands. The record stands. But the match — the encounter between two particular bodies, two particular minds, in one unrepeatable moment — remains open. I believed for many years that my silence was a form of dignity. I have come to suspect it was a form of cowardice, and that the two are less distinguishable than I once needed them to be.

These letters were written to me. I have chosen to share them with you, the reader. I have added annotations where I judged them necessary. I have not altered a single character of Hideo's text. I offer this collection in the same spirit in which one might return a sword to the house of the man who forged it: with respect, with formality, and with the full knowledge that the act of returning it is not the same as the act of laying it down.

My hands, I notice, are not steady as I write this. I choose to attribute this to age.

## The Forty-Second All-Japan Invitational: A Reconstruction from Public Sources

Before the letters themselves, I feel it is my obligation to reconstruct, as faithfully as the contradictory record allows, the event that occasioned them. I draw here on contemporaneous press accounts, published interviews with spectators and officials, and the small library of essays, articles, and one deeply unreliable doctoral dissertation that has accumulated around the incident in the decades since. I draw also, reluctantly, on my own memory, which I trust least of all these sources.

The 1967 All-Japan Invitational was held at the Butokuden in Kyoto — the old martial arts hall near Heian Shrine, with its dark wood and cathedral hush. The finals were scheduled for the afternoon of October 14th. The weather was clear. I remember this because I had spent the morning walking the grounds of the shrine in my competition *hakama*, attempting to convince my body that what was about to happen to it was not a form of dying. Hideo, I was later told by a mutual acquaintance, had spent the morning in seated meditation in his hotel room. He had eaten a single rice ball. He had spoken to no one.

By 1967, Nakamura Hideo was already something more than a competitor. He was a problem the kendō world had been unable to solve. He had debuted at sixteen in the Osaka Prefectural Championships, where he defeated three opponents ranked *godan* or above and was reprimanded afterward by tournament officials who felt his performance constituted a discourtesy to his seniors. His technique was not unorthodox; it was, by every technical measure, flawless — so correct, so classically precise, that opponents described facing him as fighting a textbook that had learned to breathe. And yet there was something else, something the technical vocabulary could not contain. Watanabe Sensei, who refereed several of Hideo's early bouts, once described it to a journalist as "the feeling you get when you realize the man across from you is not reading your movements but your *intentions,* and not your intentions of this moment but your intentions of three moments from now, and he is already bored by them." Hideo himself never spoke publicly about his abilities. He competed. He won. He bowed with the same precise forty-five-degree inclination whether he had won in ten seconds or ten minutes. He was, by every account, courteous to the point of opacity.

I will not pretend I was his equal. I was very good. I was, by the consensus of people whose opinions I respected, the second-best kendōka in Japan, which is to say I was the best kendōka in Japan who was also a human being operating within human limitations. I had a powerful *kote* strike, an excellent sense of distance, and what my own teacher once called, in a rare moment of praise, "a talent for making your opponent feel he has made a mistake, even when he hasn't." I trained with the single-minded desperation of a man who knows he is destined to stand second on every podium. I was familiar with my position. I had made a kind of peace with it. I walked into the Butokuden that afternoon prepared to lose beautifully — to give Hideo the final he deserved, the kind of bout that would be discussed for decades. I had choreographed my own defeat, not in the sense of planning to lose, but in the sense that I had already rehearsed the emotional architecture of noble failure so many times it had become a stance I could assume as automatically as *chūdan-no-kamae.*

The hall was full. Standing room had been gone for an hour before the bout. The press section was three rows deep. There is a photograph, often reproduced, taken from the balcony: the two of us at center court, facing each other, the dark wood of the floor gleaming between us like a frozen river. In the photograph I appear calm. I was calm. This was, I understood even then, the calm of a man who believes his story has already been written.

We assumed our positions. We tied our *men.* We drew our *shinai.* We bowed to the referees. We faced each other.

What happened next takes, in the telling, much longer than it took in the living. The official record, such as it is, states that the bout lasted between ten and fifteen seconds. No strikes were exchanged. What happened in those seconds has been described by witnesses with a degree of variation that I have long since stopped finding surprising. Memory, under the pressure of the incomprehensible, behaves like water under pressure: it finds cracks, fills them with invention, and then freezes into shapes it insists were always there.

Here is what the witnesses agree on: We assumed our opening stances. Hideo took *chūdan-no-kamae* — the middle guard, the most fundamental, the most classical. I mirrored him. For a span of time that lasted somewhere between four seconds (according to the head referee's post-event report) and "nearly a full minute" (according to a feature article published in *Kendo World* in 1985), neither of us moved. This is not, in itself, unusual. The opening of a kendō bout is often a period of stillness, a mutual reading. Two bodies in *kamae* are not inactive; they are conducting a conversation at a frequency below the visible, each testing the other's center, balance, intention. Spectators unfamiliar with the art sometimes perceive this stillness as hesitation. It is the opposite. It is the most loaded silence in combat — a silence full of potential strikes, each one imagined, tested, and discarded in the shared space between two minds.

Then Hideo moved.

He did not strike. He lowered his *shinai.* He stepped back. He performed a formal bow of concession — not the brief, perfunctory nod of a man acknowledging a point against him, but the deep, deliberate bow one makes to a teacher, or to a shrine, or to the dead. He straightened. He turned to the panel of judges. He spoke a single sentence. Then he walked off the court.

The sentence, as recorded by the chief judge and confirmed in his written report, was: "I withdraw from competition with full respect to my opponent and to the judges of this tournament." This is the official version. Two of the four corner judges later recalled the sentence differently. One remembered: "I ask that the bout be declared forfeit." The other remembered: "I cannot continue." A spectator in the third row, an elderly instructor from Hokkaido who gave a newspaper interview the following day, insisted that Hideo said nothing at all — that he simply placed his *shinai* on the floor, bowed to the judges, and left, and that the chief judge's account was a face-saving fabrication designed to impose grammar on what had no grammar.

I stood at center court. My *shinai* was still raised in *chūdan-no-kamae.* I remained in that position for what I am told was eight seconds but what I experienced as approximately the length of a human life. A referee approached me. He explained that the bout had been declared forfeit. He said that I was the champion of the Forty-Second All-Japan Invitational. He said other things that I have no memory of. Somewhere in the hall, someone began to clap, and then others joined, and the applause built and built like something structural coming apart, and I stood at center court and received the standing ovation in the manner of a man who has won a championship he did not earn, in a bout that did not happen, against an opponent who had looked at him — just once, in the instant before everything changed — with an expression the standing ovation was specifically designed to prevent anyone from having to name.

The applause sounded, I recall, like the noise a body makes falling down stairs. I do not mean this as metaphor. I mean that the rhythm was wrong — syncopated, lurching, as though the audience could not agree on what emotion they were expressing and so expressed all of them simultaneously, producing a sound that was not celebration and not mourning but something structurally identical to physical collapse.

In the years that followed, the Forfeit — it acquired the capital letter within months, and has never relinquished it — became one of those events that belongs less to the people who lived it than to the culture that metabolized it. The theories multiplied. Hideo had suffered a sudden spiritual crisis. Hideo had received news of a family death (his parents, I can confirm, were alive and healthy and as bewildered as everyone else). Hideo had seen a vision — an actual divine or mystical intervention — that convinced him combat was incompatible with the path to enlightenment. Hideo had thrown the match for gambling purposes (this theory was popular for about six months, then collapsed under the weight of its own absurdity; Hideo had no debts, no vices, and no material possessions beyond his training equipment and a modest collection of books). Hideo had been intimidated by the opponent's superior preparation. This last theory was advanced only once, by a journalist for a minor sports weekly, and I will not pretend it did not cause me a pain so specific and so clarifying that I have never fully recovered from it.

The theory I have never seen advanced, in four decades of reading every word published about that day, is this: that Hideo forfeited because he saw something. Not a vision. Not a spiritual truth. Something specific, technical, and small — a thing that, once perceived, could not be unperceived, and that transformed the bout from a contest he might win into a situation he could not inhabit.

I advance this theory now because the letters, I believe, advance it for me. But I also advance it because of what I saw in his eyes.

This is the detail I have never shared, in forty years of interviews declined and inquiries deflected. In the moment before Hideo lowered his *shinai,* in the stillness of the opening — that silence full of potential motion — our eyes met through the grilles of our *men.* Kendōka speak of *metsuke,* the quality of the gaze. You learn to look through your opponent, not at him — to soften the focus so that you perceive the total field rather than any single part. Hideo's *metsuke* was famous; opponents described the sensation of being seen by him as distinctly uncomfortable, as though his eyes were not observing the surface of your body but auditing the structure beneath it.

In that moment, his gaze changed. I felt it the way you feel a shift in air pressure — not with any single sense but with the animal totality of the body. His eyes, which had been performing the wide, soft focus of combat-sight, narrowed. Focused. Fixed on a point I could not identify but could feel, the way you feel someone staring at a scar you have forgotten you carry. And then his expression — visible only in fragments through the grid of the face guard, readable only to someone standing as close as I was, which was very close, because in that stillness we had drifted toward each other with the unconscious gravity of two bodies in the same orbit — his expression passed through several states in rapid succession. I have spent forty years trying to catalogue them. The closest I have come is this: he saw something. He understood it. He was stricken by his understanding. And then he felt, for me, something I have never been able to name, because the closest words are *pity* and *love,* and I have never been able to determine whether these are two things or one thing wearing different masks.

Then he bowed. And I became the champion of something I did not want to be the champion of, in a way that I could not refuse without dishonoring us both.

Hideo entered Enryaku-ji monastery five days later. He has not left it since.

Seven months after the tournament, I received a letter.

### Letter 1

*[Postmarked Otsu, Shiga Prefecture. No return address. Received by the editor on May 3, 1968. Hand-brushed in ink on plain washi paper, the calligraphy disciplined but slightly stiff, as though the hand had not held a brush in some time. — Ed.]*

I hope this letter finds you well, and that the intervening months have brought you health, continued success in training, and some measure of satisfaction in your achievement. I offer my congratulations, belatedly, on your championship. I am aware that the circumstances of your victory were not the circumstances you would have chosen, and I regret any difficulty my actions may have caused you. This is not an apology — I do not believe I acted wrongly, though I am no longer certain I understand what wrongly means — but it is an acknowledgment that my decision was not mine alone. A forfeit requires two people. I made the gesture. You received it. What it became after that was as much your burden as my choice, and I have thought about this more than you might expect.

I am writing from a place I will not name, though I suspect the postmark will name it for me. The days here are structured. We rise early. We sit. We chant. We work — I am assigned to the kitchen garden, which is a small plot of mostly radishes and a stubbornly unproductive cabbage row that I have come to regard with something approaching personal rivalry. My body, which spent its first twenty-one years learning a single, very specific language, is now learning to forget that language and speak another. The grammar of the hoe is not the grammar of the *shinai.* Some nights my arms rehearse cuts in my sleep. I wake with my hands in the shape of a grip I no longer hold.

I do not know if you will read this. I do not know if you should. I am writing because there is something I have not been able to say, and I have discovered that the act of not saying it requires, paradoxically, an audience. A thing unsaid in a room alone is merely a thought. A thing unsaid in the presence of the person it concerns is a *withholding,* and a withholding has a shape and weight that a thought does not.
