# The Thirty-Seven Letters of Monk Hideo, As Compiled by His Opponent

---

## Compiler's Preface

In the autumn of the thirty-first year of Shōwa, at the Seventeenth All-Japan Martial Arts Invitational held in the city of Kumamoto, a swordsman named Hideo Katsura forfeited the final match of the open kenjutsu division. He did so without explanation, without prior indication, and without any visible injury. He bowed to his opponent, laid his bokutō on the floor of the shiai-jō, and walked from the hall. Within the year he had entered the monastery at Eihei-ji, taken vows, and effectively disappeared from the martial world.

The forfeit was and remains the most discussed event in postwar kenjutsu. It has been the subject of two doctoral theses, a chapter in Yamaoka's *The Ethos of Renunciation in Modern Budō*, and countless speculative accounts in the popular press. None of these, to my knowledge, are accurate. The doctoral theses are particularly wrong.

I was the opponent.

My name is Takashi Ōnishi. I am seventy-nine years old. I am dying of a disease of the lungs whose name I find aesthetically displeasing and will not record here. The physicians at Keio University Hospital have told me I have between three and five months, which I appreciate for its precision even as I suspect they have rounded generously. I have been a practitioner and teacher of kenjutsu for sixty-one years. I held the title of kyōshi for twenty-eight of them. I was, evidently, the final-round opponent of a man who chose not to fight me, and I have carried the weight of that distinction for the greater portion of my adult life.

Over the course of forty years following his departure for Eihei-ji, Hideo Katsura wrote to me thirty-seven times. I responded to none of his letters. I kept all of them.

To explain why I kept them would require me to say things I do not intend to say in a preface, or perhaps anywhere. What I can say is this: Hideo died on the fourth of February last year, in the infirmary of a sub-temple of Eihei-ji, and a junior monk wrote to inform me, and the letter from the junior monk was the first piece of correspondence I have ever received from that monastery that I wished had not arrived. I sat in my study for two hours after reading it. Then I took the wooden box from the upper shelf in my closet and I reread all thirty-seven letters in sequence, which I had not done before, having always read them singly, on the day of their arrival, and then put them away. Reading them in sequence was a different experience. I do not wish to characterize it.

I have decided to publish them. I have arranged them in chronological order, which is the only order they can bear. I have added annotations where context requires. I have not altered any of the text.

I am aware that this publication will be of interest primarily to historians of kenjutsu and to those who have followed the various speculative accounts of the Kumamoto forfeit. I welcome the former audience and am indifferent to the latter.

A final note on editorial method. I have in several places provided contextual information about persons, places, or concepts that Hideo mentions. These annotations are factual in nature and are offered in the service of clarity. I have restricted myself to supplementary material that I judged necessary for a reader without specialized knowledge of monastic life, classical kenjutsu, or the circumstances of the tournament. Where I have overstepped this mandate, I trust the reader will extend the tolerance customarily granted to a dying man.

— Takashi Ōnishi
Tokyo, September 2996

## Letters 1–4

### Letter 1

*Received March 1957, approximately five months after the Kumamoto tournament. No envelope preserved. Written on temple stationery.*

Ōnishi-san,

The mountain received its first true snow two days ago. I say "true" because there was an earlier snowfall in late November that the older monks refused to acknowledge—a dusting, they said, unworthy of the name, an embarrassment to winter. The recent snow was apparently adequate. I was told this by a monk named Daitō who has been here forty years and who has, in that time, developed an elaborate private taxonomy of snowfall. He distinguishes nine varieties. I will not inflict them on you, but I confess I found the conversation more engaging than any I have had in months.

I write to you for no particular reason. Or rather: I write to you because I found myself thinking about whether you had experienced snow yet in Tokyo this year, and then I found myself reaching for paper, and it seemed dishonest to resist a motion that had already completed itself before I recognized it. I have been told that dishonesty of this kind—the suppression of an impulse after the body has already committed to it—is a form of spiritual vanity. My teacher here would say that the hand knows before the mind, and the mind's refusal is merely theater. I am not certain he is right, but I have decided to let the letter stand.

I hope you are well.

Hideo

---

### Letter 2

*Received July 1957. Envelope postmarked Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture.*

Ōnishi-san,

Summer here is an argument between the forest and the heat, and the forest is losing. We wake at half past three in the morning to sit zazen before the day becomes intolerable. The sitting itself is a complicated negotiation. My knees, which endured twenty years of training without meaningful objection, have developed strong opinions about stone floors. I have been told that the appropriate response to pain during zazen is to observe it without narrative—to experience it as sensation rather than story. This is exactly as difficult as it sounds. The knees insist on narrative. They are verbose and self-pitying. They compose elaborate elegies about the life they might have led.

I spend the afternoons in the garden, which is small and formal and requires more maintenance than seems philosophically consistent with a tradition that preaches non-attachment. A rock garden is, I have come to believe, a kind of joke—an exercise in pretending that the most meticulously arranged surface in the world is a portrait of spontaneous nature. I say this with great affection. I have grown fond of the rocks.

We had a visitor last week—a young man from Osaka who is considering ordination. He stood in the courtyard for some time before entering, performing a series of movements that I recognized as preparatory stretches from a martial tradition, though not one I could specifically identify. He had good instincts. Clean structure. But his weight favored his right side in a way he clearly could not feel, and when he turned, there was a momentary collapse in his left hip that would have been—

The plums this year are exceptionally good. The head cook makes a pickled preparation that I suspect violates several precepts regarding attachment to sensory pleasure, but no one complains.

Hideo

---

### Letter 3

*Received November 1957.*

Ōnishi-san,

Autumn. The ginkgo beside the meditation hall has turned the color of a fire that has given up on being dangerous and decided instead to be beautiful. I sat beneath it this morning for twenty minutes, which is too long to sit beneath a ginkgo tree and not long enough to justify calling it meditation, so I occupied a useless middle ground between leisure and discipline, which is, I suspect, where I have always lived.

I have been reading. The monastery has a library that someone assembled with great care and then abandoned to decades of indifferent stewardship—shelves of texts on ethics, aesthetics, several volumes of Chinese poetry, and an inexplicable collection of German philosophy in translation that no one claims to have ordered. I found a treatise by a monk of the Kamakura period on the obligations of a man who possesses knowledge that, if shared, would cause suffering, and if withheld, would allow suffering of a different kind. He presents the dilemma as insoluble and then, in the final passage, suggests that the obligation is not to resolve it but to *remain in the presence of its irreducibility*—to sit with the knowledge as one sits with a kōan, not reaching for the answer but allowing the question to do its work on the body.

I do not know if this is wisdom or merely a dignified form of paralysis. The distinction may not matter.

Hideo

---

### Letter 4

*Received February 1958.*

Ōnishi-san,

A brief letter. We are in the period of intensive winter sitting, and my days are structured such that even writing feels like a small rebellion. I do not mind the rebellion. I mind the brevity it imposes.

I wanted to tell you something. I have been thinking about the ethical life of a swordsman—not a monk, not a philosopher, but specifically a swordsman, a man whose primary expertise is the reading of another body's capacity for harm and vulnerability. It occurs to me that we speak often about the swordsman's obligation to restrain his *capacity for violence*, but we speak very little about his obligation to restrain his *capacity for seeing*. These are not the same thing. A man may set down his sword and still carry his eyes. The renunciation of the weapon is simple. The renunciation of the perception that renders the weapon effective—this is something else entirely. I do not know if it is possible. I do not know if it should be.

I am asking you this, I think, because you would understand the question. There are very few people alive who would.

Hideo

**Compiler's Note:** The temple Hideo mentions in his second letter as the site of the rock garden is the sub-temple of Rurigi-in, which is associated with the Eihei-ji monastic complex but situated approximately four hundred meters from the main hall. The garden was designed in the seventeenth century and is considered a minor example of the *karesansui* style. It is not typically accessible to visitors. I include this note for the benefit of readers unfamiliar with the layout of the monastery.^[1]

## Letters 5–9

### Letter 5

*Received September 1958.*

Ōnishi-san,

Your silence is a kind of answer. I do not say this to reproach you—please believe that. Your silence has a quality I recognize. It is the silence of a man who has heard the question and judged that a response would constitute a kind of admission. I respect this. I have practiced several versions of this silence myself.

I want to tell you about an exercise we perform here called *kinhin*, which is walking meditation. The instruction is to walk so slowly that each step becomes an event—to place the foot with the deliberateness of a man crossing a frozen river who must test each inch of ice. I have been performing this exercise daily for nearly two years, and I have discovered something troubling about it. The slower I walk, the more I see. At normal walking speed, the other monks are simply robed figures moving through corridors. At the speed of kinhin, they become *readable*—their weight distribution, their compensatory habits, the particular asymmetries of bodies shaped by decades of specific labor. I can tell which monks were once farmers by the way they plant their left foot. I can tell which ones have injuries they conceal. One elderly monk walks with a very slight rotation of his right shoulder that he has carried for so long it has become invisible to everyone, including himself. I see it every time.

I came here to stop seeing, and instead I have merely slowed myself to a speed at which seeing is inescapable.

Hideo

### Letter 6

*Received January 1959.*

Ōnishi-san,

A new year. I will spare you the ceremonies, which are long and involve quantities of chanting that strain my conviction that repetition produces enlightenment rather than merely hoarseness.

I have been given a new task: I am helping to maintain the monastery's small cemetery, which involves pulling weeds from between gravestones and replacing flowers according to a schedule I do not fully understand but follow obediently. The dead here are monks of varying antiquity. Their stones carry dharma names that compress entire lifetimes into two or three characters. I find myself reading these names the way I once read—that is—the way one reads anything that has been reduced to its essential information. A name on a stone is a kind of *kamae*: a posture taken toward eternity, meant to present a single face to all comers.

But even stones have vulnerabilities, if you look.

I did not intend to write that sentence. I will not cross it out, on the principle I described to you earlier regarding the hand and the mind.

I hope the new year finds you in good health and good practice.

Hideo

---

### Letter 7

*Received May 1960. This letter was significantly longer than the preceding six and arrived in a larger envelope, folded into thirds. The paper was of a different quality—heavier, with a faint texture—suggesting Hideo obtained it from a source outside the monastery's usual supply.*

Ōnishi-san,

I ask your patience. I am going to write at length about something I have been thinking about for several years, and I wish to do so with the precision the subject demands. I will frame it as theory. You will, I hope, accept the frame without testing its edges too aggressively.

I wish to discuss the concept of *suki*.

As you know—forgive me, I do not mean to condescend; I write to organize my own thinking—*suki* in its classical usage refers to the opening, the gap, the moment or position in which a combatant's defense presents an exploitable vulnerability. It is typically discussed in temporal terms: a *suki* is created by a premature commitment, a delayed recovery, an instant of inattention. The literature treats it as a lapse, an error, a failure of vigilance. This is inadequate.

There exists a second category of *suki* that is not a lapse but a *consequence*—not a failure of the stance but its logical, structural outcome. Consider a stance, hypothetically, built upon a wide left-forward base. The *hidari hanmi* in its classical form, though what I describe applies to several related configurations. The practitioner in this stance achieves formidable stability. The left side, advanced and grounded, presents a wall of structural integrity—rooted, coiled, capable of generating enormous forward force. In competition, this stance is prized precisely for the authority of its left-side presence. The left side says: I am here, I am immovable, and my counterattack will come from a foundation you cannot uproot.

This is true. It is also, from a certain angle—and I mean this literally, geometrically, a matter of degrees—fatal.

The very rootedness of the left base creates a dependency. The wide, planted stance that generates such forward power does so by committing the pelvis to a fixed rotational axis. The left hip, locked into its stabilizing function, becomes the fulcrum around which the entire body must turn. If the opponent's attack arrives not from the expected angle—front, or oblique front—but from a position approximately seventeen to twenty-two degrees off the right lateral axis, the practitioner must rotate through the full arc of his committed base to bring his center to bear. There is a moment—I estimate it at three-tenths to four-tenths of a second, depending on the practitioner's conditioning—in which the rotation is incomplete, the weight is caught between the planted left foot and the pivoting right, and the cervical spine is exposed above the left trapezius in a window approximately eight centimeters wide.

I should be more precise. The exposure is not of the cervical spine generally but of the junction between the third and fourth cervical vertebrae—C3-C4—where the lateral flexion of the neck during the rotation opens a gap in the muscular shielding that is, at rest, adequate. During the rotation, it is not. A descending lateral strike delivered to this window, if executed with proper angle and the full weight of the attacker's hip behind it, would transmit force directly into the vertebral column at a point above the diaphragmatic nerve root. The consequences of such a strike, delivered with a hardwood bokutō at full competition force, would range from permanent partial paralysis of the upper limbs to—depending on the angle of cervical displacement—loss of autonomous respiration.
