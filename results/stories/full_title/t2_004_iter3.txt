# The Hateful Immortals

### A Documentary History of the Greenbriar-Chen Marriage, 1847–2024

**By Dr. Amelia Thornton**
*Harwick University Press, 2024*

---

## I. The Wedding and the World

---

The daguerreotype survives in the archives of the Massachusetts Historical Society, catalogued under *Misc. Portraiture, Pre-Civil War, Box 14.* It is not a remarkable image by the standards of the form. A young couple stands before a painted backdrop of Ionic columns. The man is tall, narrow-shouldered, Chinese American, wearing a suit that fits him poorly across the chest—a detail consistent with Margaret's later recollection that he borrowed it from his shorter, stockier cousin, David Chen, because he could not afford one of his own. The woman is white, auburn-haired, small, and visibly delighted about something. Her left hand rests on his forearm. His right hand covers hers. The exposure time for a daguerreotype of this period was between fifteen and sixty seconds. For that duration, at minimum, they held perfectly still, touching, looking into the lens with expressions that sixty seconds of frozen posing could not flatten into the customary Victorian death-mask.

They were married on the eleventh of October, 1847, at the Third Congregational Church of Boston, by the Reverend Ezra Holt. Holt's personal journal, which his descendants donated to the Andover-Harvard Theological Library in 1923, contains a brief notation for that date:

> *Married today a Mr. James Chen and Miss Margaret Greenbriar. An unusual match. The groom is a Celestial, well-spoken, employed at the Cushing & Sons counting-house. The bride is the daughter of the late Alderman Greenbriar, whose estate, I gather, was less considerable than his reputation suggested. They seemed a pair of genuine affection. God speed them.*[^1]

[^1]: Holt, E. *Private Journal, 1845–1851.* Andover-Harvard Theological Library, MS 1187, p. 204. Holt's use of "Celestial" reflects the common mid-nineteenth-century American term for Chinese immigrants. James Chen was, in fact, born in Boston to parents who had arrived from Guangdong province in 1820. He spoke English with a Massachusetts accent and Cantonese poorly, a source of private shame that Margaret mentions in her 1982 interview with the journalist Patricia Wen. See Appendix C.

The society page of the *Boston Evening Transcript* ran a two-line notice, the brevity of which likely reflected the paper's ambivalence about mixed-race marriages rather than any editorial judgment on the couple's newsworthiness. At that time, the marriage of a white woman to a Chinese American man was legal in Massachusetts but socially conspicuous. Margaret's mother, Helen Greenbriar, did not attend the ceremony. Her absence is one of the few points on which both Margaret and James agree, though they disagree—177 years later—on the reason.[^2]

[^2]: Margaret maintains that her mother boycotted the wedding out of racial prejudice and that she stated, on the morning of October 11th, "I will not watch my only daughter marry a Chinaman and make herself ridiculous." James recalls—or claims to recall—that Helen was ill with influenza and sent her regrets with a "perfectly civil note." When I pressed Margaret on this discrepancy in our interview of March 2023, she closed her eyes as if reading from the wall of a room visible only to her and said: "She was standing at the window in her blue morning dress. The left sleeve had a mended tear near the cuff. She was holding a cup of coffee in her right hand. She had not been coughing. She was not flushed. She did not look ill. She looked angry." James, when presented with this account, said: "I don't remember the dress." The reader will note that this is an early and characteristic example of the evidential asymmetry that structures the entire archive of this marriage.

None of this—the daguerreotype, the journal, the newspaper notice—tells us what we most want to know, which is what the wedding *felt like.* For that, we are dependent on Margaret.

In our interviews, which were conducted over the course of fourteen months in the living room of the Greenbriar-Chens' current residence in Portland, Oregon, Margaret described the day with a precision that I have come to understand is not performative but neurological.[^3] She did not narrate so much as *replay.* She told me the temperature that morning: forty-one degrees Fahrenheit. She told me the particular quality of the light through the church's east-facing windows: "a pale amber, almost the color of weak tea, because the glass was old and unevenly blown, and there was a ripple in the third pane from the left that threw a little wobble onto the floor near James's feet." She told me the scent of the church: tallow, wood polish, and the faint vinegar smell of a hymnal that had been imperfectly dried after a spill. She told me her dress was ivory cotton with a French lace collar that her aunt had sent from Providence and that the lace itched terribly on the left side of her neck but she did not scratch it because she did not want to ruin the daguerreotype.

[^3]: Margaret's ability, which contemporary ability researchers classify as "absolute eidetic persistence" (AEP), emerged at age thirteen. She describes the onset as sudden and distressing: "One morning I simply stopped forgetting. Everything that happened stayed. I didn't understand it then. I thought I was going mad." For a clinical overview of AEP and its relationship to other memory-class abilities, see Harmon, P., *A Taxonomy of Persistent Abilities* (Cambridge University Press, 2019), pp. 88–112.

And she told me how James looked.

"He was terrified," she said. "He kept pulling at his collar with his index finger—his right index finger, always the right—and he was sweating, even though the church was cold enough that I could see the Reverend's breath during the Psalms. When I came down the aisle—and it was a very short aisle, it was a very small church—he looked at me as though he had been drowning and I was the surface of the water."

She paused.

"I have lived a long time, Dr. Thornton. I have seen that expression on other people's faces—in trenches, in hospitals, in camps. The look of a person who believes they are about to be saved. But I had never seen it before that day, and when I saw it on James, in that cold little church, I thought: this is what every person who has ever lived has been looking for. This."

She paused again, longer.

"I was wrong, of course. Not about what he felt. He felt it. I was wrong about what it meant. I thought it meant we would be happy. What it actually meant was that we were two young people so full of hope that we could not conceive of its eventual weight."

It is necessary to state, plainly and without scholarly hedging, that I believe her. It is also necessary to state that James, when I asked him about the same day, sat for a long time in the kitchen (he prefers the kitchen; Margaret prefers the living room; they have not voluntarily occupied the same room during an interview) and said: "I remember it was a good day. I think the weather was nice. She looked beautiful. She always looked beautiful—that hasn't changed. I was nervous, probably. I would have been nervous."

He remembers the shape of the day. She remembers every atom.

This, in miniature, is the entire marriage.

---

Before proceeding, it is necessary to equip the reader with certain contextual information that will make the subsequent narrative legible.

The existence of "specials"—the term is colloquial and imprecise, but it has proven ineradicable[^4]—was, by 1847, a fact of human life, but it was not yet a *cultural fact* in any organized sense. There were no registries, no research institutes, no government agencies, no popular media representation. There were simply people—perhaps one in every three to five thousand, based on the best modern epidemiological estimates[^5]—who, at or around puberty, manifested a persistent, involuntary ability that defied conventional material explanation.

[^4]: The more formal terminology—"ability-positive individuals," "persistents," or the clinical designation "Anomalous Capacity Persons" (ACPs)—has never achieved popular currency. "Specials," with all its condescending overtones, persists, particularly in American English. The British preference for "gifted," with its own distinct set of condescending overtones, is only marginally better.

[^5]: The precise prevalence remains contested, not least because many ability-positive individuals conceal their status. Harmon's estimate of 1:3,200 is the most widely cited; Okafor and Liu (2021) argue for a higher figure of 1:2,500 based on anonymized medical data from the National Health Service.

The abilities were—and remain—wildly heterogeneous. They appeared to follow no genetic pattern, no environmental logic, no moral or narrative arc. A butcher's daughter in Leeds might discover she could heat metal with her hands. A senator's son in Virginia might find that he could not be submerged in water—that the water would simply *move around him,* as if repelled. A seamstress in Kyoto might learn that she could feel the emotional states of any person within forty feet as a kind of thermal gradient on her skin, warm for joy, cold for sorrow, scalding for rage. There was no grand design. There was no shared purpose. There was simply an arbitrary rupture in the expected capabilities of the human animal, distributed randomly across the species, emerging at puberty like acne or romantic longing, and equally ungovernable.

In 1847, the cultural response to this phenomenon was predominantly local, ad hoc, and frightened. Communities dealt with their specials the way communities have always dealt with anomalies: through a mixture of awe, suspicion, exploitation, and violence, the proportions depending largely on whether the ability in question was useful to the neighbors or threatening to the minister. A woman who could make plants grow faster might be tolerated, even celebrated, in a farming community. A man who could see through walls would be run out of almost any town in Christendom.

Margaret's ability—her total, persistent, involuntary recall of every sensory experience from the age of thirteen onward—fell into a category that was more curiosity than threat. As a girl, she had been shown off at her father's dinner parties. "Tell us what we had for supper on your birthday, Maggie." "Recite the sermon from three Sundays ago." She performed. People marveled. It was, in the language of the era, her "parlor trick." No one, including Margaret, understood it as anything other than an eccentric gift—mildly useful, mildly entertaining, essentially benign.

James's ability was slower to reveal itself, for the obvious reason that unkillability requires a near-death experience to manifest. His came at fourteen: he fell sixteen feet from the hayloft of his uncle's barn, landed on the back of his neck with a sound that witnesses described as "like a green branch snapping,"[^6] and stood up. He was not injured. He was not sore. The fall had simply *not registered* on his body. His uncle, who was ability-positive himself (a minor telekinetic capacity, limited to objects under approximately two pounds), recognized the signs and sat James down for a conversation that James does not remember in detail but that Margaret reports—based on James's own earlier, more detailed retelling, decades ago—included the sentence: "You're going to have to be very careful about who knows."

[^6]: The witness was David Chen, James's cousin, who described the incident in a letter to his mother dated June 1839. Chen family correspondence, privately held, copies provided to the author by the Greenbriar-Chen household.

James was careful. Through his adolescence and early adulthood, his unkillability was an untested hypothesis confirmed only by the barn fall and a handful of minor incidents—a knife that slipped while he was cutting rope and drew no blood, a bout of scarlet fever that lasted six hours instead of six days, a carriage wheel that rolled over his foot without consequence. He did not advertise. He did not experiment. He went to work at Cushing & Sons and kept his head down and married Margaret Greenbriar on a cold October morning and believed, as she did, that they were beginning an ordinary life.

Their vows were the standard vows of the Third Congregational Church, transcribed in Reverend Holt's journal:

> *I, James, take thee, Margaret, to be my wedded Wife, to have and to hold from this day forward, for better for worse, for richer for poorer, in sickness and in health, to love and to cherish, till death us do part, according to God's holy ordinance; and thereto I plight thee my troth.*

Neither of them had yet understood what their abilities meant, and so neither paused at the clause that has since become, in Margaret's words, "the longest-running joke in our marriage." *Till death us do part.* The exit clause. The merciful finite horizon that makes the promise bearable. Ordinary couples speak it knowing, even if only dimly, that death will honor the contract—that thirty, forty, perhaps fifty years will be asked of them, and then the debt will close. Margaret and James spoke it in the same faith. They could not have known.

James Chen, as of the date of this publication, has survived seventeen gunshot wounds, four building collapses, three plagues, one direct lightning strike, two attempts at execution by state authorities (see Chapter 7), and the complete severing of his left arm in 1944 (it regrew over the course of eleven weeks). He has not aged past approximately twenty-five. He cannot die.

Margaret Greenbriar, as of the date of this publication, has not aged past approximately twenty due to a secondary and apparently unrelated effect of her AEP that remains poorly understood[^7]—though she is not unkillable in the way James is. She could, in principle, die. She simply has not.

[^7]: The connection between absolute eidetic persistence and physical age-stasis is observed in roughly 40% of AEP cases. The leading theory (Harmon, 2019) suggests that AEP involves a comprehensive fixation of cellular state as well as neural state—that the same mechanism that prevents memory decay also prevents somatic aging. This remains highly speculative.

*Till death us do part.* Neither of them can fulfill the condition. They have been married for 177 years.

I write this history in full knowledge that it is, at its core, a love story—but that it is a love story the way that a geological survey is a landscape: stripped of romance, measured in strata, and shot through with fault lines that will, eventually, produce earthquakes. What follows is my best professional attempt to tell the truth about a marriage that has outlived every institution, nation, and social norm that witnessed its creation. I rely on documents, on third-party accounts, and most of all on the testimony of the two principals, who have been generous with their time and who are, at this writing, still legally married, still cohabiting, and still, by their own separate admissions, "not sure how to answer that question" when asked if they love each other.

## II. Notes on Method, or: The Trouble with Margaret

---

It is customary, in a work of documentary history, for the author to address her sources and their limitations in a preface or appendix. I have chosen to address the matter here, in the body of the text, because in this case the limitations of my sources are not incidental to the narrative. They *are* the narrative.

My primary source is Margaret Greenbriar.

Margaret can recall, with zero degradation, every sensory experience she has had since the age of thirteen. This is not a figure of speech. When I asked her to describe the apartment where she and James lived in Paris from 1871 to 1889, she did not offer the kind of impressionistic sketch that even the finest memoirist would produce. She told me the precise dimensions of the front parlor ("fourteen feet by eleven feet eight inches; I measured it once, with a string, out of boredom, in the spring of 1874"). She told me the number of tiles on the kitchen floor (two hundred and sixteen, of which four were cracked, and she specified *which* four).
