# Perdition Gulch

## I.

The nail had been loose since April. Silas knew this because he had noted it on the seventh of that month while leaning against the stockyard fence waiting for Clell Morton to finish shouting about his eastern property line, and he had noted it again on the twelfth while separating two drunk cattlemen who each believed the other's horse was his, and again on the nineteenth, the twenty-third, and the second of May, each time thinking he ought to hammer it flat and each time not doing so, because the nail was not his business, the fence was not his fence, and the principle that the council did not own the town's infrastructure but merely maintained the conditions under which that infrastructure could survive was one of the five rules they had established in the spring of 1869 and had not, in twenty years, violated.

On the fourteenth of June, 1889, during a dispute between a rancher named Cartwell and a surveyor from the Union Pacific named Briggs—a routine matter, the sort of thing the council arbitrated twice a month, Cartwell insisting his grazing rights extended to the creek bed, Briggs unrolling a map that said otherwise—Silas set his right hand on the fence post at the wrong angle and drew his palm across the nail's exposed head and opened a cut three inches long from the base of his thumb to the heel of his hand.

He did not look at it immediately. Cartwell was mid-sentence, something about his grandfather's claim, and Briggs was smiling the way railroad men smiled, which was with the mouth only, and the morning was bright and dry and the kind of June morning when Perdition Gulch looked almost like a real town—the storefronts whitewashed, the church steeple upright, the schoolhouse chimney producing its responsible thread of smoke—and Silas was thinking about none of this because Silas was thinking about the fact that his hand was wet.

He closed his fist around the wound. The blood came through his fingers in the thin, purposeful way blood comes through when it has somewhere to go. He transferred his hand to the pocket of his coat. He settled the dispute in Cartwell's favor, because the map was six years old and the creek bed had moved, and because Briggs knew it had moved and had brought the map anyway, and because the purpose of the council was not to adjudicate who was right but to make certain that the town's equilibrium held until the next dispute, and the next, and the next, in a chain of small corrections that together constituted something close enough to justice that no one had yet burned anything down.

He shook Cartwell's hand with his left. He did not shake Briggs's hand at all. Briggs folded his map and said he'd be back with a new survey, and the way he said it—speculative, patient, like a man measuring a building he intended to buy—was the first thing that happened that day that mattered, though no one recognized it at the time.

Silas walked to the pump behind the dry-goods store and held his hand under the water and watched the blood curl away in the dust. He waited.

Nothing happened.

He waited longer.

The cut continued to bleed. It bled the way cuts bleed on the hands of men who are not Silas Crutchfield, which is to say it bled with the ignorant, animal persistence of a body that does not know it has been promised anything better. He pressed the wound with his thumb. The flesh did not knit. The skin did not seal. He held his hand up in the noon light and saw, for the first time in twenty-one years, the interior of his own body presented to him as a question he could not answer.

He tore a strip from the bottom of his undershirt. He wound it around his palm three times and tucked the end under and flexed his fingers to test the tightness. The rag bloomed red in the center. He put his hand back in his pocket.

He showed it to no one.

---

That evening, at eight o'clock, Isobel March sat at her desk in the land office—which was not properly a land office but the back room of what had been a feed store and before that a claim shack and before that a tent, the layers of the town's history visible in the architecture the way strata are visible in a canyon wall—and entered the deed transfer for the Cartwell parcel in her ledger. Her handwriting was small and precise and identical on every page of every volume, a consistency that would have been remarkable in anyone and was, in Isobel, merely automatic. She had not misremembered a date, a figure, or a name in her life. She was fifty-four years old. The inside of her mind was a library with no lost books, a courthouse with no misfiled papers, every document of her existence catalogued and cross-referenced and available for retrieval at a speed that made the act of *remembering* indistinguishable from the act of *seeing*. She could close her eyes and watch the afternoon of November 12, 1871, play behind her lids as though she were standing in it: the slant of the light, the smell of the cold, the exact words the minister used at Thomas Fain's funeral, the direction the wind pushed the cottonwood seeds. She could hold two decades in her head, simultaneously, and compare them the way a surveyor compares two maps.

She turned a page. She dipped her pen.

At the same hour, on the roof of the church three blocks north, a child who had no business being on a roof and who was known in Perdition Gulch only as Emmett sat cross-legged in the shadow of the steeple and listened.

Emmett was not listening to anything that sound could carry. Emmett was listening to Dolly Parsons four houses east thinking about how much she hated her sister's cobbler recipe. Emmett was listening to Bill Kettleman in the saloon replaying an argument with his wife in which he had lost badly and was now, in the privacy of his skull, winning. Emmett was listening to the Cartwell boy—fourteen, sun-dark, serious—lying in bed and thinking about the surveyor's assistant, a young man from Omaha with red hair, and the thoughts were confused and afraid and Emmett turned away from them with the practiced gentleness of someone who has been overhearing the private for so long that discretion has become autonomic. Emmett was listening to all of it—the two hundred and nineteen minds of Perdition Gulch producing their nightly murmur, the hum of a hive settling, each thought distinct if attended to and blurred into warm noise if not—and Emmett's face, tilted up toward the emerging stars, wore the expression of a child listening to a lullaby it has heard every night of its life and which it does not know it will soon never hear again.

Emmett was not, by the calendar, a child. Emmett was twenty-six years old. But something about the telepathy—whether the power itself or the isolation the power enforced—had preserved in Emmett a quality that everyone in town recognized and no one named, a quality that made grown men modulate their voices and women touch Emmett's shoulder as they passed, and which the council, in their private language, called *the listening*, as though Emmett were not a person but a quality of attention that the town could not do without.

Three streets south and one street west, in a room above the hardware store that they had shared for nineteen years, Jubal and Cora Mast played cards.

They were twins, though they did not look alike—Jubal was tall and raw-boned and carried himself like a man expecting to be hit, and Cora was short and dark and carried herself like a woman who had already been hit and found it unimpressive—but they moved with a single timing that was visible to anyone who watched for more than a minute. Jubal reached for the deck as Cora withdrew her hand. Cora said, "You're holding the—" and Jubal said, "—jack, I know, I'm getting to it." They did not need to look at each other's cards. They did not need to look at each other at all. The thing between them was not telepathy—Emmett had confirmed this years ago, had described the twins' connection as "not hearing, more like being in the same water"—but it was *something*, a shared sensorium, a doubled awareness that let them feel the emotional weather of any room they entered and, more importantly, let them feel *each other* with a completeness that made the word "twin" feel like a diminishment. They were one system in two bodies. They had always been one system. When the council voted, they counted as two, though everyone understood they were one.

Cora laid down a queen. Jubal said, "You always—"

"—lead with her, yes."

"I was going to say—"

"You were going to say I'm predictable."

"I was going to say you're consistent. There's a—"

"—difference, yes. Shuffle."

They were forty-nine years old. They had gray in their hair. They played cards every evening because the cards were not the point; the rhythm was the point, the steady back-and-forth that kept the channel between them open the way a pump must be primed to remain functional. They had discovered, years ago, that if they spent too long apart, the connection dimmed—not painfully, but noticeably, like a lamp turned low—and that the restoration, when they came back together, was accompanied by a disorientation that felt like surfacing from deep water. So they stayed close. They finished each other's sentences not as a trick but as a necessity. They played cards.

And Silas Crutchfield, in his cabin at the edge of town, sat on the edge of his bed and unwound the rag from his hand and looked at the cut, which had stopped bleeding but had not closed, and which was, in the lamplight, simply a wound—open, ordinary, and his.

---

This is what should be understood now, before anything else is understood:

Within two months, all of this will be gone.

The council will fracture. The powers—healing, memory, telepathy, the twins' shared perception—will fail, one by one, in an order that will seem like cruelty and will in fact be something worse than cruelty, which is *logic*. The town that these five people held together through twenty years of arbitration and quiet miracle will come apart at its joints, and the railroad will move in, and the people of Perdition Gulch will disperse into the ordinary brutality of the territory, and the place itself will become what most places in the West become, which is a set of foundations that the grass takes back.

The five will not die, though some of them will wish to. They will simply become what they were before the powers, which is to say: people who once stood on a ridge above a massacre and chose, with reasons, to do nothing.

But that has not happened yet.

It is June. The church steeple is upright. The schoolhouse chimney produces its thread. On the roof, the child listens. In her office, the memory-keeper remembers. In their room, the twins speak in halves.

And the healer sits alone with his open hand and understands, with the calm and total comprehension of a man who has spent twenty years waiting for a bill to come due, that it has arrived.

## II.

This is the part that happened first, though it will be told second, because the town of Perdition Gulch was built on the principle that sequence could be rearranged if you had the right people doing the arranging, and because the five members of the council had spent two decades proving that the order in which a thing was known mattered less than the *fact* of its knowing, and because the story of what they allowed to happen in the autumn of 1868 is not a story that can be told all at once—it must be developed like a photograph, each immersion in the chemical bath darkening the image further, until what emerges is too clear to look away from and too late to change.

What is known now, and what will become clearer later:

In October of 1868, the five people who would become the council of Perdition Gulch were not yet a council. They were not yet anything. They were five strangers and near-strangers traveling west through the territory for five different reasons, each carrying an ability they had never named and had learned, through separate and painful educations, to hide. Silas had been a field surgeon in the war and had watched his patients recover faster when he touched them, and had been asked questions by his commanding officer that he could not safely answer, and had left Virginia with the sound of those questions still sifting through his sleep. Isobel had been a schoolteacher in Ohio who had been dismissed for knowing things she should not have known—the contents of sealed letters, the dates of events she had not witnessed—and had gone west because the West was where you went when the East had decided you were strange. The twins had been exhibited, briefly and degradingly, in a traveling show in Missouri, billed as "The Siamese Minds," until Cora broke the showman's wrist and they crossed the river at night. Emmett had been born on the trail, parentage uncertain, raised by a succession of camps and way stations, hearing thoughts before learning words, speaking late and oddly, passed from hand to hand like a parcel no one had ordered.

They found each other the way such people find each other, which is to say: by accident, by proximity, and by the slow recognition that the person across the fire is flinching at things no one else can see.

By October they were traveling together. Not a group, not yet. A loose association of the wary. Silas could heal but did not advertise it. Isobel remembered everything but kept her observations spare. The twins kept their synchrony private. Emmett listened and said little.

They were camped in a gully—dry, shallow, red-walled, unremarkable—eight miles south of what would become Perdition Gulch, when Isobel climbed the ridge to check the weather and saw the smoke.

---

What Isobel saw from the ridge was this:

A settlement. Small. A dozen structures, some canvas, some wood. A corral with horses. A creek. The people moving between the structures were Pawnee—a mixed group, families, a seasonal camp near the creek's good water. Isobel had seen enough of the territory to recognize the arrangement: the distribution of cookfires, the way the horses were picketed, the unhurried quality of people who had been in a place for weeks and expected to remain.

Coming from the north, on horseback, in a formation that was not military but was also not civilian—too tight, too deliberate, rifles visible—was a party of twelve white men.

Isobel watched. She had the eyes for it. She could see, from three-quarters of a mile, the way the lead rider's horse stepped, the angle of the rifles, the fact that no one in the approaching party had raised a hand in greeting. She had, in her memory, fourteen separate occasions on which she had seen a group of armed men approach an encampment in this fashion, and on eleven of those fourteen occasions what followed was—

She went back down the ridge. She did not run. Running would have required a decision, and she had not yet made one.

"There's a camp," she said. "Pawnee. Maybe thirty people. And riders coming."

The twins looked at each other, which was redundant, since they were already looking at everything together, but the habit of human gesture persisted even in people for whom it was unnecessary.

"How many riders," Jubal said.

"Twelve. Armed."
