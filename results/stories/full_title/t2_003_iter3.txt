# Perdition Gulch

## I.

The nail had been driven crooked sometime in the previous decade, probably by the man who built the porch and who was now buried in the alkaline flat east of town where the ground was too hard and too white for anything but the dead. It protruded at an angle from the second rail, rusted to the color of the surrounding earth, and on the afternoon of August 11th, 1887, it caught the meat of Elias Cull's left palm as he pressed a warped board back into place.

He did not cry out. He was not a man who cried out. He pulled his hand free, watched the blood well in the crease below his fingers — a clean, deep slice, the kind that looks worse than it is — and wrapped it in the rag he kept in his back pocket for sweat. The rag had been white once. Now it was the color of everything in Perdition Gulch: dust, rust, ochre fading toward bone.

He finished the rail. He went inside and drank water from a clay pitcher and sat at the table where he ate his meals alone. The light came through the window in a single bright plank, and the flies moved through it with purposeless industry. After an hour he unwrapped the rag.

The wound was still bleeding.

This requires a moment's consideration, because the man sitting at that table had, over the course of the previous twelve years, been shot twice, stabbed once, trampled by a mule, and thrown from a horse onto a bed of shale that should have opened his skull like a dropped melon. Each time, the flesh had closed. Not slowly, the way flesh closes for ordinary people — the scabbing, the itching, the pink scar tissue tightening like a drawstring — but immediately, with a soft heat that moved from the center of his chest outward through the veins to the site of the insult, and then the skin simply *was* again, smooth and whole as though the injury had been a rumor. He had not been sick since he was nine years old. He had no scars. His body was, and had always been since whatever silent mechanism activated in his adolescence, a country that could not be permanently invaded.

He looked at the cut. He pressed the edges together with his thumb and forefinger and held them, counting to sixty. He released. The edges fell apart. The blood resumed its slow, patient work of leaving him.

He rewrapped the hand. He put on a clean shirt. He walked out into the late-afternoon light and crossed the packed-earth street to the assay office, where the council of five met every Thursday to discuss the things that kept a town of four hundred souls alive in a landscape that seemed designed, down to the molecular composition of its soil, to kill them.

---

The assay office had not been used for assaying in three years, not since the last of the prospectors gave up on the claims north of the ridge and drifted south toward Tucson or west toward nothing. The equipment remained: the scales, the crucibles, the fire-blackened counter where metals had been tested and weighed and declared valuable or worthless. Now the back room held a table, five chairs, a kerosene lamp, and on Thursday evenings, the five people who ran Perdition Gulch.

They were, in the order they were seated that evening:

Constance Alder, who sat at the head of the table because someone had to and because no one else wanted to face the door. She was forty-six and had gray hair she wore pinned tight to her skull and eyes the color of creek water over granite. She kept her hands flat on the table when she spoke, which was seldom, and when she did speak, it was with a precision that made other people feel clumsy in their own mouths.

Solomon and Saul Vane, who sat to her left, side by side, close enough that their elbows touched. They were not identical — Solomon was taller by an inch and carried his weight in his shoulders, while Saul was narrower and sharper in the jaw — but their movements existed in a synchrony that made people uneasy in ways they could not articulate. When one reached for the water glass, the other's hand twitched. When one laughed, the other was already smiling. They were thirty-eight.

Reuben Ibarra, who sat across from the twins and who was, at nineteen, the youngest person in the room by nearly two decades. He had dark, watchful eyes and the particular stillness of a person who is always listening to more than what is being said aloud. He had a habit of looking at people a half-second before they spoke, which he had learned, over years of practice, to disguise by pretending to glance at something behind them.

And Elias Cull, who sat at the foot of the table with his left hand in his lap, beneath the table's edge, where the blood was slowly soaking through the rag and onto his trouser leg in a pattern he could feel spreading, warm and wet, against his thigh.

The business that evening was water. The business was almost always water. The Jericho Creek allotment had been a source of dispute between the Parr and Devereaux ranches for the better part of two months, and Constance had brokered an agreement that gave Parr upstream access three days a week and Devereaux the remaining four, rotating seasonally, with a penalty clause involving livestock that was Byzantine in its specificity and had been designed, she told the others, to be so annoying to invoke that neither party would bother trying.

"Parr won't hold," Solomon said.

"Parr will hold," Saul said, and then, a beat later, with the faint grimace that meant he was conceding a point to himself: "Parr will hold for the summer. After that—"

"After that we revisit," Constance said. "The drought won't last. It never lasts."

"This one might," said Reuben.

Everyone looked at him. He shrugged — a gesture he had cultivated to deflect the implication of his certainties. "I've heard people talking. The old-timers. They say the aquifer's lower than '79."

He had not heard people talking. He had heard people *thinking*. He had heard Harold Parr lie awake at night composing letters to his brother in Kansas, rehearsing the words in his mind — *land's no good, water's gone, Mary won't stop coughing* — and he had heard Constance, earlier that afternoon, reviewing the Jericho Creek agreement in her memory, every clause preserved in the amber of total recall, and worrying, in the quiet back room of her thoughts, that it would not be enough.

"The aquifer is a concern," Constance said. "I've noted it."

Of course she had noted it. She had noted everything. She had noted the rainfall totals for every year since her arrival in 1875, stored in the unbreakable archive of her mind alongside the price of flour in 1879 and the exact phrasing of every argument the council had ever had and the number of boards in the floor of this room (forty-seven, with a knot in the twelfth board from the east wall that looked, if you stared at it, like a child's drawing of a horse). She had noted the color of the sky at dawn on over four thousand consecutive mornings. She had noted everything, because she could not do otherwise, because the world entered her and stayed, every detail permanent, every moment preserved in a fidelity that no journal or photograph or act of God could match.

She did not notice Elias Cull's hand.

None of them noticed his hand. He kept it still. He kept his face arranged in the expression of mild, competent attention that had served him for twelve years in these meetings, and he felt the blood wick into the fabric of his trousers, and he said the things he was expected to say about the Devereaux fence line, and when the meeting ended he was the last to stand, waiting until the others had filed out so that no one would see the dark stain on his leg.

He walked home in the dark. The stars were out — the terrible, profligate stars of the territory, dense as spilled salt — and the town was quiet. A dog barked in the distance. Somewhere a door closed.

He unwrapped his hand in his kitchen, by the light of a single candle, and looked at the wound. It had not closed. It had not begun to close. It looked exactly like what it was: a cut on a man's hand, ordinary and unhealing, the body performing its slow, inadequate repair — clotting, scabbing, the same fragile mechanism that every other human being on earth relied upon and that he had not needed since he was a boy standing in a field in eastern Missouri, watching a copperhead's fang marks vanish from his ankle as though they had never been.

He washed the wound. He bandaged it properly. He sat at his table for a long time, in the dark, after the candle guttered out.

He told no one.

## II.

How they found each other is a question Constance Alder could have answered with an exhaustiveness that would have taken days, because she remembered every moment of it — every hour, every word, every change of light — in the same way she remembered everything: completely, involuntarily, and without mercy.

She arrived in Perdition Gulch in March of 1875, when it was not yet called Perdition Gulch but simply "the crossroads" or "the water stop" or, by one optimistic cartographer in Prescott, "Millhaven," a name no one who lived there used. There were eleven structures. There was a well. There was a creek that ran six months of the year and a road that ran all twelve, connecting the mining settlements in the hills to the cattle country in the basin. She came from the east. She came from everywhere. She had been moving west for five years, staying nowhere longer than a season, because in every place she stayed, she eventually made the mistake of knowing too much — of recalling, in conversation, a detail she should not have retained, a date, a figure, the exact words a man had spoken to his wife in what he believed was privacy but which Constance had overheard once, from a distance, and could reproduce with the fidelity of a phonograph.

People did not like being remembered so precisely. She had learned this young. Total recall, in a woman, was received not as a gift but as an accusation.

She rented a room above the trading post. She kept to herself. She watched.

---

Elias Cull arrived in June of that year, on a dun horse with a saddle so old the leather was cracked to the color of charcoal. He was thirty-two. He had the bearing of a man who has been many things — soldier, laborer, drifter — and has quit each one not out of restlessness but out of the growing, tiresome necessity of concealment. He took a room. He did odd jobs. He was quiet and competent and made no trouble, which in a settlement of eleven structures was the highest possible recommendation.

Constance recognized him on the second day.

She did not recognize his face, precisely — though she had seen it, and she never forgot a face, and she could have described the exact configuration of his features as they had appeared to her three years earlier in a saloon in Abilene. What she recognized was his walk: the particular, unhesitating quality of a man who does not guard his body. Most people, she had observed over thirty-some years of involuntary observation, moved through the world with a subconscious architecture of self-protection — a flinch near hot metal, a brace against a stumble, hands raised in a fall. Elias Cull moved like a man for whom the concept of physical damage was theoretical.

She had seen him in Abilene. She had been passing through, eating a meal in the back of a saloon she should not have entered, and a fight had broken out near the bar — the usual frontier arithmetic, whiskey plus pride plus a disputed card hand — and a man had drawn a pistol and fired twice and hit a bystander in the chest. The bystander was Elias Cull. She saw him take the impacts, saw him stagger backward into a table, saw the blood on his shirt. And then she saw him straighten up. She saw him look down at his chest with an expression not of pain but of weary irritation, the way a person looks at a coffee stain on a clean shirt, and she saw him walk out of the saloon, and she saw — because she was Constance Alder and she saw everything and forgot nothing — the holes in his shirt begin to darken with clotting blood that should not have been clotting, not from chest wounds, not that fast.

She followed him out. He was already gone. She stored the memory. She could not have done otherwise.

Three years later, she watched him carry a beam for a man building a corral, and she saw the beam slip and a nail — another nail, a predecessor to the one that would matter twelve years further on — drive into his forearm, and she saw him wince and pull the nail out and wrap the arm without urgency, and she saw, beneath the wrap, the wound close.

That evening, she sat next to him at the trading post, where four or five residents gathered most nights to eat communally from a pot of whatever had been caught or slaughtered, and she said, without preamble, "I saw you get shot in Abilene three years ago."

He stopped chewing.

"I saw you take two bullets in the chest," she said, "in Brennan's saloon on East First Street, on the evening of October 14th, 1872. The shooter was a man in a brown coat with a harelip. You were wearing a gray shirt with a tear in the left cuff. You walked out. The shirt had two holes. You should have been dead."

He put down his fork, very carefully, the way a person puts down a weapon.

"Who are you?" he said.

"My name is Constance Alder. I remember everything. I remember every moment of every day I have ever lived, in perfect detail, including the moment I watched you survive something that should have killed you."

They stared at each other. The fire cracked. Someone across the room laughed at a joke neither of them heard.

In that stare was the entire future of Perdition Gulch, though neither of them knew it. In that stare was the pact and the council and the town rising from eleven structures to four hundred souls and the seven people dying in the valley and the boy listening to their final thoughts and the journals and the fire and the slow, mysterious unraveling of everything they would build. But in that single moment, before the future arrived, there was only this: the annihilating relief of being *known*. The end of hiding. It hit them both like a physical force — a warmth behind the sternum, a loosening of muscles held clenched for so long that the clenching had become invisible, indistinguishable from selfhood. Elias Cull's eyes filled with water, and he looked away, and Constance Alder noted the exact quality of the firelight on his face when it happened, and she stored it, and she would carry it for years with the same fidelity she carried everything: perfectly, involuntarily, and without mercy.

---

The twins arrived in August, on foot, carrying between them a single carpetbag and an air of wary, compressed intensity that made people step aside on the road without knowing why.

Solomon and Saul Vane had been born in a mining camp in Nevada in 1837, nine minutes apart, to a woman who died bringing the second one into the world. They had grown up knowing what they were — or rather, what they *was*, because the plural pronoun was, for them, a grammatical fiction. They were one mind in two bodies. Not telepathy, not the vague, sentimental "twin connection" that people spoke of to explain coincidental thoughts or sympathetic pains. They were *one mind*. When Solomon saw a thing, Saul knew it. When Saul felt a thing, Solomon suffered or savored it. They could not surprise each other. They could not lie to each other. They could not be in separate rooms without experiencing a disorientation so profound it resembled the onset of a seizure — a scattering of consciousness that only cohered again when they were close enough to touch.

They had tried, over the decades, to live as two people. They had worked separate jobs, courted separate women, adopted separate mannerisms and separate opinions. It never held. The performance of individuality exhausted them in a way that no physical labor could, because it required them to constantly, actively suppress the single most fundamental fact of their existence. They were one. They had always been one. The world's insistence on their twoness was the world's problem.
