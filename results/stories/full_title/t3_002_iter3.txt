# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

---

You learn to walk a certain way on a ship that lists four degrees to starboard. You don't think about it after a while. Your hips know. Your ankles know. I could cross the galley in the dark with a full pot of stock and not lose a drop, and I mention this not because it matters to anyone now but because it is the kind of thing that was true, and I have become careful about what is true.

The *Mara Celeste* was a hundred and twelve feet, Indonesian teak over steel, and she smelled of diesel and garlic and the particular sweet rot of wood that has been wet and dried and wet again more times than it should have been. Her engine was a Yanmar six-cylinder that hadn't run right since before I came aboard, and when she idled she made a sound like a dog dreaming — short catches, almost a whimper, then a long shuddering exhale. You could feel it in the galley floor through your shoes.

The Sargasso is not what people think. They think of a graveyard, a stillness, weeds that grab at your hull and hold. And that is true enough. But what I remember is the color — a brown-green that is not the color of any other sea, a color that says: what comes here stays here. Things do not pass through the Sargasso. They arrive.

Typhoon Sixteen — they called it Sixteen because it was the sixteenth named system of that season, though by the time it reached us it had been downgraded and then upgraded again and the numbers hardly mattered — came from the southeast on a Thursday. But I am getting ahead of myself. I have done that before, telling this. I get to the weather too quickly. The weather is not the story.

The story is Wen.

I will tell you about her hands. She had a scar on the left one, across the meat of the thumb, from a winch cable that had jumped its drum when she was nineteen. The scar was white and slightly raised and when she held her cards — we played cards, I will come to that — she held them in her right hand and rested them against the pad of her left thumb, against that scar, and I don't think she knew she did it. People don't know the things they always do. That is what someone who watches learns: the people you watch cannot see themselves, and you cannot stop seeing them, and so you carry things they never knew they gave you.

---

The wheelhouse had a wooden console that Wen's grandfather had built with his own hands in 1974. I know the year because it was carved into the underside — I saw it once when a fitting broke loose and Wen went under there with a flashlight and I brought her a wrench and looked over her shoulder and saw: *Wen Jiahao, 1974*, cut into the wood with something sharp and steady. A man's name and a year and nothing else. Below the console he had also mounted a small brass hook for no reason anyone could determine, and Wen hung her reading glasses from it every night before she went to her bunk, and that hook is the kind of detail that will not mean anything to you and that I cannot stop thinking about.

The ship had been in the family. I say it that way — *in the family* — because that is how Wen said it, the few times she said it, and the phrase carried a weight that made further questions seem impolite. Her grandfather had built portions of it himself, or rebuilt them. The galley table had knife marks that were not mine, cuts in the wood that predated me by decades, and I laid my cutting board over them each morning but I knew they were there. You do not cut directly into a table unless it is yours fully, yours the way a body is yours, and someone had used that table that way.

She would not replace the depth sounder even after it began giving false readings. It was the original, mounted by him, and when I once suggested — not even suggested, merely observed aloud — that the numbers it gave seemed unlikely, Wen looked at me with an expression I had never seen her wear before and have seen on no other face since. It was not anger, exactly. It was the face of someone who has been asked to explain something so fundamental to them that the request itself is a kind of violence.

I did not mention the depth sounder again.

The letters came to whatever port we docked in. Wen had them forwarded. For a long time she opened them at the galley table after dinner, while I washed up, and she would read them and fold them back into their envelopes and put them in the breast pocket of her jacket, and I would hear the paper against the fabric when she moved. Then she began to open them in her cabin. Then she began to bring them to her cabin without opening them. I could tell the difference because the opened ones left a torn strip of envelope in the waste bin and the unopened ones left nothing.

I stopped finding torn strips in February.

Meals changed. Wen had always been particular — not fussy, but she tasted things. She noticed when I used the smoked salt instead of the white. She would say, "The smoked," and I would nod, and that was conversation enough to carry a whole evening. After February she ate what I put in front of her the way you eat when eating is a task to be completed. Fork, mouth, swallow. She did not remark on the salt. I began using only the white because there was no one to notice and the smoked was expensive and we were — though she did not say this — watching money.

There was a pipe in the forward head that had been leaking since I came aboard, a slow drip that Wen had always met with plumber's tape and a wrench and a particular string of Hokkien that I will not translate. By March the drip had become a trickle and the trickle had stained the steel below it in a long orange tongue, and Wen walked past it every day and said nothing. I bought tape at our last supply port and left it on the ledge by the pipe and it sat there, unused, its packaging slowly dampening, and if you want to know exactly when I understood that something in Wen had broken, it was the morning I saw that tape still sitting there with a skin of mildew on the cellophane.

Her grandfather had named the ship something else. I never learned what. The stern had been painted over — the letters *Mara Celeste* were Wen's, and beneath them, if you looked in the right light when the paint was wet with spray, you could see a ghost of something longer, a word or words in characters I could not read. Wen never spoke the original name. It was there the way the knife marks were there, beneath my cutting board, beneath the paint, beneath everything she had laid on top of what had been given to her.

---

Typhoon Sixteen arrived the way trouble arrives when you've been watching for it: slowly and then everywhere. The barometer dropped for two days before the wind came, and the Sargasso, which is usually still enough that you can see your own reflection from the rail, began to develop a surface like hammered tin, small dents appearing and disappearing without pattern or rhythm, as if the sea were being struck from below.

The first morning of the storm I made tea. I say this plainly because it was a plain act and because plain acts are what I have to offer. I boiled the water in the kettle that was gimbaled to the stove, and I brewed the tea in the steel pot with the broken handle that I gripped with a folded cloth, and I poured it into the cup that Wen preferred — white enamel, chipped at the rim, bought in Surabaya before I knew her — and I carried it to the wheelhouse with both hands and my body angled into the roll the way my body knew to angle.

Wen was standing at the wheel. The window was a wall of grey-green water that fell away and then rose again, and the sound was not the sound of wind — it was deeper, it was structural, the sound of a ship disagreeing with the forces around it. Wen's hands were on the wheel and her feet were planted wide and she did not look at me when I came in.

I set the tea in the holder mounted beside the console.

"Morning," I said.

"Morning," she said.

The tea sat there. The ship rolled. The cup slid to the end of its holder and clinked against the brass rim and slid back. I watched the surface of the tea tilt — fifteen, twenty degrees — and a little slopped over the chipped edge and ran down the enamel and I could see the steam coming off it, and then the steam stopped coming off it, and it was just a cup of cold tea in a holder on a dying ship, and I took it away and poured it out and washed the cup and the next morning I made another.

We played cards that afternoon. This requires explanation only if you have never been in a typhoon and possibly not even then. We played every morning — had played every morning since my second week aboard, when Wen found me in the galley with a deck and said, "What do you play?" and I said, "Anything," and she said, "Big Two," and from then on it was Big Two at the galley table after breakfast, and neither of us had ever discussed whether this was a ritual or a habit or something else, and I suspect that the distinction would not have interested Wen even if I had raised it.

So we played Big Two on the first day of Typhoon Sixteen. The cards were damp. They stuck together when I shuffled and I had to peel them apart, and the ship was rolling enough that we weighted our discard pile with the salt cellar, the white salt, and Wen studied her hand with the focus that she had always brought to cards even when she could no longer bring it to meals or mail or leaking pipes. She played a pair of sevens. I played a pair of jacks. She looked at me over her cards with one eyebrow slightly raised, which is what she did when I played well, and for a moment the wind outside was just noise and the grey water slamming the porthole glass was just weather and we were just two people at a table with a game between us.

She won. She usually won. I am not a card player of any skill but I know enough to know that Wen played Big Two the way she did everything, which was with a kind of fierce, quiet attention that I have never managed to bring to any single thing in my life. She remembered every card I played. She knew my tells. I suspect she knew that I sometimes lost on purpose, in the early months, before I realized she could tell and that it offended her. After that I always played to win and almost always lost and this was, I think, the correct arrangement.

The storm worsened through the night. I did not sleep. I lay in my bunk and listened to the engine — the dog-dream whimper and exhale — and beneath it a deeper groaning that was the hull itself, the steel and the teak and the ribs that Wen's grandfather had laid or replaced with his own hands, all of it flexing against pressure it had not been designed to take. I have heard people describe the sound of a ship in a storm as screaming. It is not screaming. It is the sound of a thing trying to hold its shape.

The second morning I made tea. I carried it to the wheelhouse. Wen was still standing at the wheel. I am not certain she had moved. The tea from the day before was still in its holder — I had not taken it away the night before, a lapse, and it was cold and a film had formed on its surface and I took it and replaced it with the hot cup.

"You should eat," I said.

She said nothing.

I went back to the galley. I made congee because it was what I could keep in the pot with the seas running as they were, and I put it in a bowl with a lid and I brought it to the wheelhouse and I set it on the shelf beside the tea and I stood there. The wind was coming from the east, which was wrong — it had been coming from the southeast the day before, which meant we were in or near the eye wall, which meant things were going to get worse before they improved, though improved is a word I should not have used even then.

Wen's hands on the wheel were red and the knuckles were white and I could see the scar on her left thumb and I wanted to say something that I did not say and have not said and will not say here because this is not my story. This is hers.

The congee went cold beside the tea.

---

I should tell you about Bermuda.

I should have told you earlier, perhaps, but there is no right time for this part and so I will tell it now, which is when it surfaced for me — not as new information but as the fact that finally attached itself to all the other facts and made them mean what they had always meant.

There were men in Bermuda. I call them men though I never saw them and for all I know they were women or a committee or a computer that generated letters, but in my mind they were men and they wore suits and they had clipboards and they stood on a dock that I could see clearly even though I had never been to Bermuda and for all I know the dock I imagined does not exist. They worked for a bank whose name I saw on the envelopes — I will not write it here, the name means nothing, all bank names mean nothing, they are names designed to mean nothing — and they had been writing to Wen since before I came aboard.

I knew because of the envelopes in the waste bin, and then because of the absence of torn strips, and also because once, only once, in November, Wen had been drinking the whiskey she kept in the cabinet above the charts — not the good whiskey, the cheap kind, the kind you drink not to taste but to arrive somewhere else — and she said, without looking at me:

"They've drawn up the papers already. They've drawn them up and they're waiting."

I was drying a pot. I kept drying it.

"They think because it's a thing, because it has a number and a registration and a — a *valuation* —" She said the word the way you say the name of a disease. "They think that means it's theirs to take."

I put the pot away. I hung the towel on the hook.

"It was his," she said. "And then it was my father's. And then it was mine. And they've drawn up papers."

She did not say anything else. She finished the whiskey and she put the glass in the sink and she went to her cabin, and the next morning we played Big Two and she won and neither of us mentioned it.

But after that night I understood the things I had been seeing. The unopened letters. The uneaten meals. The pipe she could not bring herself to fix because fixing a pipe is something you do on a ship you are keeping, and Wen was no longer keeping a ship. She was holding one. There is a difference. Keeping is for the future. Holding is for now, for the closed fist, for the grip that knows it cannot last.

If she made port — any port, but Bermuda was closest and Bermuda was where the men with the papers waited — the *Mara Celeste* would be seized. That is the word. Seized. A word that means *taken by the hand*, though the hand that takes is not a hand you can see or bite or peel away finger by finger. It is a hand made of paper, which is stronger than any hand made of flesh. I have come to believe this.

And so when Typhoon Sixteen came from the southeast and the barometer dropped and the logical act — the sane act, the seamanlike act that any captain with any sense would perform — was to run north-northwest for Bermuda and shelter, Wen did not run. She held her course, which was no course. She stayed in the Sargasso. She stayed in the weeds that grip and the water that keeps what it catches, and I understood, though she never said it in plain words, that she had made a calculation that was not madness even though it looked like madness.

If the sea took the *Mara Celeste*, the sea took it honestly. The sea did not draw up papers. The sea did not send men with clipboards to stand on docks. The sea simply came over the rail and into the hold and down through the planking that her grandfather had laid, and it took everything at once and it did not pretend that what it was doing was anything other than what it was.

I am not saying she was right. But I have never been able to say she was wrong, and I have had a long time — a very long time, longer than you might think — to consider it.
