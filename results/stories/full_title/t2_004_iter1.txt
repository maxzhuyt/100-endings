# The Hateful Immortals

## A Documentary History of the Greenbriar-Chen Marriage, 1847–2024

### Compiled and Narrated by Dr. Amelia Thornton, Ph.D.
### Department of Anomalous Longevity Studies, Radcliffe Institute

---

## I.

The wedding daguerreotype is held in the permanent collection of the Peabody Essex Museum, though for decades it was misattributed. Plate number 1847-0914: a quarter-plate image, copper and silver, showing a young couple in formal dress. The woman is seated—Margaret Alice Greenbriar, age twenty-two, dark hair center-parted in the fashion of the period, everything about her posture suggesting she was told to hold still and found the instruction philosophically objectionable. The man stands beside her—James Wei Chen, age twenty-four, son of Chen Baoyu, an apothecary on Salem Street, one of perhaps forty Chinese residents of Boston at the time. His collar is too tight. You can see it in the set of his jaw. But his body lists faintly toward hers, perhaps two degrees off true vertical, the way a compass needle drifts north, and her left hand rests on his forearm with a specificity of grip that the photographer could not have posed. She is not resting her hand there for the composition. She is holding on.

They were married on September 14, 1847, at the home of Reverend Theodore Marsh, a Unitarian minister of sufficiently heterodox conviction to perform the ceremony despite what one attendee's letter called "the rather Oriental complication." Eleven people were present. Margaret's parents were not among them. James's father attended but left early, having expressed his objections in Cantonese with what James later described to Margaret as "an eloquence I hope you never acquire sufficient vocabulary to translate."

It was a love match. This is the foundational fact from which all subsequent catastrophe proceeds.

I should say a word about the phenomenon itself, since the popular literature has done such violence to the subject that a moment of clinical sobriety seems warranted. The condition known colloquially as "manifestation"—the spontaneous development of anomalous biological capabilities during or shortly after puberty—affects, by the most recent epidemiological estimates, approximately one in four thousand individuals worldwide. The mechanism is not understood. The capabilities vary enormously and follow no discernible taxonomy. For every case that makes the newspapers—flight, pyrokinesis, the fellow in Düsseldorf who can photosynthesize—there are a hundred that amount to little more than a biological oddity: the woman in Osaka whose hair is literally unbreakable, the man in Recife who has not blinked since 1987 but suffers no ocular consequence. Superpowers, the tabloids call them. The clinical literature prefers "idiopathic phenotypic novelty," which has the advantage of being honest about how little we know.

Margaret Greenbriar manifested at fourteen. The capability presented as what her father, a schoolteacher, initially believed was simply an excellent memory. It was not simply an excellent memory. Margaret Greenbriar possesses absolute eidetic retention: every sensory experience, every spoken word, every emotional state, stored at full fidelity and accessible on demand, with no measurable degradation over time. At the time of her manifestation in 1839, this seemed a remarkable if largely academic gift—a parlor curiosity, useful for reciting poetry and remembering recipes. Its actual implications would not become apparent for years.

James Chen manifested at sixteen, when he fell from the roof of his father's shop onto the cobblestones of Salem Street and stood up with a bruise that faded in the time it took a neighbor to cross the road. Subsequent incidents—a laceration that closed within the hour, a bout of typhoid that lasted a single afternoon—confirmed the pattern. James Chen is, in the clinical terminology, biologically indestructible: his body repairs all damage, resists all pathology, and does not age. He cannot be burned, broken, drowned, poisoned, or, as it transpires, killed, though not for lack of trying on the part of various parties across the centuries, occasionally including his wife.

That last remark is only partly facetious. As of this documentary's completion in September 2024, Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen are still legally married. They have been married for one hundred and seventy-seven years. They currently reside on opposite ends of the same property in rural Vermont—she in the farmhouse, he in the converted barn—an arrangement Margaret describes as "optimal" and James describes as "the DMZ." In separate interviews conducted for this project, both used the word "love" to describe their feelings for the other, though in each case the word was delivered in the specific tone one might use to identify a chronic illness. "Of course I love him," Margaret told me, seated in a farmhouse kitchen that contained, I noted, a single coffee mug. "That was never the question. The question is whether love, in sufficient quantity over sufficient duration, becomes its own particular form of hatred, and the answer is yes, obviously, any honest married person will tell you that, I simply have more data."

James, in the barn, said: "She remembers everything. Everything. Every good thing I've ever done, every terrible thing, every mediocre thing, all of it stored at the same volume, all of it available for deployment at any moment. Do you know what it's like to argue with someone who can quote your exact words from 1858? Not the gist. The *words*. With the original inflection?" He paused. "I love her completely. I have also, at various points across the last two centuries, parsing the feeling with some care, hated her so intensely that I walked into the ocean and tried to breathe water, just to feel something that wasn't this marriage. I was, of course, fine. I am always fine. That's the whole goddamn problem."

The Greenbriar-Chen marriage is, to the best of my research, the longest continuous marriage in recorded history. It is also, by any honest assessment, a disaster—though its particular flavor of disaster has much to teach us about the nature of love, memory, and the mercifully limited span of ordinary human life.

This is their story, or as much of it as two profoundly stubborn people were willing to let me tell.

## II.

They met on an evening in October 1845, at a public lecture on galvanism at the Boston Lyceum, which Margaret was attending out of genuine scientific curiosity and James was attending because a friend had told him there would be refreshments. Here I must note a methodological advantage unusual in historical scholarship: Margaret's testimony about events of the 1840s carries an evidentiary weight equivalent to that of a living eyewitness, because she is one. Her recall is not reconstruction. It is playback. When I asked her to describe the evening, she closed her eyes for a moment—a habit, I came to learn, not because she needs to concentrate but because the vividness of full-resolution memory is disorienting when layered atop present sensation—and then spoke in a continuous, detailed narrative that lasted forty minutes and included the weather (clear, 47 degrees, a north wind carrying the harbor smell of salt and tar), the color of the lecturer's waistcoat (bottle-green, with a gravy stain on the left lapel he kept touching as if hoping it would disappear), and the precise moment she noticed James Chen standing near the back of the hall.

"He was the only person in the room," Margaret told me, "who looked like he was actually thinking about what the lecturer was saying. Everyone else was performing attention. He was just—" She paused, and something shifted in her face, a tectonic movement beneath composed features. "He was chewing his thumbnail and frowning, and when the lecturer made an error about volta piles, James said 'No, that's wrong' under his breath, not to anyone, just to the air, and I thought: oh, there's a person."

James's recollection of the same evening is less granular—his memory is human, merely human—but he remembers Margaret. "She was standing too close to the demonstration apparatus," he told me. "Everyone else had stepped back when the lecturer started running current, and she had stepped forward. I thought she was either fearless or not paying attention, and then I looked at her face and it was obviously the first thing."

Their courtship unfolded across the autumn and winter of 1845–1846, conducted in the manner available to a young Chinese-American man and a young Anglo woman in antebellum Boston: carefully, partially in secret, and with the constant awareness that they were visible in a city that preferred not to see them. James's father, Chen Baoyu, operated a small but respected apothecary; James had been educated at Boston Latin through a combination of his father's insistence and a sympathetic headmaster's intervention, and he moved through white Boston with the particular alertness of a man who knows exactly how conditional his welcome is. Margaret, whose father taught arithmetic at a grammar school in Roxbury and whose mother had aspirations of a slightly better marriage for her daughter—specifically involving a man whose surname did not require explanation—had already demonstrated, by this point in her life, a constitutional inability to do what was expected of her.

They walked along the harbor. They attended lectures. Margaret brought James a copy ofErta's *Treatise on the Chemical Arts*, which she had read once, at age sixteen, and could therefore reproduce from memory in its entirety—a fact she demonstrated one evening to James's astonishment, reciting pages verbatim while he checked against the physical text. "She got every word," James recalled. "Every word on every page. Including a typographical error on page 114 that she reproduced without noticing, because her memory doesn't interpret, it just—stores. I remember thinking: this woman is extraordinary. I also remember thinking: this woman is going to be very difficult to be married to. Both assessments have held up."

Margaret's journals from this period—handwritten, though they function less as personal reflection than as verification against her own memory, a kind of external backup she maintained for decades before abandoning the practice—record the hours with the specificity of a court stenographer and the emotional transparency of someone who has not yet learned that her feelings, once recorded, will never degrade. An entry from February 1846:

> *He brought me a camellia today. Not a rose—he said roses are a cliché perpetuated by men who can't be bothered to learn the names of other flowers. It was white, just beginning to open, and he'd carried it inside his coat to keep it from the cold, and when he gave it to me it was warm. I held it against my cheek. He watched me do this and his face did something I don't have the vocabulary to describe except to say that I have seen that expression now and would like to see nothing else for the rest of my life. I think the rest of my life might be quite long. I think his might be too. I think I do not care, so long as he keeps looking at me like that.*

Reader, he did keep looking at her like that. For a while.

The marriage itself, in September of 1847, was a small affair—necessarily so, given that Margaret's parents refused to attend and James's father came only under protest. Reverend Marsh performed the service in his own sitting room, beside a window that overlooked a garden where the roses had gone to hip. Margaret wore a dress she had sewn herself, blue cotton, not white—a choice she made, she told me, "because I knew I would remember it forever and I have never looked well in white." James wore a borrowed suit. His hands, Margaret recalls, were shaking—a detail that still carries the odd, faintly cosmic humor of a man who could survive a building collapse but could not hold a ring steady.

The vows were standard. The kiss was not. "He put both hands on my face," Margaret said, during our interview, her own hands resting palm-down on the kitchen table, very still. "Both hands. And he said—this was not part of the ceremony, Reverend Marsh had finished, this was just James—he said, 'Margaret, I am going to be good to you for the entire rest of our lives, and I need you to understand that when I say that I mean something more than most people mean, and I mean it anyway.' And he was—" She stopped. She pressed one hand flat against her sternum, as if holding something in place. "He was looking at me. In that way."

I waited.

"I remember it perfectly," she said. "That is, as I hope you appreciate by now, exactly the problem."

## III.

The argument took place in April 1853, in the parlor of their rented house on Myrtle Street, Beacon Hill. They had been married five and a half years. Margaret was, in the calculus of the world outside their door, twenty-eight years old and beginning to draw the occasional comment about how *well* she looked for her age—comments that were still complimentary in 1853 but would curdle into suspicion within the decade. James was working as a clerk at a shipping firm, a position beneath his education and abilities but consistent with what was available to a man of his appearance in that city and that era.

The proximate cause of the argument was money. James's father had died the previous autumn, leaving debts rather than inheritance, and the cost of settling the estate had consumed their savings. Margaret had suggested—carefully, she thought—that they might write to her parents, with whom she had maintained a thin epistolary connection since the wedding. James had heard, not unreasonably, a different suggestion: that his family's failure had created a problem that her family's condescension might solve.

What followed was a marital argument of the kind that any married person will recognize: a disagreement about a specific, practical matter that, within the span of twenty minutes, became an argument about everything—about gratitude and sacrifice and who had given up more, about the unspoken hierarchies of their respective losses, about whether Margaret's willingness to marry across the color line entitled her to some credit James found obscene. These are not unusual arguments. They are, in fact, so common that the therapeutic literature has taxonomized them: the Foundational Grievance Spiral, in which a surface conflict peels back to reveal the older, deeper wound beneath. Every marriage has a version. In most marriages, they are survivable, because human memory is engineered for survival—it blurs, it softens, it edits, it lets the sharpest edges of old pain wear down to something handleable.

I want to be precise about what James said, because Margaret was precise about it with me, and precision is the mechanism of the wound.

According to Margaret—and there is no reason to doubt her, as there is no reason to doubt a high-fidelity recording—James, at the peak of the argument, standing near the fireplace with one hand on the mantel, said: "You want to write to your parents. Fine. Write to them. Tell them their daughter married a Chinaman who can't keep her in the style she was promised, and see if their pity comes with a bank draft. You were always good at performing suffering for an audience."
