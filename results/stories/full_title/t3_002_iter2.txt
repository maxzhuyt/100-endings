# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

---

I was the cook.

I was the cook aboard the *Mara Celeste*, a 3,200-ton general cargo vessel, registered out of Hamilton, Bermuda, owner-operated by Captain Wen Tsai-Lien, who was also the sole licensed officer aboard during the period in question. I held no maritime certification. I was hired in Kaohsiung in March of the previous year to prepare meals and maintain the galley and living quarters. My duties also included cleaning the captain's cabin, laundering crew linens when there was crew to launder for, and performing such general maintenance as fell within my competence, which was not inconsiderable. I am not going to tell you my name. It doesn't bear on anything. I am going to tell you what happened during the last three days aboard the *Mara Celeste*, which are the last three days that vessel existed in the world, and I am going to tell it in order, and I am going to tell it once.

You may note what you wish. I don't know who you are.

---

Before the weather, you have to understand the letters.

Captain Wen's grandfather, Wen Bao-Yu, purchased the hull of the *Mara Celeste* in 1987 from a Panamanian outfit that was liquidating its fleet. She was called the *Doña Esperanza* then. He re-engined her, replated the forward holds, and rechristened her himself with a bottle of Shaoxing wine because he did not believe in champagne. This is something Captain Wen told me once, and only once, on a night when she had drunk two beers, which for her constituted excess. Her grandfather had welded a small brass plate inside the engine room, just above the main coupling, engraved with characters I could not read. Captain Wen told me it was a phrase from the *I Ching* — something about the persistence of water, or the patience of water, I can't recall which. I cleaned around that plate every two weeks. The brass stayed bright because I kept it bright.

The ship had been in the family since then. Grandfather to father, though the father had not wanted it. Father to Captain Wen, who had.

I cleaned the captain's cabin every third day. This was the arrangement. She was a private woman and three days was the interval she permitted. I stripped the bunk, swept the deck, emptied the wastebasket, wiped down the chart table. I was not instructed to attend to her correspondence, but correspondence accumulates in a small space, and a person who cleans a small space comes to know it as she knows her own body — by feel, by weight, by what has shifted.

The first letters from the bank arrived in Yokohama, five months before the storm. They were white envelopes, heavy stock, with a blue logo in the upper left corner — a compass rose circumscribed by a circle, beneath the words ATLANTIC MERIDIAN TRUST. I did not open them. I did not have to. I have cleaned the quarters of enough people in my life to know what a bank letter looks like when it is not bringing good news. Good news comes in thin envelopes. These were thick. They had heft. They were the kind of envelope that contains a second envelope, pre-addressed, for your reply.

Captain Wen opened the first four. I know this because I saw the slit edges in the wastebasket, neat cuts from the ivory letter opener she kept in the chart table's top drawer. I saw the envelopes but never the letters themselves. She disposed of those separately, or kept them, or — for all I know — ate them. I did not look. But I cleaned around them, and I noticed things the way a person in a house notices: not by investigation but by proximity.

The fifth letter she did not open.

It arrived in Guam and sat on the chart table for six days. I cleaned around it. The sixth letter arrived before we left Guam and she placed it on top of the fifth, aligned, the way you'd stack plates. By Honolulu there were nine. By the time we took on the cargo for Bermuda — machine parts, crated, forty-two tonnes, barely enough to justify the fuel — there were fourteen. I would not swear to that number, but I am close. They accumulated the way a season accumulates: you don't watch it happen, and then one day the table is different, the way a garden is different in October from how it was in June.

She stopped slitting them open on a specific day. I can tell you the day because it was the day she also stopped taking sugar in her tea, and I have always tracked people by how they take their tea, which is a more honest indicator of a person's state than anything they might say with their mouth. It was the eleventh of September. We were four days out of Honolulu, heading southeast, the sky that blank Pacific blue that looks painted on. I brought the morning tea — oolong, the good leaves, not the dust — with one sugar, as she took it. The next morning she said no sugar. She said it without looking up from the chart. "Plain today." And every day after.

A person who gives up sweetness is preparing for something.

The letters I understood this way: the *Mara Celeste* was leveraged. I do not know the financial terminology exactly and would not insult either of us by pretending. What I understood, from the letters I did not read and the replies she did not send, was that the ship owed more than the ship was worth, and that the entity to which it was owed — Atlantic Meridian Trust, with its blue compass rose — had decided that the deficit would be resolved in Bermuda. I understood that men would be waiting at the dock with papers. I understood that the papers meant the ship would stop being hers.

I did not understand this all at once. I understood it the way you understand that someone in the house is dying — not from the diagnosis but from the way the food comes back to the kitchen untouched, from the way the mail piles up, from the way the silence in a room changes weight.

Captain Wen never spoke of it. Not to me. But she began, in the last weeks, to touch things on the ship in a way I recognized. She would run her hand along the bridge console. She would pause at the engine room hatch and rest her fingers on the frame. Once I saw her standing in the cargo hold after the machine parts were loaded, just standing there with her palm flat against the bulkhead, the way you'd put your hand on a horse's neck.

She was saying goodbye to it. I knew that. I cleaned around that knowledge the way I cleaned around the letters.

---

I brought her tea on the first night of the typhoon.

This requires context. The storm — Typhoon Meral, they called it later, I learned this afterward, though aboard we called it nothing because we were inside it and you do not name a thing that is eating you — the storm was not a surprise. Captain Wen had the weather reports. She tracked the system for two days on the satellite feed, and I know this because the printouts were on the chart table, laid over the bank letters like a second geological layer, and when I cleaned on the twenty-sixth I saw them: isobar maps, wind-field projections, the storm's predicted track drawn in red grease pencil in Captain Wen's hand. The track and our course converged. I could see that. I am not a navigator, but I can read two lines that cross.

She could have turned north. Later I sat with this knowledge the way you sit with a stone in your shoe, and I will sit with it until I stop sitting with anything. The reports were clear. She could have diverted to Wake Island, or turned back, or simply stopped and let it pass to the south. She did not. She maintained course for Bermuda, which was the course that intercepted the storm, which was the course that ended.

But the tea.

I brewed it at 2200 on the night of the twenty-seventh. The seas had been building all day — long swells, the kind that don't break but roll under you like something breathing in its sleep. By evening the wind was twenty-five knots and rising, and the *Mara Celeste* had developed a slow, heavy roll to port that made the galley cabinets creak in a rhythm I had not heard before. I wedged the kettle into the stove gimbals. I measured the leaves into the steel pot — the white porcelain one had gone to pieces weeks ago when we hit weather off the Marshalls, and I had swept it up and said nothing because it was Captain Wen's mother's pot, I think, or her grandmother's, and I did not want to make her look at the pieces. The steel pot. Oolong. No sugar. I poured at exactly 2200 because she expected it then and a person in a storm needs to know that some things are still where they should be.

I carried it to the bridge. This was a walk of perhaps forty feet, but in the swell it took me nearly a minute, bracing against the bulkhead with one hand and holding the cup — the heavy ceramic mug, the blue one with the chip on the rim, the one she always used — in the other. The tea sloshed but I did not spill it. I have carried tea in worse.

Captain Wen was standing at the helm. The autopilot was engaged but she stood there anyway, feet apart, one hand on the console, watching the darkness through the bridge windows as though the darkness might eventually explain itself. She was wearing the grey sweater she wore in all weather below twenty degrees — a wool thing, too large, stretched at the cuffs, that I had mended twice at the left elbow. Her hair was tied back. She did not turn when I entered.

I set the tea on the console shelf, in the fiddle rail where it would not slide. She said thank you. Her voice was steady.

I went back to the galley.

At 2300 I returned to collect the cup. I will tell you what I found: the tea was exactly where I had left it. The cup was full. The surface of the liquid was still — whatever motion there was in the ship, the tea had long since stopped responding to it, the way a thing stops responding when it has cooled past the point of having any energy of its own. I touched the side of the mug. It was ambient. The tea was the temperature of the air, which was the temperature of the bridge, which was the temperature of a room in which no one is paying attention to being alive.

Captain Wen was still standing at the helm. Same posture. Same window. I picked up the cup and she did not look at it and she did not look at me.

I poured the tea into the galley sink and watched it drain out. It was the colour of rust.

I brewed a second cup. I brought it at 2340. By then the wind was perhaps thirty-five knots and the roll had deepened so that I had to brace in the passageway with both feet and one shoulder, holding the cup against my sternum like something I was protecting. I set it in the fiddle rail. She said thank you. Same voice. Same inflection. I went back.

At 0045 I came for the second cup. It was full. It was cold.

I could tell you that this is when I knew. But a person does not know a thing at a single moment. A person knows it the way a cloth absorbs water — slowly, then all at once, and by the time you notice, the cloth is heavy and your hands are wet and you cannot say which drop was the one that saturated it. I had been absorbing what I knew about Captain Wen for weeks. The cold tea was not the knowing. The cold tea was the moment the cloth could not hold any more.

I did not brew a third cup. There is no use in asking a question when you have already received the answer twice.

---

On the morning of the twenty-eighth — the second day — the typhoon had arrived in full, and by arrived I mean it was no longer approaching or building or threatening but had simply *become the world*, the way a fever becomes the world for the person inside it: there was no outside, no before, no place the wind was not. The *Mara Celeste* was taking seas over the bow. The forward hold was taking water; I could hear it, a sloshing beneath the deck plates that had a heaviness to it, a patience, the water not crashing but accumulating, taking up residence. The ship's motion had changed overnight from a roll to something more complex — a corkscrew, a stagger, the gait of a thing that has been hit and is deciding whether to fall.

At 0700 I went to the bridge because it was Tuesday.

On Tuesday mornings Captain Wen and I played cards.

This was an old arrangement — older than the typhoon, older than the bank letters, almost as old as my employment. It began in our second week out of Kaohsiung, when I found a deck of cards in the galley drawer, a cheap thing with a tourist print of Taipei 101 on the backs, and I set them on the mess table one morning and she sat down and we played *Big Two*. You may know it by another name — some call it *deuces*, the Cantonese call it *choi dai di* — but in Captain Wen's family it was called simply *the game*, as though there were no other. She had learned it from her grandfather, who had played it every morning of his life aboard this ship, and she told me this on that first Tuesday in the offhand way that people share information they do not know is sacred.

We played every Tuesday that we were both aboard and conscious. We kept a running score in a small notebook — a pocket-sized thing with a green cover — that lived in the galley's top drawer beside the cards. The score went back eleven months. I was ahead by thirty-seven points, or perhaps thirty-eight. She disputed one hand from July and I let her dispute it because the argument was part of the game.

On the morning of the twenty-eighth I brought the cards and the notebook to the bridge because the mess was taking spray through a seal that had failed in the night and the table was wet. Captain Wen was in the helmsman's chair. She had not slept; I could see this in the particular stillness of her face, which was the stillness of a person who has gone past fatigue into the flat country on the other side. I set the cards on the console. She looked at them. She looked at me.

"Dealing or cutting?" she said.

I dealt.

The wind was — I don't know numbers. High. The bridge windows were white with spray and vibration, and the wipers had stopped working sometime in the night, so the world outside was an abstraction, a smear. The ship was groaning. I should tell you about the groaning because it is important and because I do not think I can make you hear it, but I will try: it was not a single sound. It was a conversation. The hull plates spoke in low, stressed vowels — a flexing sound, an *eee* that rose and fell with the wave cycle. The frames behind the plates answered in a different register — a cracking, popping sound, like knuckles, like ice. Somewhere below us, in the engine room or the shaft alley, something metal was rubbing against something metal with a rhythmic shriek that came and went as the ship's motion loaded and unloaded the hull. The pumps — the pumps were still running then — had a battered, labouring sound, an asthmatic chugging that I had been listening to for hours and that had become, for me, the sound of hope, or at least of continuation, because as long as the pumps were coughing the water was going somewhere that was not the inside of the ship.

Against this: the snap of cards on the console.

Captain Wen arranged her hand. She had a particular way of holding the cards — fanned tight, close to her chest, the edges aligned with a precision that always struck me as architectural. I had thirteen cards. She had thirteen cards. The rules of *Big Two* are simple and I will not bore you, but the gist: you play combinations — pairs, triples, straights, full houses — and the goal is to empty your hand first. The two of spades is the highest card. It is the executioner. Captain Wen's grandfather had bent the corner of the two of spades in this particular deck, years ago, either by accident or as a private joke, and we played with that marked card because to replace the deck would have been to break something that should not be broken.
