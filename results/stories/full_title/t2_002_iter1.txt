# The HOA Board Will Now Come to Order

---

## I.

The notice arrived in a clear plastic sleeve, laminated against weather, which Meredith later thought was a nice touch — as though the HOA expected her to need to consult it outdoors, in rain, over a period of months. She found it zip-tied to the railing of the ramp itself, which was either an administrative coincidence or a small, perfect cruelty. She chose not to determine which.

She read it standing at the mailbox, though the mailbox was twenty feet from the ramp and the notice had not come through the mail. Someone had walked up the driveway, past the ramp, and affixed the notice to the ramp, and walked back down the driveway, past the mailbox, without using it. She held a water bill in her left hand and the notice in her right, and the sun was the honest, indifferent sun of late September in a suburb thirteen miles from a city that did not think about this suburb at all.

> **GREENRIDGE COMMONS HOMEOWNERS ASSOCIATION**
> **NOTICE OF VIOLATION — CC&R SECTION 4.7.2**
>
> Dear Homeowner(s) of Record — Unit 14, 2407 Larkspur Ct.:
>
> This letter respectfully reminds you that all exterior structural modifications, including but not limited to ramps, railings, pergolas, trellises, raised garden beds, and permanent or semi-permanent accessibility fixtures, require prior written approval from the Architectural Review Committee (ARC) per Section 4.7.2 of the Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions, as recorded with the County Recorder's Office on March 14, 2003, and amended June 11, 2011.
>
> A recent property survey conducted on September 22 identified an unapproved wooden ramp structure at the front entrance of the above-referenced unit. As of the date of this notice, no ARC application has been received for this modification.
>
> Pursuant to Section 9.3.1 (Enforcement and Remedies), a fine of $50.00 per day will be assessed beginning seventy-two (72) hours from the date of this notice and will continue to accrue until (a) the modification is removed, or (b) a retroactive ARC application is submitted, reviewed, and approved. Please note that retroactive applications are subject to an additional processing fee of $200.00 and a review period of sixty (60) to ninety (90) business days.
>
> The Board values the character and curb appeal of our community and appreciates your prompt attention to this matter.
>
> Warmly,
> Douglas R. Whitmore
> President, Greenridge Commons HOA Board of Directors

Meredith read it once. She read it again. She did not need to read it a third time.

Fifty dollars a day. The notice was dated September 24. Seventy-two hours' grace meant the fine began accruing on the twenty-seventh. The retroactive application — if she filed it tomorrow, if she found the form, if the form existed in a findable location, which in her experience of HOA forms it would not — carried a sixty-to-ninety-business-day review period. Business days. She converted: twelve to eighteen calendar weeks. Call it four months at the optimistic end. Four months times thirty days times fifty dollars was six thousand dollars. At the pessimistic end — ninety business days, which was eighteen weeks and change, which was one hundred and twenty-six calendar days — it was sixty-three hundred. Plus the two-hundred-dollar processing fee. Plus whatever additional fines might be levied for noncompliance during the review period if the review period itself was considered a period of ongoing violation, which, knowing the CC&Rs — and she had read them, once, the way she had once read the Federal Reserve's actuarial tables, with the grim procedural attention of someone accustomed to documents that governed fates — it almost certainly was.

Or she could remove the ramp.

Inside the house, through the front window she could see from the mailbox, Richard was in his wheelchair. He was facing the window. He may have been watching her, or he may have been watching the elm, or he may have been watching nothing in particular. His body, which had for sixty-one years been a body in the way that bodies are — present, functional, unworthy of detailed consideration — was now the central fact of their lives. The ALS had started in his left hand, a dropped coffee cup in January that they had both agreed to call clumsiness, and it had moved with the unhurried, confident pace of something that knew it had nowhere to be and all the time in the world. By June he needed the chair. By August the visiting nurse had started coming Tuesdays and Thursdays, then Tuesdays through Fridays, then every day. The ramp had appeared on a Saturday. Their son, Michael, had driven up from Denver with two-by-fours and a circular saw and a printout from a website about ADA-compliant slopes, and he had built it in five hours, badly, with the furious competence of a man who could not fix the thing that needed fixing and had found a thing that a saw could touch.

The ramp was ugly. Meredith could admit this. The wood was pressure-treated pine, the color of week-old guacamole, and Michael had not sanded the railings, and the angle was slightly steeper than the printout had recommended, so that pushing Richard up it required a certain commitment, a leaning-in, that she could feel in her lower back and her right shoulder and the place behind her sternum where feelings she did not have time for tried to accumulate. The ramp was necessary. Either they had a ramp, or Richard did not leave the house. Those were the options. She had considered them as a set.

She stood at the mailbox and did the math.

She could not help it. The math was always there, waiting under every situation like groundwater, and all you had to do was poke the surface and it came up. When Richard was diagnosed, she had calculated his expected remaining lifespan within forty minutes of leaving the neurologist's office — not because she wanted to, but because her brain was what it was, a machine built over forty years at Pacific Mutual to assign numbers to catastrophe, and it ran whether she told it to or not. Median survival from onset of symptoms: two to five years. Richard's progression was faster than median. She knocked eighteen months off the upper bound. She was not cruel. She was accurate. These were the only two things she had ever reliably been.

Now she did the math on the fine.

If Richard lived another eight months — this was her private estimate; she had not shared it with anyone except the visiting nurse, who had said neither "that seems right" nor "that seems wrong" but had looked at Meredith with an expression Meredith had catalogued as *professional compassion with mild statistical agreement* — then the fine, running at fifty dollars a day for two hundred and forty days, would total twelve thousand dollars. If the review period overlapped, the fine continued during review, and the review resulted in denial (which it might, because the ARC had three members and Doug Whitmore chaired the ARC, because Doug Whitmore chaired everything, because Doug Whitmore had constructed a system in which removal from Doug Whitmore required the approval of Doug Whitmore), then the fine would continue past the review period, and she would need to reapply or appeal, and the appeal process was — she was certain of this without checking — also chaired by Doug Whitmore.

Twelve thousand dollars was not going to bankrupt them. It was not going to cascade into foreclosure, into homelessness, into any of the dramatic financial dooms she had spent her career modeling for other people. Their savings were adequate. Richard's pension was decent. She had planned well, because planning well was the thing she did.

But twelve thousand dollars was Richard's remaining expected lifetime multiplied by the daily rate of a petty man's authority, and when she thought of it that way — as a product of those two variables, her husband's dying and another man's rule-following — something happened in her chest that was not grief and was not anger and was not anything she had a column for. It was the sensation of looking at a spreadsheet and realizing that the formula was correct and the inputs were correct and the output was *monstrous* and there was no cell to highlight, no error to trace, because the monstrousness was not a bug. It was a feature. The system was working exactly as designed.

She went inside. She put the water bill on the kitchen counter. She put the laminated notice on the kitchen table. She sat down across from it as though it were a person she intended to negotiate with. Richard, in the next room, said something she didn't catch. "Just a minute," she said.

She looked at the notice for a long time.

Then she opened her laptop and navigated to the Greenridge Commons HOA website, which had last been updated in 2019 and which still featured a banner advertising the community Fourth of July barbecue from that year. She clicked on "Governing Documents." She downloaded the CC&Rs. She downloaded the bylaws. She downloaded the architectural guidelines, the meeting minutes from the past six years, and the financial disclosures from the past three, which were the only ones posted.

She read all of them.

It took her the rest of the afternoon and most of the evening, and when she was finished, she made a cup of tea and sat in the kitchen in the dark, because Richard was asleep and the house was quiet and the elm outside was doing the thing it did in September, dropping leaves with a kind of stately resignation that she had always admired and now found nearly unbearable.

She was not going to write a letter. She was not going to attend a board meeting and make a tearful public comment. She was not going to start a petition or post on the neighborhood Facebook group that she did not belong to or call the local news. She was not going to do any of the things that the system was designed to absorb, process, and excrete without changing.

She was going to read the bylaws again. And then she was going to do something with them.

## II.

Linda Okafor had been a court stenographer for twenty-three years, which meant she had spent twenty-three years turning human speech into an official record and had discovered, through this process, that official records were largely fiction.

Not fiction in the sense of fabrication. Every word was captured. Every syllable was rendered into the clean Courier font of the transcript with the impartiality of a seismograph. The fiction was in what the transcript *excluded*: the pauses that meant someone was constructing a lie in real time, the vocal fry that indicated rehearsal, the micro-expressions of attorneys who knew their witnesses were perjuring themselves and had decided, through some private calculus of duty and ambition, to find this acceptable. Linda heard all of it. The transcript recorded none of it. And for twenty-three years, she had considered this a necessary limitation of the form — the way a photograph necessarily excludes the photographer's feelings about the light.

Then came the Gessler deposition.

Gessler Fabrication and Coating, Inc. A workplace injury case. A man named DeShaun Williams had lost three fingers to an insufficiently guarded stamping press, and the company's safety director had taken the stand to explain that all OSHA protocols had been followed. Linda had watched the safety director speak. She had watched him produce documents. She had watched him describe, with the measured fluency of someone who had been coached for exactly this performance, a training regimen that existed on paper and nowhere else. She knew he was lying. She knew it the way a sommelier knows a wine is corked — not through any single datum but through the entire gestalt, the composite signal of a human being producing sounds that did not match the shape of truth.

And in the transcript, between the safety director's answer about lockout/tagout procedures and the plaintiff's attorney's follow-up question, Linda had typed:

> [Witness is clearly lying.]

She had not planned to do it. She had not weighed the consequences. She had simply reached a point — and she would later describe this to Meredith, sitting on Meredith's couch, holding a mug of tea, with the bewildered clarity of someone recounting a religious experience — where the distance between what was happening in the room and what was appearing on the page became a physical sensation, a pressure behind her eyes, and her fingers had moved, and the words had appeared, and she had looked at them and thought: *Yes. That's the record now.*

The transcript was filed. The transcript was read. The transcript occasioned a judicial inquiry, a mistrial motion, a disciplinary hearing, and, ultimately, a decertification. Linda lost her license in April. By June, she was doing freelance closed captioning for a streaming service, typing [DRAMATIC MUSIC INTENSIFIES] forty times a week, and trying to determine whether she felt remorse.

She did not feel remorse.

She felt something else — an unfulfilled certainty that she had been *right*, that the record *should* include the lie, that a transcript without interpretation was not neutrality but complicity — and this feeling had no outlet, no professional application, no community of practice. She attended no support groups for disgraced stenographers. There were none. She was, as far as she knew, the only person who had ever been fired from court reporting for the crime of editorial accuracy.

She was also Meredith Chen's neighbor. Unit 22, across the cul-de-sac and two doors down. They had not been close. They had been the kind of neighbors who waved from driveways, who accepted packages for each other during vacations, who had once shared a pleasant twenty-minute conversation at the 2018 block party about the difficulty of finding a reliable auto mechanic. Linda had noticed the ramp. She had noticed the laminated notice. She had noticed Meredith standing at the mailbox, holding the notice, not moving, for what Linda estimated was four full minutes. She had not intervened, because she did not yet know that intervention was what she was for.

Then Meredith knocked on her door.

It was a Tuesday evening. Linda was captioning a reality dating show. On the screen, a man with geometric facial hair was explaining that he wasn't here to make friends. Linda paused the video. She opened the door. Meredith was standing on the porch holding a manila folder and wearing the expression of a woman who had made a decision and was now executing it with the calm, sequential efficiency of someone following an actuarial table to its conclusion.

"Linda," Meredith said. "I need someone who can take minutes."

"Minutes of what?"

"The annual meeting. October fifteenth."

"The HOA already has someone who takes minutes."

"The HOA has Doug Whitmore's wife, who takes minutes that Doug Whitmore reviews and edits before they are posted to the website. I need someone who will take minutes that are *accurate.*"

Linda looked at Meredith. Meredith looked back. Between them, unspoken but structurally present, was the entire history of Linda's career — the twenty-three years of fidelity to the record, the one act of infidelity that was actually the most faithful thing she'd ever done, the decertification, the [DRAMATIC MUSIC INTENSIFIES].

"I'm not a stenographer anymore," Linda said.

"I know," Meredith said. "That's why I want you."

Linda looked at the manila folder. "What's in there?"

"The bylaws. The CC&Rs. The last six years of meeting minutes as posted. And a list of seventeen discrepancies between what the minutes describe and what actually happened, as reported to me by four people who attended the meetings in question."

"Seventeen discrepancies."

"Some are substantive. Some are procedural. Three involve votes that the minutes record as unanimous that at least two attendees recall as contested."

Linda took the folder. She opened it. She read the first page. She read the second page. On the third page, Meredith had created a table — a simple, devastating table — with three columns: *What the minutes say*, *What attendees recall*, and *Structural consequence*. The third column noted, for each discrepancy, the specific decision that had been ratified or the specific objection that had been erased by the altered record.

Linda closed the folder.

"When do we start?" she said.
