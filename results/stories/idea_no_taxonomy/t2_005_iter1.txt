# The Closed City

## I.

The two clocks on the wall of Corridor Six disagreed by eleven minutes, and Zoya Konstantinovna noted this the way she noted everything in the city that did not officially exist: precisely, silently, and without any expectation that anyone would fix it.

The clock on the left — round-faced, manufactured in Chelyabinsk, bolted to the wall with military-standard brackets — read 7:49. The clock on the right — square, manufactured in Sverdlovsk, hung on a civilian-specification hook — read 7:38. Each had been set by a different authority and each was serviced by a different maintenance schedule, and somewhere in Moscow two deputy ministers had presumably each signed an order that their clock, and their clock alone, should govern the shift rotations for the workers of Installation K-31. The result was that every morning, for eleven minutes, half the city was asleep while the other half was already irradiated and eating breakfast.

Zoya adjusted neither clock. She copied both times into her maintenance log, noted the discrepancy, and moved on. The log would pass through two review offices — one military, one ministry — and each would read only the entry that pertained to its own clock, and each would find its own clock satisfactory, and the log would be stamped and filed in two separate archives located in two separate basements of the same building, and neither archive would ever be cross-referenced against the other, because cross-referencing would require acknowledging the other archive's existence, which would require acknowledging the other directorate's jurisdiction, which would require a memorandum of cooperation that, as of the latest version of Administrative Circular 1178-bis, remained "under interministerial review" — a phrase that, in the vocabulary of Soviet administrative science, meant the same thing as "heat death of the universe," only less final.

She walked Corridor Six with her toolbox and her laminated work pass clipped to the breast pocket of coveralls that had been issued by the military side but laundered by the ministry side, a combination that nobody had authorized and nobody had forbidden, because nobody had imagined that a single body could accumulate obligations to both halves of a divided house. She was a Category D maintenance technician, the lowest grade that still permitted access to both zones, a classification originally designed for furnace-stokers and latrine-cleaners and anyone else whose function was too menial to weaponize. It was, in terms of security clearance, roughly equivalent to being a corridor. Things happened on either side of you, and nobody considered you a participant. This suited Zoya exactly.

The city had a name — or more precisely, it had a postal code, a grid reference, a set of administrative designations, and a colloquial handle that workers used when they thought they were among friends — but it did not appear on any published map. It sat in a valley between two ridgelines in the Kazakh steppe, forty kilometers from a town that did appear on maps but that served no function except to provide a plausible destination for the rail spur that actually terminated here. The valley was ideal. The soil drained well. The prevailing winds carried contamination toward nothing. And the two ridgelines created a natural perimeter that reduced the fencing requirement by forty percent, a fact that two separate committees in Moscow had independently calculated and independently claimed credit for in two separate reports to two separate ministers, which was, Zoya had come to understand, the foundational act of the entire catastrophe: the moment when dual jurisdiction had been not a bug but a selling point, because each ministry got to report a cost savings to its patron.

The perimeter road ran nine and a half kilometers around the installation, and on it stood two checkpoint gates.

The military gate — Gate North, painted the particular green that Soviet military procurement called "protective color number three" — was manned by Interior Ministry troops under the command of a captain named Volkov who had a trimmed beard and a copy of Lermontov's *A Hero of Our Time* permanently wedged in the cargo pocket of his trousers. Gate North processed military-side personnel, military-side supply vehicles, and military-side documentation, and it maintained a logbook that was collected every forty-eight hours by a courier who drove it to a regional military-intelligence office in a city that was itself semi-classified.

The ministry gate — Gate East, painted the grey that the Ministry of Medium Machine Building apparently considered its institutional color, though no one had ever seen this codified — stood fifty meters south along the same road. It was manned by ministry security staff, technically civilians, though they carried the same Makarov pistols and wore the same suspicious expressions. Gate East processed ministry-side personnel, ministry-side supply vehicles, and ministry-side documentation, and it maintained its own logbook, which was collected every seventy-two hours — a different interval, for reasons no one could reconstruct — by a different courier who drove it to a different office in a different semi-classified city.

Between the two gates, the perimeter road passed through a strip of gravel that belonged to neither jurisdiction. Zoya had walked this fifty-meter interval many times. It was, in a sense, the only free territory in the installation. On windy days the gravel made a sound like static, and she could stand there and be, for a moment, administratively invisible — a person between two systems of accounting, subtracted from both ledgers, present and uncounted.

She did not linger there now. She had twenty-three maintenance calls to complete before the military-side shift change at 15:00 — or the ministry-side shift change at 14:49 — and the order in which she completed them mattered, though not for the reasons her supervisors assumed.

Today's route took her through Building 12, a low concrete block that housed, on its left wing, a military communications office, and on its right wing, a ministry storage facility for "inert administrative materiel," which was the filing term for components that had once been part of nuclear devices and were now, depending on which side's paperwork you consulted, either safely decommissioned or awaiting reclassification. The building had one entrance and two fire-evacuation maps, each showing a different floor plan, because when the building was constructed in 1962 the military and the ministry had each submitted their own as-built drawings to their own archives and neither had reconciled them. According to the military map, Room 12-14 was a storage closet. According to the ministry map, Room 12-14 was a ventilation shaft. Room 12-14, in physical reality, was a small windowless space containing a desk, a broken chair, and eleven filing cabinets of ambiguous provenance. Zoya had been inside it. She was tasked with checking its radiator once a month.

She checked the radiator now. She noted its temperature, its valve condition, the presence of minor corrosion at the inlet fitting. She did not note the contents of the filing cabinets. She did not need to. She had read them weeks ago — methodically, one drawer per visit, wearing cotton gloves she brought from her quarters and removed before exiting. The cabinets contained what any room in this city might contain if two bureaucracies each believed it belonged to the other's housekeeping responsibilities and therefore neither cleaned it: twenty years of accumulated paper, misfiled or unfiled, carbon copies of requisition orders and transfer authorizations and inventory reconciliation reports that had been routed to a room that didn't, depending on which map you trusted, exist.

She finished the radiator. She closed the door. She walked to her next call.

The cafeterias — two of them, naturally, on opposite sides of the central lane — were already diverging on today's lunch. The military cafeteria was serving buckwheat kasha with stewed meat, according to the menu board visible through its window. The ministry cafeteria was serving millet porridge with fish. The city had one food-supply depot, located outside the perimeter and managed by a third bureaucratic entity — the regional consumer-services directorate — that delivered identical raw ingredients to both kitchens, which then prepared different meals according to different nutritional guidelines issued by different authorities. Zoya ate in whichever cafeteria's line was shorter. This was permitted. It was one of the few advantages of her classification, though "advantage" was generous language for the right to choose between two mediocre meals.

She was, in most visible respects, what her file said she was: a forty-three-year-old woman with a demotion on her record and a job calibrated to her disgrace. Her coveralls were too large. Her toolbox was the old model, pressed tin with a broken clasp she'd fixed with wire. She wore no jewelry except a plain wristwatch — she set it to the average of the two clocks, a private calibration that pleased her — and she kept her hair short, practical, close to the shape of her skull. People who noticed her at all, which was few, noticed the flatness with which she moved: unhurried, systematic, a woman who had reconciled herself to the perimeter of a small life.

She had been here five months.

In five months she had mapped the entire installation — not physically, which was trivial, but *administratively*, which was the task that actually mattered and that no single person in the city's thirty-year history had apparently ever attempted, because no single person had ever had both access and motive. She knew which documents were routed through which offices. She knew which courier came on which day, which burn bags were collected promptly and which sat in hallways for hours, which supervisors checked logs and which only pretended to. She knew the military authentication-code registry was housed in a safe in Building 3 whose combination was changed quarterly — in January, April, July, and October — and that the ministry's parallel registry was housed in Building 9 and changed on a different cycle — March, June, September, December — and that for two weeks every quarter, both safes were simultaneously at their oldest, weakest combinations, because no one coordinated the rotation. She knew that the city's warhead inventory existed in two separate counts maintained by two separate custodial officers who met once a year in a perfunctory reconciliation session that involved comparing totals but not serial numbers, because serial-number comparison would require both sides to declassify their individual tracking formats, and declassification required approval from Moscow, and Moscow had not responded to the most recent request, which had been filed in 1979.

She knew that the city held forty-one warheads according to the military count and forty-three according to the ministry count, and she knew that neither number was wrong, exactly, because each side counted slightly differently — the military counted warheads-in-housing as single units, while the ministry counted warheads-and-housings as two line items — and the discrepancy had been noted in the annual reconciliation minutes as "a formatting variance under review."

She knew all of this, and she wrote none of it in her maintenance logs. She wrote down the temperatures of radiators.

The evening was cold and dry and the steppe wind came over the ridgeline and pressed against the windows of Dormitory Block 4, which was military-side housing but contained, on its third floor, six apartments allocated to "interministerial support staff," which was the administrative euphemism for people like Zoya — people who fit no single column in anyone's organizational chart and so had been assigned to a physical space that was, like Room 12-14, a kind of jurisdictional remainder.

Zoya ate bread with tinned fish at her small table and looked at the photograph she kept in her toolbox, under the tray of wrenches, in a place where an inspector would have to disassemble the kit to find it. It was not hidden, exactly. It was just below the threshold of effort that anyone with her clearance category would ever warrant. The photograph showed a girl of about twelve, narrow-faced, with the particular gravity that children raised in closed cities acquire — knowing that the world exists beyond the fence but not having a single memory of it.

Katya was sixteen now. She lived in Dormitory Block 7, on the ministry side, in a room she shared with another dependent, a girl whose father had died of a lymphoma that the medical office had classified as "idiopathic" with the serene confidence of an institution that had never once, in three decades, found a cause of death it needed to attribute to radiation exposure. Katya attended the installation's school, which was a single building with two principals — one appointed by the military directorate's education office, one by the ministry's social-services division — who alternated days of authority in a schedule so baroque that students had learned to check, each morning, which principal's rules were in effect before deciding whether they were permitted to use the library after 15:00.

Zoya had seen Katya three times since arriving. Twice in the cafeteria — the ministry one, where Katya ate because her dormitory was on that side — and once on the central lane, walking with the other girl, the roommate. On each occasion, Katya had looked at her mother with an expression that Zoya had cataloged with the same diagnostic precision she applied to everything: recognition, wariness, and a carefully maintained blankness that said *not here, not now, I know you are here but I don't know what you are doing and I have learned not to ask questions in this city.*

On none of these occasions had they spoken beyond a greeting.

This was not estrangement. This was protocol. Zoya had written Katya a letter before arriving — sent through the civilian post, which was monitored, in language that would survive monitoring — explaining that she had been offered a position at the installation. That she would arrive in April. That she hoped they might see each other when duties permitted. She did not write: *I am coming to get you out.* She did not write: *I have spent eleven months studying the structural flaw that will let me dismantle this place.* She wrote: *I hear the millet porridge is better on the ministry side. I will have to confirm.*

Katya had not written back. This, too, was correct. Outgoing mail from dependents was reviewed by both directorates, and a daughter's enthusiastic reply to a newly arriving parent might prompt someone to note the coincidence, to flag the transfer request, to wonder why a disgraced technician had chosen a posting where her child happened to be held. Silence was the prudent move. Zoya would have expected nothing else from the girl she had raised — or rather, from the girl she had raised for twelve years before the girl's father, Zoya's ex-husband, had accepted a senior engineering position at the installation and taken Katya with him, and then had died in the third year of the posting, in an accident involving a crane and a loading bay that was genuinely accidental and genuinely unrelated to nuclear anything, leaving Katya classified as an orphaned dependent, a category that entitled her to housing, education, and medical care within the installation but not, under any provision of the relevant statutes, to departure. A dependent's exit required the termination of the sponsoring parent's assignment or the decommissioning of the site. Katya's sponsoring parent was dead. The site showed no signs of decommissioning.

So Zoya had gotten herself assigned here.

This was the fact that she returned to every evening, at the small table, after the bread and fish: that the simplest thing in the world — a mother retrieving her child — was, inside this particular arrangement of fences and paperwork and overlapping jurisdictions, a thing so impossible that the only path to it ran through the controlled demolition of an entire administrative structure.

She wrapped the photograph in its cloth. She returned it to the toolbox. She reviewed, from memory, the next day's route.

## II.

The relevant facts about Zoya's disgrace were these:

She had been, for nine years, a senior technician in the reactor-design division of the Kurchatov Institute in Moscow. Her specialty was thermal-hydraulic modeling — the mathematics of how coolant flows through a reactor core, how heat distributes, where the tolerances thin. She was good. She was better than good; she was the kind of competent that in a functioning institution gets promoted and in a dysfunctional one gets assigned more work at the same rank, which is its own form of recognition. She had published seven internal technical reports. She had co-authored a classified monograph on emergency cooling that, four years after its completion, appeared almost verbatim in a ministry directive without attribution, which meant it had been read at the highest levels, which meant she was both valued and invisible, the ideal combination for a Soviet technical career.

Then she had filed a deficiency report.

The report concerned a valve assembly in a new reactor design — a specific component, a specific failure mode, a specific recommendation. The technical content was sound. Three colleagues who reviewed it privately agreed with its conclusions. The problem was jurisdictional: the valve assembly was manufactured by a division under military oversight, the reactor design was under ministry oversight, and Zoya's report, by identifying a flaw that crossed that boundary, had committed the bureaucratic equivalent of pointing out that the emperor's left hand did not know what his right hand was doing, in a system that had resolved this problem by declaring that the emperor had only one hand and it was whichever hand your directorate controlled.
