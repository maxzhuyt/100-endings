# The Calculus Beyond Mercy

## I.

The residential cluster numbers came in at 6:40 Europan Standard, same as every allocation cycle for twelve years. Solenne pulled them onto her display while her coffee was still too hot to drink and the recycler in the corner of her office ticked through its third filtration pass with the faint bronchial wheeze that meant the gasket seals were degrading again. She made a note about the gasket. She'd make the requisition after the cycle was done.

Cluster Seven was drawing fourteen percent above nominal. That was the first number that mattered. She expanded the population register: 206 residents, up from 191 at last audit. Fifteen new births and transfers, mostly young families relocated from Cluster Three during the pressure-loss remediation. They'd brought their respiratory profiles with them. Infants under six months metabolized less but required higher O₂ partial pressures for neural development. The mothers' metabolic rates were elevated postpartum. She could see the draw increasing further over the next sixty days unless she rebalanced.

The colony's total atmospheric synthesis capacity was what it had been for three years: 4.12 million liters per cycle, plus or minus two percent depending on electrolyzer efficiency and ice-feed purity. The number did not grow because someone needed it to grow. The ice-bore teams had petitioned for a fourth extraction shaft in the last colony session, and the petition had been tabled for the sixth time because shaft construction required diverting structural engineers from habitat maintenance, and the south residential ring's pressure integrity was already below the threshold where Solenne would start losing sleep, which was a lower threshold than the one printed in the emergency manual.

She did not think any of this with bitterness. Bitterness would have required the belief that another arrangement was possible. She had run the models. She had run them under optimistic assumptions and pessimistic assumptions and assumptions so conservative they amounted to fantasy. The arithmetic always resolved to the same shape: there was not enough. There was never enough. The only question, the question that had been hers for twelve years, was the distribution of not-enough across 2,407 human beings living in a pressurized architecture on an ice moon 628 million kilometers from the nearest open sky.

She pulled up the allocation matrix. Cluster Seven's fourteen percent overage had to come from somewhere. She could shave two percent from the commercial district during off-hours — the fabrication workshops were running a reduced schedule this month, and the atmospheric load when the furnaces were cold was negligible. That gave her a fraction. She could reroute a portion of the emergency reserve in Corridor Nine, which had been holding a full-pressure buffer since the micrometeorite scare eight months ago with no structural justification beyond the maintenance chief's anxiety. She made a note to speak with Dahl about that. He would resist. He always did. He kept his corridors pressurized the way some people kept food they'd never eat.

That still left a gap. Not a large one. Four-tenths of a percent of total output, which sounded like a rounding error until you translated it into respiratory capacity for approximately nine people at full allocation.

She opened the medical registry for Cluster Four. The geriatric ward had eleven residents on palliative status — patients whose conditions were irreversible and whose physicians had filed terminal prognosis reports within the last ninety days. Under standard allocation protocol, palliative patients drew full oxygen provision unless a rebalancing flag was raised, at which point the attending physician could authorize a diminished-flow regimen. The diminished regimen was not dangerous. It was not comfortable. The patients would experience intermittent dizziness, mild cognitive fog, shortened breath during exertion. For patients who were bedridden and terminal, exertion meant attempting to sit up. The fog meant they would sleep more, and the sleep would be deeper, and their families would sometimes mistake the deeper sleep for improvement.

Solenne flagged seven of the eleven. She chose them on the basis of their prognosis reports, their respiratory reserves, and — this was the part that had taken her years to learn to do without the specific, physical sensation of something tearing behind her sternum — their capacity to suffer from the reduction. Maret Halle, eighty-one, advanced neural degradation, largely insensate. Tomek Pava, seventy-seven, hepatic failure, sedated twenty hours per cycle. Anneke Soor, sixty-eight, earlier than the others, but her respiratory function was already so compromised that the difference between full and diminished allocation was, medically, less than the variance caused by her own labored breathing.

She flagged the seven. She cross-referenced to confirm that none of their attending physicians had submitted objection flags. She checked the family notification status — families were notified of palliative reclassification as a medical decision, which it was, technically, because the attending physician had to sign the authorization. The families were not notified that the reclassification was downstream of an atmospheric allocation decision. They were not notified because there was no protocol requiring it, and there was no protocol requiring it because the protocol had been written by the Resource Allocation Office, and the Resource Allocation Office was Solenne.

The recycler ticked. Her coffee was drinkable now. She drank it while she ran the final numbers. The four-tenths-percent gap closed. Cluster Seven's infants would receive their neural-development allocation. The seven palliative patients in Cluster Four would breathe less. The families of those patients might notice a slight change in breathing rhythm during their visits, or they might not. The attending physicians would note the diminished-flow authorization in the medical file with the same clerical dispassion as a dosage adjustment.

Solenne opened the ledger.

It was a personal file, encrypted under her administrative credentials, stored on a local drive rather than the colony's shared system. She had started it in her second year, when she'd realized that the official allocation logs recorded outcomes — how much went where — but not the reasoning chain that produced them. The official logs could tell you that Cluster Four's palliative ward drew 0.4 percent less oxygen this cycle. They could not tell you that Solenne had chosen those seven patients, by name, over three other potential adjustment points, for specific and defensible reasons. The official system had no field for *why this person and not that one*. So she'd built the field herself.

She entered the date, the cycle number, the rebalancing parameters. She entered the names: Halle, M. Pava, T. Soor, A. Kindt, R. Lus, B. Odem, D. Vasil, Y. She entered the projected allocation reduction per patient in liters per hour. She entered the projected duration of the diminished-flow period, which was sixty days barring a change in colony extraction capacity or a reduction in Cluster Seven's draw. She entered the cross-referenced justification codes, which she had invented herself, a private taxonomy that mapped her reasoning: P-PROG for prognosis-weighted, R-CAP for respiratory capacity, S-THRESH for suffering threshold.

She did not enter: *I'm sorry.* She did not enter: *this is necessary.* She did not enter anything that was not a fact or a traceable judgment. The ledger was not a confession and not a justification. It was a record. It was the only record anywhere in the colony that contained, in one place, the full causal chain from atmospheric constraint to individual human consequence. She maintained it because someone should know. Because the decisions should exist somewhere in their true shape, not dissolved into the sanitizing bureaucracy of medical authorizations and allocation metrics that let everyone—the physicians, the administrators, the families, the 2,407 breathing colonists—not know what was actually happening.

She saved the file. She closed it. She finished her coffee and drafted the requisition for the recycler gaskets, which she submitted to Maintenance with a priority-two flag, and then she opened the next item in her queue, which was a heat-exchange dispute between Clusters Two and Five that had been festering for a month and would require her to review thermal distribution data for the better part of the morning.

Her office was in the administrative core of the colony's central axis, a pressurized cylinder where the atmospheric feed was always slightly positive—a structural feature, not a privilege, because the administrative core housed the colony's computational systems and the machines needed stable pressure more than the people did. The hiss of the feed was constant. She had long ago stopped hearing it the way she'd stopped hearing her own breathing. Outside her sealed porthole, Europa's ice field stretched to a horizon so close it looked wrong, a curvature that the human eye processed as the top of a hill rather than the edge of a world. Jupiter hung where it always hung, filling a quarter of the black sky with its banded, indifferent mass.

Somewhere in Cluster Seven, an infant drew its first full breath of the new cycle.

Somewhere in Cluster Four, Maret Halle slept a little deeper.

## II.

She recognized him before she placed the name. That was how it always happened — the body knew before the record caught up. She was walking the connecting corridor between the administrative core and Residential Ring North when a man came through the junction ahead of her and something in the architecture of his face, the way grief had reorganized it, made her step slow by half a pace. Not stop. Not falter. A half-pace deceleration, the kind a person makes when they see a step they almost missed.

His name was Kem Aurides. She had not seen him in six months, maybe longer. He was thinner. He had the specific thinness of a parent who has been awake too long — not emaciated but *used*, the fat burned off not by hunger but by the sustained metabolic cost of worry. His eyes were the problem. They were attentive in a way that most people's eyes on Europa were not. Most colonists had developed, over the decades, a kind of visual efficiency — they looked at what they needed to see and let the rest go soft, because the colony was small and repetitive and the brain learned to save energy on environments it already knew. Kem's eyes had not learned this. He was looking at the corridor as though he had never seen it, noting the pressure seals, the conduit runs, the color-coded atmospheric indicators above each hatch. He was looking at infrastructure.

She knew why. His daughter — Lira, age four, now five — had been on diminished allocation for fourteen months. Not through Solenne's current cycle; the flag had been raised in a previous rebalancing, when the Cluster Two pressure-loss event had required a system-wide redistribution and Solenne had pulled from the pediatric margins in Ring South. Lira Aurides had been flagged as a low-risk candidate for reduction because her respiratory profile, at the time, had shown above-average lung capacity for her age cohort. Above-average. Solenne remembered the number: 107 percent of predicted FVC. The child had strong lungs. Strong lungs meant she could tolerate a reduction that would have put a weaker child in the medical ward.

Solenne had entered the name in the ledger. Aurides, L. She had entered the code: R-CAP. She had entered the projected duration: ninety days.

The ninety days had extended. They always extended when the underlying constraint didn't change I. The Cluster Two pressure repairs had taken longer than projected. The extraction capacity hadn't improved. Lira Aurides had been on diminished flow for fourteen months, and her respiratory profile — Solenne had checked, because she always checked — had shifted. The 107 percent FVC was now 89 percent. The child's lungs, which had been strong enough to tolerate the reduction, had remodeled around the reduction. She was developing the shallow-breath architecture that the colony's medical staff called "adaptation" and that Solenne, in her private terminology, called what it was: damage.

Kem did not know any of this.

What Kem knew — what every parent in his cluster knew — was that his daughter sometimes turned blue at the fingertips after climbing a stairwell. That she slept with her mouth open. That she had been seen by the cluster physician three times in the past year for what the physician called "developmental respiratory variation," which was a phrase that meant nothing and was designed to mean nothing because the physician had signed the diminished-flow authorization and could not, within the colony's bureaucratic grammar, both authorize the reduction and diagnose its consequences.

"Allocator Dienne."

He had seen her. She had not been fast enough or, more accurately, had not tried to be fast. She did not avoid the families. Avoidance would have been an admission that there was something to avoid, and she had disciplined herself, over twelve years, against every form of flinching, including the spatial ones.

"Kem." She used his first name because she had met him before, at a residential planning session two years ago, when he had argued articulately and unsuccessfully for an expansion of the Ring South playground. He had been a different person then. Not lighter — that would be sentimental, and it would also be inaccurate. He'd had the same attentiveness, the same quality of precision in his observations that had made him effective in the planning session and that made him, now, in this corridor, something more than another grieving parent. He had been specific then, and he was specific now, and Solenne understood, with the pattern-recognition that twelve years of triage had honed to an instrument, that specificity in grief was the most dangerous thing a colony could produce.

"I submitted a reassignment request for Lira's residential unit," he said. "Dr. Kessler recommended a higher-pressure environment for her respiratory therapy. The request was forwarded to your office."

"I saw it." She had. The request was medically sound. Moving Lira to a higher-pressure residential cluster would functionally restore her to near-full allocation without requiring a rebalancing of the atmospheric matrix. It was the kind of request that Solenne would normally approve without hesitation, except that approving it required freeing a residential slot in a higher-pressure cluster, and the higher-pressure clusters were at capacity because everyone wanted to live where the air was better, and the waiting list was eleven months long unless Solenne flagged the transfer as medically urgent, which she could do, but which would create a paper trail linking a medical respiratory condition in a child to a residential pressure differential to an allocation decision, and that chain, if anyone ever followed it to its origin, would end at her ledger.

She approved the request that evening. She filed the medical urgency flag. She moved Lira Aurides up the waiting list. She did this because it was the right thing to do for the child, and because the alternative was to let a five-year-old's lungs continue to remodel around deprivation to protect the obscurity of a record that Solenne kept precisely so that the truth would not be obscure. The contradiction was not lost on her. She entered it in the ledger with her usual notation: the date, the action, the justification. She did not enter: *I owe this child.* Debt implied that the original decision had been a wrong. It had not been a wrong. It had been an allocation.

The transfer went through in nine days instead of eleven months. Kem Aurides, who noticed things, who followed threads, who had the kind of mind that did not accept "developmental respiratory variation" as an explanation for his daughter's blue fingertips, noticed that too.

She was in her office when the maintenance alert chimed — not the recycler this time but a notification that her local drive had been accessed from an external terminal. She stared at the notification for four seconds. The number four mattered because it was the interval between recognition and response, and in that interval the specific, controlled architecture of her thinking did something it had not done in twelve years of allocation cycles and diminished-flow authorizations and names entered in precise notation: it went white. Not blank. White, like a display overloaded, every pixel firing at once.

The access had originated from a terminal in Residential Ring South. The file accessed was the ledger.

Her encryption was administrative-grade, the highest available to a non-Council user. Breaking it required either a computational attack that would have taken months on colony hardware or the administrative credentials themselves. She checked the access log. The credentials used were hers. Copied, not stolen — she still had access. Someone had duplicated them.

She thought, immediately and with terrible clarity, of the residential transfer. The medical urgency flag she had filed for Lira Aurides had routed through two administrative systems: Medical and Housing. Both systems required her credentials for override authorization.
