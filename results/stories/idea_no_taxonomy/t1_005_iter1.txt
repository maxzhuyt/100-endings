## I.

Now, why would a man with two children under the age of six never put out his recycling? You already know. Somewhere in that house, the bins are being used for something else. Your kids. My kids. The water's warm and the door is closed and nobody asks where Coach Reimer takes the slow learners for their 'private sessions.' Nobody asks because asking means knowing and knowing means acting and acting means—well. You know what acting means. You wouldn't be listening if you didn't."

The voice paused. It was a good pause—not dead air, not hesitation, but the pause of someone setting down a glass, thinking, arriving at something reluctantly. You could hear the chair creak. You could almost hear the lips part again before the words came.

"Fourteen families on that block. You know them. They're your people. Dale Reimer is not your people. Dale Reimer is what happens when good people look away for eleven years. So I guess the question is: how long do you want to keep looking away?"

The broadcast ended with a tone—a low, warm chime, like a meditation bell, or the sound a church makes inside your memory when you haven't been in years.

By Tuesday evening the cars had begun to gather. Not all at once. First the white Dodge Ram that belonged to Harlan Pruitt, who parked it across the street with the headlights off and sat in the cab for forty minutes checking his phone. Then two more trucks. Then a sedan. Then people on foot, appearing from the cross streets like filings pulled toward a magnet's point. They didn't wear masks. They didn't carry signs. Some of them had baseball bats but held them low along their legs, the way you carry a bat when you're walking to the park, not the way you carry a bat when you're walking to a man's house. The difference was a matter of wrist angle. Anyone watching from a window could tell themselves they were seeing the first thing.

Inside the house on Oleander, someone turned off every light.

It didn't help.

The windows went at 9:47. Not all at once—the living room first, then the two small bedroom windows on the east side, one of which had a Paper Mario sticker on it that caught the streetlight as it fell inward. No one cheered. The sound of breaking glass was met with the kind of silence that follows a hymn's final note: reverent, purposeful, full of the shared conviction that what had just happened was not violence but *correction.* Harlan Pruitt, who'd served twenty-two months at Lorain for aggravated assault before he found the broadcast and started calling it his church, walked to the front door and knocked politely. The door had already been kicked in by someone else, but Harlan knocked on the frame, three times, the way his mother had taught him.

By midnight the Reimer family was loading a Kia Sorento with two suitcases and a garbage bag full of children's clothes. Dale's wife, Mary Beth, carried the six-year-old on her hip. The boy was asleep. His head rested against her collarbone and his lips were parted and he looked, to the neighbors watching from their porches, like a painting of innocence being carried away from its contaminator. That was the story, anyway. The neighbors believed this story because the broadcast had told it to them and the broadcast had never, in fourteen months of operation, been proven wrong—which was not the same as being right, but in a city where the Chrysler plant had closed and the municipal pool had drained and the school superintendent had been indicted for embezzlement, the difference between "never proven wrong" and "right" was a crack too thin for most people to see through.

The Reiners drove north on Route 58. They did not come back.

On Wednesday, the house on Oleander was quiet. A woman from three doors down left a casserole on the porch, the way you do after a death.

On Thursday, the broadcast ran again. A new name.

## II.

Elena Marsh heard her own name in a laundromat on Depot Street, seventeen days after her release from Marysville.

She was folding a fitted sheet, which is an act of faith or stubbornness depending on temperament, and the sheet was the color of a Band-Aid because it had been the cheapest one at the Goodwill on Fifth, and the laundromat was the SudSmart, which was warm and smelled of fabric softener and chlorine and which she came to at 10 p.m. on weekdays because at 10 p.m. on weekdays the SudSmart was empty. The condition of her parole was not that she avoid laundromats. The condition of her parole was, among other things, that she reside at her approved address, that she not associate with known felons, that she report to her parole officer every Thursday, that she submit to random urinalysis, and that she not leave Lorain County without written permission. But the feeling of her parole—the texture of it, the thing it did to her body when she stood in a public space—was that she should not be seen. That she was a thing that had been put away for good reason and was now out again and the world had not asked for her back.

The broadcast came from the phone of a man sitting on the plastic chairs by the vending machines. He was sixty or so, with a Carhartt jacket and a newspaper folded to the crossword, and he was listening the way people listen to sports radio: half-attending, occasionally grunting in agreement. The volume was up because his hearing was not good. The sound filled the laundromat the way sound fills a tiled space: completely, with no soft surface to absorb it, every word reflected and doubled.

"—Elena Marsh, forty-one years old, released October third from the Ohio Reformatory for Women on a three-to-five for a fraud conviction that, if you read the trial transcripts—and I have, I've got them right here, I'll post them tonight—was pled down from something much, much worse. Now, I want to be careful here. I want to be precise. Because what I'm going to tell you isn't speculation. It's pattern."

The fitted sheet went slack in her hands. One corner popped free and hung toward the floor like a flag lowered to half-mast.

"Elena Marsh was convicted of wire fraud in connection with a scheme to bilk the Lorain County Community Trust out of two hundred and sixteen thousand dollars earmarked for—listen to this—earmarked for *child nutrition programs.* She stole from children's mouths. That's on the record. That's what she pled to. But the Trust also administered funds for foster placement oversight, and if you look at who had access to placement records, who had signatory authority on the intake evaluations, whose name appears on sixteen separate foster home certifications that were later flagged—"

She set the sheet down. She did not fold it. She placed both hands flat on the folding table and pressed down as if the table might move.

"—you start to see a different picture. Not a fraudster. A *gatekeeper.* Someone who decided which children went where and who made very sure that certain homes stayed open despite complaints, despite incident reports, despite the kind of red flags that should have ended the whole operation years ago. Now, I'm not saying Elena Marsh is a predator. I'm saying she *enabled* predators. I'm saying she sold access to children for money and when she got caught, the system pled it down to fraud because the alternative was exposing the entire placement apparatus and every official who—"

The man in the Carhartt jacket looked up from his crossword. He looked at Elena. She understood, in the way that prey understands the geometry of a clearing, that her face was not yet known. The broadcast was new. The photo would be posted tonight. She had time that was measured not in days but in the speed at which an image propagates through a network of phones, and in this city, on these blocks, that speed was very fast.

She picked up the sheet. She left the rest of her laundry in the dryer. She walked out of the SudSmart and into the October dark and stood on the sidewalk for eleven seconds, which was the amount of time it took for the specific quality of cold air—Lake Erie cold, damp and mineral and uninterested in her—to convince her lungs to work again.

She had not stolen from child nutrition programs. She had stolen from the Trust, yes. Two hundred and sixteen thousand dollars, yes. But the mechanism had been check kiting between the Trust's operational accounts, and the target had been the administrative surplus, and no child had ever missed a meal. This was not a moral defense. She had stolen money. She had gone to prison. But the distance between "embezzled administrative funds through check kiting" and "sold access to children for money" was the distance between what she had done and what the broadcast needed her to have done, and that distance was exactly the space in which the lie could live and grow and become, by Thursday, a baseball bat held low against someone's leg.

She knew who had done it before she reached her sister's car.

She knew because the broadcast had mentioned the foster placement records, and there were only five people who could connect Elena's fraud conviction to the Trust's foster placement apparatus, and four of them were attorneys or officials who had no reason to reopen a closed case. The fifth was Marguerite, who had sat in the courtroom every day of the trial, who had heard the prosecutor's opening statement describe the Trust's functions in detail, who had taken notes in a leather journal she bought for the occasion, and who owned a house on Garfield Avenue that their mother had left to both of them.

The house on Garfield Avenue. Three bedrooms, vinyl siding the color of a robin's egg, a backyard that sloped down to a drainage ditch their father had spent every spring reinforcing with sandbags. Their mother had died in 2019 while Elena was in Marysville, and the will—handwritten, unwitnessed, a document that an estate attorney would later describe as "the kind of thing that keeps us in business"—left the house to "my girls, both, to share or decide." Elena's conviction had made it easy. Marguerite had moved in. Marguerite had replaced the furnace. Marguerite had painted the kitchen and torn out the carpet in the living room and found, beneath the carpet, oak floors that she refinished herself, on her knees, with a rented sander, over a three-day weekend. The house was Marguerite's now in every way that mattered except the law, and Elena, released, paroled, standing on the sidewalk outside the SudSmart with the taste of dryer sheets in her mouth, was the law's loose end.

She did not call Marguerite.

She did not call the police.

She sat in the borrowed Civic—it belonged to a woman from her reentry program, Donna, who asked no questions and charged no interest and had simply handed over the keys with the words "tank's half full, don't crash it"—and calculated. Today was Monday. The pattern, observed across fourteen months and at least eleven names she knew of, was consistent: the broadcast aired Monday evening; the photo and documents posted Monday night; the community digested Tuesday; the cars gathered Wednesday; by Thursday the family was packing or the windows were breaking or both.

Seventy-two hours.

She could run. Donna's Civic, half a tank, north on 90 toward Erie or east toward Ashtabula and then what. Parole violation. Warrant issued. Her face in a different database now, the official one, and when they found her—because they would find her, because she was not a person who had resources or connections or a plan beyond the next folded sheet—when they found her, the broadcast's narrative would be *confirmed.* She fled. Innocent people don't flee. The system validates itself.

She could go to her parole officer. Gerald Nuñez, who was overworked and underpaid and decent in the way that people are decent when they haven't yet been tested. She could sit in Gerald's office and explain that she'd been falsely targeted by an anonymous broadcast that accused her of facilitating child abuse, and Gerald would listen, and Gerald would make a note, and Gerald's note would enter a system that moved at the speed of bureaucracy while the mob moved at the speed of outrage. By the time anyone with authority even heard the name "The Lamplighter," her windows would already be broken.

She could go to the warehouse.

She knew about the warehouse the way everyone in the city knew about the warehouse—not as fact, but as rumor that had calcified into geography. The old Thatcher Stamping plant on the river, east of the rail yard, a hulk of corrugated steel and broken clerestory windows that had been "being redeveloped" for nine years according to a sign that no one had ever seen updated. People went in and didn't come out, or came out different, or were seen on the catwalks at dawn. It was where The Lamplighter lived, or broadcast from, or in some versions where The Lamplighter held court like a king of ruin. No one had ever gone in with the intention of challenging the operation and come back to report on it. This was not because such people had been harmed—or not only because of that—but because the kind of person who would walk into the Thatcher Stamping plant to confront a vigilante broadcast apparatus was not a kind of person that this city, in its current condition, produced in any quantity.

Elena was not that kind of person either. She was a person who had folded other people's hospital corners for twenty-seven months and who had learned, in that time, that the most dangerous thing about institutions was not their cruelty but their *logic*—the way each rule connected to the next rule and the next, forming a chain that was unbreakable not because any single link was strong but because the total length was so great that you could never find both ends at once.

But she had seventy-two hours, and she had no other door.

She drove east.

## III.

The Thatcher Stamping plant sat on the Vermilion River at the end of a road that the city had stopped servicing in 2016. The potholes were not so much holes as absences—places where the road had given up the pretense of being a road and returned to the gravel and clay it had always wanted to be. Elena drove slowly, the Civic's suspension protesting each crater, and the headlights caught chain-link fencing topped with razor wire that had been cut and re-tied and cut again, the wire curling in on itself like dead vines.

She parked behind a dumpster enclosure two hundred yards from the main structure. The plant was enormous—a quarter mile long, maybe, built in the decade when American manufacturing expressed its confidence in steel and square footage. The main building was flanked by outbuildings: a boiler house, a tool-and-die shed, something that might have been a workers' canteen. All of it dark. But not empty-dark. Dark the way a house is dark when someone inside has chosen not to turn on the lights.

The front entrance—a pair of steel doors wide enough to admit a truck—was visible from the road and, she noted, from a camera mounted on a telephone pole. Not a security camera in the commercial sense. A ring camera, the kind you'd put on a porch. Domestic surveillance repurposed for institutional use.

She circled.
