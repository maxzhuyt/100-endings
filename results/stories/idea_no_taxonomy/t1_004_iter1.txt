# The Erasure

## I.

The hospital visit was canceled because Mrs. Eddings died at 4:17 a.m. instead of lingering through the afternoon the way the hospice nurse had predicted, and so Pastor David Brennan came home at ten in the morning on a Tuesday in March carrying a sausage biscuit he'd bought for the drive and did not eat.

He came in through the garage because that was how he always came in. He set his keys on the hook by the dryer because that was where he always set them. He heard the shower running upstairs and thought nothing of it, then thought something of it, because Ellen showered at night. She had showered at night for the entirety of their marriage. She had once explained to him that morning showers felt like starting the day already behind, and he had found this so perfectly, irreducibly her that he'd written it in a sermon about the small liturgies of domestic life.

The shower was running at 10:08 on a Tuesday morning.

He stood in the kitchen. He set the biscuit on the counter. He did not go upstairs.

He noticed: two coffee mugs in the sink. His own sat on the drying rack where he'd left it at six. These were different mugs. One was the blue stoneware Ellen used. The other was the tall white one they kept in the back of the cabinet, reserved for guests who never came, because who visits at an hour that requires coffee?

He walked through the living room. The couch pillows were displaced, not from sitting but from the particular compression of weight that the cushions took when someone lay fully along them. He knew this shape because he made it sometimes on Saturday afternoons, and Ellen would tease him for it, and he would promise to fluff them before company arrived, and she would say *what company*, and they would both laugh at the smallness of their social world. But the impression was on the wrong side—the side nearest the window, not the television. David always faced the television. He didn't watch it. He just faced it. The window side was for people who wanted to watch the street.

He stood very still.

He was a man for whom stillness was professional. He had stood at seventeen bedsides while the monitors went flat. He had absorbed, over twenty-three years of ministry, more confessions than a drain. He knew how to hold himself in the center of terrible information and wait for the information to settle, to declare its own shape, the way a congregation would eventually tell you what it needed if you just stopped talking long enough.

He went upstairs. The shower was still running. The bedroom door was open.

The bed was made. Ellen always made the bed. But she made it with the quilt pulled tight and the pillows stacked—two his, two hers, his on the left—and what he saw now was a bed that had been made *again*, a second time, by someone who knew the general principle but not the specific ritual. The fold was wrong. The pillows were right but the cases were turned so the openings faced the center of the bed instead of the outer edges. A small thing. The kind of thing a person might do if they were remaking a bed they had recently unmade and were working from a visual memory rather than from habit.

On the nightstand—his nightstand, the left one—a watch.

David wore a watch. A silver Seiko his father had given both sons on their eighteenth birthday, identical watches from a two-pack at Costco, which their father had chosen because he believed luxury was a sin but punctuality was next to godliness. David's watch was on his wrist. He was wearing it. He lifted his arm and looked at it and confirmed that he existed, that his arm was his arm, that time was moving.

The watch on the nightstand was the same model, the same color, the same face. But the clasp—he picked it up before he could stop himself—the clasp had a scratch along its hinge, a deep lateral scrape that he had seen a hundred times across Thanksgiving tables and at their mother's funeral and in the front pew where his brother occasionally sat like a man auditing someone else's faith. The scratch was from a college landscaping job, a riding mower, a piece of gravel thrown against the wrist. His brother had told and retold the story—*almost lost the whole hand, Davey*—with the precise degree of embellishment that was his signature.

Nathan's watch. On David's nightstand. On a Tuesday.

He put it down. He repositioned it exactly as he'd found it. He left the bedroom and went back downstairs and sat at the kitchen table and began the work of reading backward through his own life, and every page he turned said something different than what it had said that morning.

---

The time his daughter Lily, seven years old at the kitchen table, looked at him over her cereal and said, "You're being nice again." He'd taken it as a child's arbitrary observation. Children said strange things. He'd smiled and poured her juice and forgotten it within the hour. But *again* was a word that implied a pattern, a departure and a return, and seven-year-olds did not use temporal qualifiers carelessly. They had not yet learned to be imprecise.

The conference in April, three years ago, Columbus, two nights. He'd come home on Sunday afternoon and Ellen had been light, almost giddy, and he'd attributed it to the relief of having him back, the return to routine. She'd made his favorite dinner. She'd been—he searched for the word now as though performing an autopsy—*grateful*. Not the gratitude of missing someone. The gratitude of something concluded successfully.

The anniversary dinner. Their fifteenth. He'd been late from a vestry meeting and had come home to find Ellen already dressed, already finished with the story of how the dinner had been lovely. "We went to Carmela's," she said, and described the pasta, the candle on the table, the compliment the waiter had given her dress. David had thought: *She went alone. She went alone and is pretending I was there because she doesn't want me to feel guilty.* He had felt tenderness for her. He had loved, specifically, what he believed was her willingness to protect him from his own failures of attention.

Tom Grady at the hardware store: "Good talk last week, Pastor. Really needed to hear that." David had nodded, assumed he meant the Sunday sermon, moved on. But Tom came on Sundays irregularly and was usually asleep by the second hymn. *Last week* might have meant a conversation David never had, in a tone David never used, with a warmth Tom had perhaps never felt from him, because David—he could admit this to himself now, sitting at the table with the full weight of it—David was not a warm man. He was a kind man. He was a patient man. He was a man who had built a life on the scaffolding of duty and had confused the scaffold for the building. But warmth was not his gift. Warmth was Nathan's gift. Nathan, who carried it like a lantern, who could walk into a room and make every person in it feel chosen.

The parishioners who referenced conversations he didn't remember. Not one or two. *Dozens*, across years, and each time he'd chalked it up to his own distracted mind, his tendency to operate on autopilot during the unstructured social hours after services, his fundamental inadequacy at the part of ministry that required him to be a person rather than a pastor. He had *apologized* for these lapses. He had prayed about his inattentiveness. He had added "be more present" to a list of personal failings he revisited each Lent like a man returning to the same wound.

The way Ellen sometimes flinched—*flinched* was too strong—*recalibrated* when he touched her. The slight stiffening when his hand found the small of her back, as though she'd been prepared for a hand arriving from a slightly different angle, at a slightly different pressure, with a slightly different intent. He'd thought it was the ordinary cooling of a long marriage. He'd thought it was something he'd failed to maintain. He'd read a book about love languages and concluded he was illiterate in hers and had tried, periodically and without success, to speak a dialect he couldn't hear.

He understood now. He had been close-reading his marriage for twenty years and congratulating himself on his sensitivity, not realizing that the text had a co-author.

---

The shower stopped. He heard Ellen's footsteps above him, and beneath them, or perhaps imagined beneath them, the cadence of a second set of feet stepping carefully toward the back staircase. The one that led to the garage. Where David's car now sat instead of being at the hospital where it should have been.

He heard the garage door open and close. A car engine. Not his car. A car parked on the street.

Ellen came downstairs in her robe. Her hair was wet. She saw him and stopped.

"You're home," she said.

It was a sentence so ordinary it could have meant nothing. But her intonation placed the stress on *home*, and David heard in it—he could not stop hearing things now, every word a palimpsest—the faintest trace of a question. Not *Why are you home?* but *Which one of you is home?* The microsecond of calibration before her face settled into the correct configuration of a wife seeing her husband.

"Mrs. Eddings died," he said.

"Oh." Ellen pressed her hand to her chest. "Oh, that's sad. I'm sorry."

She was sorry. She was genuinely sorry. He could see it. And beneath it, moving behind her eyes like a fish under ice, the calculation: *Does he know? What did he see? How long has he been sitting here?*

"There's coffee in the pot," she said, and turned to go back upstairs, and he let her go, because he recognized—with the instinct of a man who had spent his entire adult life reading faces in pews—that he was not ready for the conversation that would follow the one question he wanted to ask, and that the question, once spoken, could not be unspoken.

He sat at the table for another forty minutes. He ate the sausage biscuit. It was cold and tasted like nothing. Then he drove to the church, because the church was the only place he had ever felt entirely like himself, and he needed to feel like himself now more than he had ever needed anything, more than he had needed his calling, more than he had needed his wife's love, more than he had needed God, whom he addressed silently from the parking lot with the single word *Please* and received no answer, which was not unusual, because God had always spoken to David in silences that David had learned to interpret as assent, but which now, in light of everything, might have been simply silence.

## II.

Four days. He waited four days. He carried the knowledge inside his body like a stone in the stomach, and during those four days he performed his life with the precision of a man who knows he is being watched—though by whom, and from where, he could not have said. He preached on Sunday. He visited the nursing home on Monday. He led the men's Bible study on Tuesday. He did all of this while holding, in a sealed chamber behind his sternum, the image of his brother's watch on his nightstand and his wife's wet hair and the pillow dented on both sides and the twenty years of small incoherences that now cohered into a single, unbearable clarity.

He did not confront Ellen. He did not call Nathan. He was waiting, he told himself, for wisdom. He was waiting for the right words, the pastoral words, the words that would open the wound cleanly and allow it to drain. He had counseled parishioners through infidelity a dozen times. He knew the vocabulary. He knew the stages. He knew the difference between a confrontation that heals and a confrontation that merely detonates.

But perhaps—and he would admit this only later, only to himself, only in the dark—he was also waiting because the knowledge, terrible as it was, still belonged to him, and the moment he spoke it aloud, it would belong to everyone, and what happened next would be out of his hands. There is a species of control in being the only one who knows the truth. He had learned this from the confessional. The person who holds the secret holds the power. For four days, David Brennan held the power, and it made him sick, and he held it anyway.

On Wednesday evening—the evening of the midweek service he did not recall scheduling—he pulled into the church parking lot and found it full.

Every space occupied. Cars lining the grass shoulder along Route 14. The Harrisons' truck. The Clemmons' van with the fish decal. Deacon Marshall's Lincoln. Board members, youth leaders, the women's auxiliary. Wednesday nights usually drew forty. The lot held eighty cars.

He sat in his own car and counted. He was a man who counted things: heads in pews, hymns on the board, beats in a silence before he resumed speaking. Counting was how he organized the world. Eighty-three vehicles. A full house. For a midweek service he had not planned, had not announced, had not been told about.

Through the glass doors of the narthex he could see people standing because the sanctuary was full. He could see the backs of heads. He could see the light from the can fixtures that he'd argued for when the building committee wanted chandeliers, because David believed church lighting should be democratic and even, no shadows, nothing hidden.

He walked in through the side entrance, the one that led past the Sunday school rooms and the kitchen and opened into the back of the sanctuary behind the last pew. He opened the door quietly, as he always did when arriving to his own services, because a pastor should not enter like a performer; a pastor should already be there when the people arrive, or he should arrive as one of them.

The sanctuary was full. Standing room only. And at the pulpit—his pulpit, the one he'd had built from reclaimed oak, the one he rested his hands on every Sunday with a familiarity bordering on prayer—stood his brother.

Nathan was weeping.

It was the kind of weeping that David recognized professionally and admired despite himself: not theatrical, not showy, but controlled in its uncontrolling. The tears came steadily. The voice broke at precise intervals. The hands gripped the pulpit's edges with a tremor that conveyed not weakness but the effort of a strong man holding himself together for the sake of others. It was, David understood immediately, a magnificent performance. It was also, possibly, not a performance at all. Nathan had always been able to feel things on command—not fake them, but *summon* them—and this was the quality that had made him, in their youth, the more beloved twin, the one strangers gravitated toward, the one their mother held a beat longer.

David stood at the back of his own church and listened to his brother tell the story of what had been done to him.

"I should have told you years ago," Nathan said. His voice caught, recovered. "I should have told you the first time it happened. But he's my brother—" The word *brother* cracked cleanly in half. "—and I kept believing he would stop. I kept believing the man I grew up with was still in there somewhere."

Nathan paused. He looked down at the pulpit. He seemed to gather himself. The silence was four beats long—David counted—and in those four beats the congregation leaned forward collectively, a single organism drawn toward the gravity of someone else's pain.

"My brother David," Nathan said, and the use of the name *David* delivered from *David's own pulpit* by a face identical to David's own face created a dislocation so complete that David, standing at the back, felt the floor shift beneath him as though the building had decided to reject him, "has been coming to my home. Has been—" Another break. A hand passing over the eyes. "Has been presenting himself to my wife as me. Using the fact that we—that we look the same. That we sound the same. To—"

He couldn't finish.

He didn't need to finish.

The congregation finished it for him. David could see it happen in real time: the intake of breath, the hands rising to mouths, Margaret Olsen clutching her husband's arm, Tom Grady's jaw working as though chewing something he couldn't swallow. The understanding moved through the room like a wave, and like a wave it was composed of individual particles—each person privately constructing the image Nathan had left incomplete, each person filling the silence with the specific horror their own imagination supplied, which was always worse than anything a speaker could say aloud.
