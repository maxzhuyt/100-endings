# The Amber Lure

## I.

The left wing struck first. It caught the ridgeline at an angle that the mountain did not negotiate, and the fuselage broke behind the cockpit in a sound that was less an explosion than a vast, percussive exhalation—the scream of aluminum deciding to become something other than a tube. Maren Eld was sitting in 14C with her seatbelt fastened and her tray table up and her eyes on the safety card she'd been studying with the academic compulsion she brought to everything, and then she was upside down in the dark with snow in her mouth, and the safety card's cheerful diagrams had no instructions for this.

She could feel her left arm. She could feel her right. She could feel her legs, which seemed important. She could not feel the seat anymore because the seat was somewhere else—behind her, maybe above her, geometrically rearranged by the same forces that had redistributed the aircraft across a quarter-mile of Arctic ridgeline.

She began to crawl toward a sound, because the sound was human, and because human sounds in the dark meant that something besides her had survived the transition from sky to ground.

The sound was a man screaming for someone named David.

She found him pinned under a section of overhead bin. His name turned out to be Frank Padilla, and the David he was screaming for turned out to be two rows back and not pinned but terribly, finally still. Frank was fifty-three, broad through the chest, bleeding from a scalp wound that painted the left side of his face with a dark that looked black in the absence of light. He stopped calling for David when Maren reached him. She did not have to tell him. He looked at her face and understood what it meant that she had come alone.

The cold was already inside the fuselage. Not creeping—rushing, filling the broken cabin like water flooding a hull, and Maren understood with the clarity that replaces shock if you are a certain kind of person that she had minutes, not hours, before the temperature killed what the impact had spared.

She helped Frank free his legs. Together they crawled through a rupture in the fuselage wall and out onto the ice.

The wind hit them like a living thing.

Later, after she had time to think, Maren would reconstruct the first hour in clean sequential order and conclude that every decision they made was correct. At the time, it was not sequential. It was a series of lurching acts committed by freezing people in the dark—dragging bodies, checking pulses, shouting names into the whiteout, sorting the living from the dead by the crude metric of who was still capable of shivering.

Seven. Out of forty-one passengers and four crew, seven people emerged from the wreckage able to stand.

Maren, because she had been lucky.

Frank Padilla, because he had been strong.

Yun Jia-Li, a structural engineer from Harbin who had been seated in the tail section, which broke away relatively intact, and who had a compound fracture of the left forearm that she had splinted herself with a piece of armrest and a strip torn from her jacket before anyone else reached her. She said almost nothing during the first hour. She worked steadily with her one good arm pulling supplies from the debris—blankets, a first-aid kit, someone's parka—and if she was in pain, she kept it off her face.

Konstantin Bruk, the only Russian in the group, a geophysicist who had been traveling to a conference in Longyearbyen and who therefore understood exactly how far they were from anything. He wore wire-rimmed glasses that were cracked across one lens and refused to remove them. His first words to Maren were: "What direction are we facing?" She did not know.

Tomasz Wójcik, a mountaineer from Kraków, who had a dislocated shoulder that Konstantin helped him reduce in a procedure that made Tomasz vomit and then laugh, high and wild and grateful. He was the youngest of them—twenty-six—and the most physically capable, even hurt. He was also the one who found the emergency radio in the cockpit wreckage and the one who confirmed, after eight minutes of trying, that it was dead.

Suki Abe, a retired schoolteacher from Sapporo who was sixty-seven years old and five feet tall and should have been the most vulnerable person on the ice, and who instead became, without any formal appointment, the person who kept count. She counted heads. She counted supplies. She counted minutes. She said: "We have perhaps ninety minutes before exposure becomes fatal for the weakest among us," and she said it calmly, as a teacher states a problem to a class, and Maren understood that Suki expected them to solve it.

Dr. Lena Haraldsen, an emergency-room physician from Tromsø who had a concussion and possibly cracked ribs and who nonetheless performed triage on the other six with the systematic efficiency of someone who has worked mass-casualty events before. She told them what they already suspected: no one was dying from their injuries tonight. The cold would get them first.

They gathered in the lee of the largest piece of fuselage, out of the direct wind, and took stock. The radio was dead. The emergency locator transmitter had been in the cockpit, which was crushed. They had no satellite phone. They had blankets, one medical kit, several liters of water that were already beginning to freeze, a handful of energy bars, and the clothes on their backs—winter clothes, because they had been flying to Svalbard, but not survival clothes, not clothes rated for standing still in a blizzard at forty below.

"If we stay here," Dr. Haraldsen said, her voice flat and medical, "we'll be dead within four hours. The wreckage isn't insulated. We can't build a fire—there's nothing to burn that wouldn't also burn us. The wind is going to keep dropping the effective temperature."

"Search and rescue will be looking for us," Frank said.

"They'll look for our last known position," Konstantin said. "Which is over the ocean. The storm blew us inland. They won't find the wreckage before morning, if then, and morning is—" He checked his watch, squinted through the cracked lens. "Fourteen hours away."

They were inside the polar night. There would be no dawn. What Konstantin meant was fourteen hours until the sun crested the horizon far enough to provide even the grey, preliminary light that passed for day at this latitude in December.

Fourteen hours was approximately ten hours longer than they had.

It was Tomasz who saw it first. He had climbed the highest piece of debris—a section of tail fin leaning against the ridge—to try to get a vantage point above the blowing snow. He came down fast, sliding on the ice, and his face, which had been tight and blanched with cold, had changed. Something had come into his expression that Maren did not identify immediately because it was so incongruent with their situation.

Hope.

"There," he said, and pointed southeast, into the wind.

They climbed, one by one, to the vantage point and looked. Maren was third. She braced herself against the tail fin, squinting through the gale, and for a long moment saw nothing but the undifferentiated white chaos of the storm. Then her eyes found it—or it found her eyes, because the color was so different from everything else in the landscape that the visual cortex locked onto it reflexively, the way the brain seizes on a face in a crowd.

Amber. A warm, deep amber, pulsing on the horizon like a heartbeat.

It was not flickering the way a fire flickers. It was cycling—brightening and dimming with a regularity that was almost metronomic. Three seconds bright, two seconds dim, three seconds bright. The kind of pattern a machine makes. A generator surging under load. A beacon on a timer.

"That's a building," Tomasz said. "That's electric light."

Konstantin looked longest. His cracked glasses caught the amber pulse and held it. "Research station," he said. "There are installations all over the Arctic—Norwegian, Russian, Canadian. Weather monitoring, geological survey. Some of them are automated. Some are manned."

"How far?" Dr. Haraldsen asked.

Tomasz squinted. "Hard to tell in this visibility. Two kilometers? Three?"

"Can we make three kilometers?" Maren asked, looking at Dr. Haraldsen.

The doctor looked at Suki, who was the oldest and smallest. She looked at Yun, who was standing with her splinted arm held against her chest and her good hand gripping a length of fuselage frame she was using as a walking stick. She looked at Frank, who was still bleeding from the scalp wound, and at Konstantin, whose breathing had a rasp she didn't like.

"We can make three kilometers," Dr. Haraldsen said, "or we can die here. Those are the options."

There was no argument. There was no vote. The logic was so clean it didn't require consensus—it simply *was*, the way gravity is, and they obeyed it the way bodies obey gravity: automatically, without deliberation, because the alternative is to remain still and be killed by the thing you're standing in.

They tied themselves together with seatbelt straps salvaged from the wreckage so that no one could fall behind and be lost in the whiteout. Tomasz took the lead because he was strongest and because he knew, from years of climbing in bad weather, how to navigate toward a fixed point when the ground and sky were the same color. Maren took the rear because someone needed to keep count, and Suki, who had been counting, was now between Frank and Dr. Haraldsen in the middle of the line, and was beginning to struggle.

They walked.

The blizzard swallowed them in forty steps. The wreckage disappeared behind them as completely as if it had never existed, and Maren understood that if the light ahead also disappeared, they were dead—there would be no finding their way back, no shelter to return to, nothing in any direction but ice and wind until they stopped walking and the cold finished its work.

The light did not disappear. It pulsed. Three seconds bright, two seconds dim. Steady as a heartbeat, steady as a machine, warm-hued and rhythmic, anchoring their navigation through the featureless white. Maren kept her eyes on it with a focus that was nearly devotional, and some part of her brain—the part that was not yet hypothermic, the part that still ran analyses unbidden—noted that the color was unusual for an electric light. Too warm. Too saturated. Not the bluish-white of LEDs or the flat yellow of sodium vapor but something richer, deeper, the color of honey held up to a candle, the color of late afternoon through amber glass. Unusual, but not impossible. Older installations used incandescent bulbs. The amber tint could be a filter, a housing, a quirk of aged wiring.

She filed the observation and kept walking, because filing it was all she could do with it, and walking was the only act that had survival value.

Twenty minutes in, Suki fell.

The line jerked. Maren felt it in her waist where the seatbelt strap was knotted. Frank was already kneeling beside the older woman, his hands under her arms, and Maren caught up in time to see Suki's face, which was the wrong color—grey beneath the wind-burn, slack in a way that suggested her body was making decisions her mind had not sanctioned.

"I'm fine," Suki said, and was not fine.

Dr. Haraldsen checked her pulse. "She's hypothermic. We need to move faster or we need to carry her."

"We can't move faster," Yun said. It was almost the first thing she'd said. Her voice was thin and tightly controlled, pain leaking through at the edges.

"Then we carry her," Tomasz said, and he did. He lifted Suki onto his back with his one good shoulder and his mountaineer's strength and he walked, and the line kept moving, and the light kept pulsing, and Maren counted steps because counting was something you could do with a brain that was beginning to agree with the cold that stopping would be simpler.

The light never seemed to get closer. It pulsed at the same apparent size, the same brightness, the same position on the horizon, and after forty minutes of walking, a suspicion began to form in the cognitive slush of Maren's hypothermic mind: that the light was not fixed, that it was moving away from them at the same rate they approached, that they were chasing something that could not be caught—

And then, between one step and the next, it was *there*.

Not on the horizon. Not distant and promising. Close, immediate, large—a wall of concrete resolving out of the white chaos, and the amber glow pouring from a series of narrow windows like the building was full of warm liquid, and a door, heavy steel, standing open against the snow.

Tomasz stopped so abruptly that the line compressed behind him. He stood at the threshold with Suki on his back and looked at the open door and the amber light beyond it and he said nothing, because there was nothing to say.

They went in.

The warmth hit Maren's face like a hand. She gasped—everyone gasped, a collective involuntary inhalation, the lungs expanding against air that was not trying to kill them for the first time in over an hour. The light enveloped them. Amber, rich, steady now and no longer pulsing, or perhaps pulsing so faintly that the difference between bright and dim was imperceptible from inside. The corridor was concrete, Soviet-utilitarian, broad enough for two people to walk abreast, and the amber glow came from panels set into the ceiling at regular intervals—emergency lighting, Maren thought, backup systems on a generator that had outlived the people who installed it.

Tomasz set Suki down gently against the corridor wall. Dr. Haraldsen was already working on her, rubbing her hands, checking her pupils. Frank leaned against the opposite wall and slid to the floor and put his face in his bloody hands and did not cry but did something adjacent to crying—a shuddering exhalation that contained all the weight of the last hour.

Konstantin closed the blast door behind them. It moved easily on its hinges—too easily, Maren thought, for a door that heavy, a door that old—and sealed with a deep, resonant thud that echoed down the corridor and faded into silence.

The wind stopped. The noise stopped. The killing cold stopped. In the amber warmth of the corridor, surrounded by concrete walls that Soviet engineers had built to withstand things more forceful than weather, the seven survivors of Flight 412 did the most human thing possible.

They felt grateful.

## II.

The facility was larger than Maren had expected. The entry corridor branched after thirty meters into a T-junction, and from there the layout proliferated—a central common area with bolted-down tables and benches, a kitchen with industrial fixtures, a series of small dormitory rooms with metal-frame cots and wool blankets folded with military precision at their feet, two bathrooms with nonworking fixtures, and, beyond a second set of blast doors propped open with a rusted fire extinguisher, a wing of laboratories.

They explored in pairs, as Dr. Haraldsen insisted—no one alone, no one out of shouting range. Maren went with Konstantin, because Konstantin could read the Cyrillic signage bolted to the walls at each junction, and because Maren needed information the way she needed warmth: constitutively, reflexively, as a condition of functioning.

"It's a research station," Konstantin confirmed, running his fingers over a placard beside the laboratory doors. "Ministry of Defense, I think—the designation is partially redacted. 'Arctic Applied Sciences Directorate.' I've never heard of it, but that doesn't mean much. The Soviets had hundreds of these installations. Many were classified. Many were simply forgotten when the funding disappeared."

"When was it abandoned?"
