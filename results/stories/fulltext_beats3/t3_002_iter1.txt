# The Last Watch of the Mara Celeste

I have hands that know when water is ready before the kettle sings. You learn this if you cook for twenty years on the water—the sound changes beneath the boil, a gathering hum that rises through the steel and into your palm where it rests on the handle. I mention this because people assume a ship's cook is someone who couldn't do anything else. They think galley, they think cans of things, they think slop. I could bone a mackerel in forty seconds. I could make a roux in a force seven that would hold its emulsion while the deck tilted thirty degrees to port. I could carry a full mug up a twelve-rung ladder in a typhoon and not spill a drop, and I did, the second night, when I brought Wen her tea.

The Mara Celeste was a sixty-eight-foot stern trawler, wooden-hulled, built in 1974 by Wen's grandfather in a boatyard outside Kaohsiung that no longer exists. He'd dragged her frame across the floor of that yard himself, or so Wen told it, though she told it rarely and only when she'd been drinking the cheap sorghum liquor she kept beneath the chart table in a bottle with no label. The boat had been through four refits. The last one, seven years ago, Wen had financed through the Cooperative Maritime Bank of Keelung, and the terms had been what terms always are when the collateral is something you love.

I came aboard in 2016. Wen needed a cook. I needed a berth. We were not friends in any way that word is commonly used. We did not discuss our lives before the boat. What we did was occupy the same small vessel in the South China Sea for three years, which is a different kind of knowing—the kind that lives in how a person moves through a doorway, or how long she takes to climb out of her bunk, or whether she salts her rice.

The typhoon was called Noru. It had been downgraded, then upgraded. We were running for Zuoying but the sea had other ideas, and by the second night the sea's ideas were the only ones that mattered. The barometric pressure had dropped to nine-fifty-six and was still falling. I remember this because I'd checked the barometer on my way to boil the water, the way you touch a wall in a dark hallway—not because it tells you anything useful, but because the gesture itself is a way of saying you are still in a place that has walls.

I boiled the water. I steeped the leaves—Wen drank only a particular oolong she bought in bulk from a shop on Qijin Island, and she was specific about the steeping time, which was three minutes, and this was the kind of specificity I respected, being specific myself about most things. I climbed the ladder. The ship was pitching in a confused cross-sea, the kind where there's no rhythm to lean into, where every third wave comes from a direction that feels personal. I held the mug close to my chest. I didn't spill.

The wheelhouse was dark except for the green glow of the radar and the compass light, which gave Wen's face a color like the underside of a leaf. She was standing at the wheel. I want to be precise about this: she was not fighting the wheel. In a sea like that, on a boat like ours, you fight. You wrestle the helm and the helm wrestles back and it is work, real work, the kind that empties you out by dawn. Wen was holding the wheel the way you hold a bannister at the top of a staircase—lightly, as if considering whether to descend. The stillness of her hands was the first thing I could not explain.

I set the tea on the console. The console was cluttered in the way of working boats: tide tables, a cracked VHF handset, a spool of monofilament someone had left there months ago. Beneath the corner of the navigation chart—the one she'd marked with our failed course to Zuoying—I could see the edges of several papers. They were on bank letterhead. I recognized the shade of blue. There were four or five of them, pinned under the chart's plastic cover, and their edges had gone soft with damp and curled upward like things trying to get the attention of someone who would not look down.

"Pressure's still dropping," I said.

"Yes," she said.

I stood there a moment longer. There was something I might have said. I've gone over this and I know there was, though I can no longer find the words that might have been, which is different from saying they didn't exist.

"Tea's there," I said.

"Thank you, Mei."

I went below. The galley was wreckage—a pot had broken free and rolled itself into the corner like a dog trying to hide during a firework show. I secured what could be secured. I wedged myself into the bench seat and closed my eyes, though I didn't sleep. Nobody slept through Noru. You just closed your eyes and let the motion move through you and waited for the motion to stop or for everything to stop together.

Two hours later—maybe three—I went back up. I don't know why. I've told myself I was checking on her, and that's true, but it's also true that I wanted to see if the tea was gone, because the tea being gone would have meant something ordinary had still happened in the world and ordinariness was what I needed from her and had always needed from her without knowing it until that night.

The mug was where I'd set it. The tea was cold and a skin had formed on its surface, thin and gray, the way a pond ices over at the edge. Wen was standing in exactly the same position. Her hands on the wheel. The green light on her face. I looked at the tea and I looked at her and something moved through me that I have spent the years since trying to name and cannot, though the closest I can come is this: it was the feeling of having carried something carefully up a ladder in a storm and set it down in exactly the right place and having it mean nothing.

I took the mug back to the galley and poured it out and washed it and set it in the drying rack and wedged a towel around it so it wouldn't shift. I did this with care. It did not require care.

We played cards every day at eleven. This was not my invention or hers—it was something that had started in the first week I came aboard, when there was a long passage and nothing to do and she'd appeared in the galley with a deck so worn the queens had no faces. We played Big Two, which she called by a name in Hokkien I could never pronounce correctly and she never corrected, though she'd smile—not quite at me, at the space near my ear where the mispronunciation lived. We'd played through three years of passages, of calm days and bad days and days I was seasick and days she was hard to be near. The game was the architecture of our time. Without it the hours would have been just hours.

The third morning the pumps failed. I knew because of the sound—or rather, because of the new sound inside the old one. The old sound was the storm, which by then had become so constant it was a kind of silence, the way a factory floor becomes silence to the people who work there. The new sound was water. Not the water outside, which was an abstraction, an everywhere—but water inside, water that had found its way through the hull and was now moving with its own intelligence through the compartments below the waterline. It moved in phrases. I don't know how else to say this. It would rush and then pause and then rush again against a bulkhead, and in the pauses there was a groan from the hull that sounded like a question asked in a language I almost knew. The wood was speaking. I understood that it was saying how it would end, and I understood this in the part of me that lives below language, in the part that knows when water is about to boil.

At eleven o'clock Wen came down to the galley.

She sat across from me in the bench seat. She'd changed her shirt. This detail has stayed with me with a clarity that shames the others: she had put on a clean shirt to play cards on a sinking boat. It was dark blue with a mended tear at the left shoulder where she'd caught it on a cleat the previous spring, and I'd sewn it for her with a needle and thread from the repair kit and she'd stood in the galley doorway holding still while I stitched it because taking it off would have meant admitting it mattered enough to repair carefully.

I dealt. We played.

She led with a pair of sevens. I answered with nines. The water spoke below us, that heaving conversational slosh against the forward bulkhead, and neither of us mentioned it. I can say now that we should have mentioned it. I can say now that there were words available—*the pumps, Wen, the pumps are gone, we should get on the radio, we should do the things that people do when the water is inside*—and that these words were not exotic or difficult and I had all of them in my mouth and swallowed every one. But I want to be accurate about what it was like in that galley. It was like this: we were playing cards and the game had rules and the rules still worked and the sevens were still less than the nines and the nines were still less than the queens and this system, this small numerical order, was a territory that had not yet been swallowed, and to break it would have been to let the water into the last dry room.

She played a two of spades, which in Big Two is the highest card. She played it on a hand she'd already won, which was wasteful, and I said so. I said, "You didn't need that." She said, "I know." We argued about this for perhaps a minute—whether it was strategically sound to spend your highest card on a hand already taken. This was an argument we had been having for three years. It was the argument itself that mattered. The argument was a room we could both live in.

She laid down her last cards and won. She always won. I am a good card player and she always won, and I never determined whether this was because she was better or because I let her, which is a thing I let myself not know.

She said, "I'm going up."

I said, "All right."

She stood. At the galley door she paused, and she looked at me, and the look lasted one second longer than any look she'd given me in three years and I have measured that second against every other unit of time in my life and it is the largest.

She went up. I sat with the cards in front of me. I could hear the water below and the storm above and between them the specific sound of Wen's boots on the ladder rungs. I should have stayed in the galley. I should have cleaned up the cards and stayed.

I followed her.

I followed her at a distance, which is itself a confession. I did not follow her the way you follow someone you intend to stop. I followed her the way you follow a procession—at the interval that says *I am here, I am witnessing, I will not interfere*. I came up through the companionway and the wind hit me and the rain hit me and the world was gray and horizontal and absurd, and through it I could see Wen at the wheel.

She had a length of line. Good line—ten-millimeter braided nylon, the orange kind we used for the outriggers. She was wrapping it around herself and the wheel post, and she was doing it well. She tied a bowline, then a second, then secured the running end with a series of half-hitches that would have held in any sea. Her hands were quick and sure. She was a woman who knew her knots the way I knew my knife work—not from study but from the body's own memory, which does not forget and does not hesitate and does not ask whether the task it is performing is the last.

I stood at the companionway hatch and watched her tie herself to the ship. The rain was between us. It was perhaps five meters.

I could say I shouted and the wind took my voice. I could say I moved toward her and the deck pitched and threw me back. I could say any number of things that would give my stillness a physical cause, a mechanical explanation, a reason that lives outside the space where guilt keeps its accounts. But I do not say these things because they did not happen. What happened is that I stood at the companionway hatch and I watched her and she knew I was watching. She looked at me once. She did not shake her head or wave me off or mouth any word. She looked at me, and the look was not permission and it was not a plea and it was not goodbye, or it was all of these at once, which means it was none of them, which means I am still standing at that hatch in the part of me that has not moved since.

I went back to the galley. I picked up the cards. I squared the deck and put it in the tin box where we kept it and closed the lid and put the box in the drawer and closed the drawer. The water below had risen to where I could hear it without listening for it—it was in the walls now, in the floor, the Mara Celeste slowly becoming the thing she'd been built to float on. I wiped the table with a rag. I hung the rag over the lip of the sink. These were the last things I did in order, and I did them in order because order was the last thing I had, and then the order was gone too and there was only the water and the listing and the strange long slide of a wooden boat lying down on its side like an animal that has decided to sleep.

---

I was in the water for eleven hours. A Coastguard cutter picked me up seventeen nautical miles southeast of Xiaoliuqiu. I had a life jacket because I had put it on in the galley after I put away the cards, which means part of me chose to survive at the exact moment another part of me chose not to stop what was happening, and I will not reconcile these two parts because they cannot be reconciled and the attempt would be a lie.

The investigators asked their questions. I answered with the facts that have shapes: the typhoon's bearing, the hull's age, the failure of the pumps, the bilge intake rate. They wrote these down. They did not ask about tea or cards or knots. They did not ask why the captain had not put out a distress call. Or they did ask, and I said I didn't know, which was true in the way that a locked door is true—it is a real barrier but it is not the absence of what's behind it.

The ship was never found. Sixty-eight feet of teak-on-frame trawler, and the sea closed over it the way a conversation closes over a silence—completely, leaving no scar on the surface. They declared it lost with all hands except one. I am the except.

What I have not said, because I've been building toward it the way you build toward the card you've held back, the two of spades you play when the hand is already won, is the last thing I saw.

The deck was awash. I don't mean waves were breaking over it—I mean the sea and the ship had become continuous, the boundary between them dissolved, so that standing on the Mara Celeste was the same as standing on the ocean that was eating it. I was at the rail, or where the rail had been. The life jacket was on. The next wave would take me or I would go into it myself—this distinction had collapsed along with everything else.

I looked at Wen.

She was lashed to the wheel post, the orange line bright against her dark blue shirt, and the water was at her waist. Her hair was plastered to her skull. Her hands were not on the wheel—there was no point anymore, no rudder response, no steerage, nothing to steer toward—and they hung at her sides, resting on the surface of the water that moved around her.

She was smiling.

I need to say what kind of smile, because that is all I have left and I have been trying to say it for six years and every word I choose is wrong. It was not brave. It was not resigned. It was not the grimace that people mistake for peace when they need the story to end well. It was—

She looked like a woman who had set something down. Something heavy, carried far, finally released. The lines from the bank were below her somewhere, in a wheelhouse that was already submerged, and her grandfather's boat was going back into the water it had come from, and she was going with it because she had chosen to go with it or because she could not imagine not going with it, which are different things or the same thing, and her face in the rain and the spray and the last gray light was the face of someone who had arrived at a place past the reach of repossession.
