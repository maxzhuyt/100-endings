### I. DESH

The thing about telepathy that nobody ever gets right in the movies is the noise floor.

You'd think it would be voices. Clean, discrete. Little radio stations you could tune between. Morgan Freeman narrating someone's inner life while you lean back and listen with your eyes closed. That's because movies are made by people with quiet skulls, people whose heads contain only one voice, and that voice just rattles around like a dried pea in a can and they call it *thinking.*

What it actually is—what it *actually* is—

*—left the burner on, I left the goddamn burner on, Mei is going to—*

What it actually is—

*—if I die here my browser history oh god oh fuck my BROWSER HISTORY—*

I'm trying to explain something. Give me a second.

*—can't breathe can't breathe can't—*

*—PARKING. TICKET. I had fourteen days to contest it and I spent eleven of those days in this fucking BUNKER and when I get out of here, if I get out of here, the FIRST thing I'm going to do is—*

What it actually is, is a room where seven people are screaming at different volumes about different things, and you can hear all of them, and also you can hear what they're *not* saying, the underneath-thoughts, the ones they don't even know they're having, the little animal machinery grinding away beneath language, and it all comes at you at once and you cannot—

You *cannot*—

*—turn it off, Desh, why can't you just turn it off, you're supposed to be the best, the BEST, that's what they TOLD us when they brought you in, "Parmar is a class-five receiving telepath, he can—"*

That's Lena. Thinking about me. Not *at* me—she knows better—but about me, the way you think about a tool you need that might be broken. Clinical. Frustrated. A little afraid, though she'd bite through her own tongue before admitting it.

I love that about her. I might be the only person in this facility who knows she's capable of fear, and I will take that secret to my grave.¹

Which, given current circumstances, may be a very short commute.

Here is what happened to Marcus Yuen seventeen minutes ago:

Marcus was our remote viewer. Best in the program. The UN scooped him out of a graduate program at Tsinghua where he'd been pretending to study applied physics while secretly mapping the interior of the Pentagon from a dorm room in Beijing.² Good guy. Nervous laugh. Always had those little individually wrapped candies from some shop in Kowloon, and he'd offer you one every time you passed him in the hall, every single time, like a tic, like a compulsion, and you'd take one even though they tasted like someone had described the concept of strawberry to a person who had never eaten fruit and that person had tried their best.

Seventeen minutes ago, Marcus was in Remote Viewing Suite C, trying to get a lock on the thing we'd let in. Because that's what we do here—*did* here—we sat in darkened rooms in electromagnetic-shielded chairs and sent our minds out like fishing lines into places they shouldn't go, and when something tugged back, we were supposed to note it in a file and move on.

We were not supposed to *keep pulling.*

But the UN wanted coordinates. The UN always wants coordinates. And Marcus was good—so good—and the thing on the other end of his line was *interesting,* the way a deep-ocean trench is interesting, the way a sound you can't identify in an empty house at 3 AM is interesting, and he kept reaching, kept pushing his perception deeper—

*—it's looking back, it's looking back, it sees me, oh god it doesn't have eyes but it SEES me—*

That was three days ago.

What came through wasn't physical exactly. Not at first. It was more like a stain. A wrongness in the air in Suite C that made your eyes water if you looked at it straight, that showed up on thermal cameras as a hole—not cold, not hot, just *absent.* A patch of nothing that ate light.

Then it started moving.

And then it started being *something.*

I couldn't read it. I want to be clear about that. I have read the minds of humans and, on one classified occasion, a dolphin that was thinking unspeakable things.³ But the thing in the hallways—when I reach toward it, when I brush the place where its thoughts should be—

There's a sound like a door opening onto a room that is too large.

And then I pull back. Every time, I pull back.

Marcus didn't pull back.

Seventeen minutes ago, I felt Marcus Yuen's mind go out like a candle in a wind tunnel. Not slowly. Not with a guttering struggle. Just—*there,* and then *not there,* and in the space between those two states, a single final thought, blazing and unmistakable and so loud it brought me to my knees in the corridor where Lena and I were trying to barricade the east stairwell:

*—I never contested that parking ticket. Sixty-five dollars. It was a TUESDAY. There was NO SIGN. I had PHOTOGRAPHIC EVIDENCE on my PHONE—*

And then nothing.

And then less than nothing.

Marcus Yuen, remote viewer, class-four psionic, the man who once psychically surveilled a nuclear submarine from a folding chair in a broom closet, died furious about sixty-five dollars and a parking sign that may or may not have been obscured by a tree.

I am so tired of knowing how people end.
