# Every Exit Collapses

---

## I. The Ordinary Weight of Knowing

The first mind of the morning shift is always the worst because it comes with coffee still cooling in the thermos and the booth's fluorescents not yet warmed to their full migraine pitch, and because Desh hasn't yet rebuilt the calluses that eight hours of sleep have softened, so when the 5:47 woman—he has never learned her name but she has been the 5:47 woman for eleven years, her tap-card always slightly too slow against the reader, her wrist perpetually crooked from an old fracture she never thinks about but that Desh can feel like an ache in his own bones—when she presses through the turnstile bar and her mind opens to him like a door blown off its hinges by wind, he receives the whole of her: the argument with her daughter last night about something trivial, a lamp, the placement of a lamp, but beneath the lamp the real argument, always the real argument, which is *I am getting old and you are getting far away from me and I don't know how to say it without screaming*, and under even that a thin bright wire of satisfaction because the daughter cried first, and under *that* the guilt about the satisfaction, and Desh breathes, nods, says "Morning," and she says "Morning," and she is through and gone to the 1847 platform because she works in the textile regulatory office, Victorian branch, and she has no idea that a man in a booth just held every room in her heart.

Forty years of this.

He'd done the math once, an idle Tuesday, scratching numbers on the back of a transfer slip. Roughly nine hundred people through his gate per shift. Three hundred shifts a year, give or take holidays. Forty years. That's—and he'd stopped, pencil hovering, because the number was obscene. That's nearly eleven million minds he's held for the half-second of a turnstile tap. Eleven million kitchen arguments and petty vendettas and the vast majority thinking about lunch, about sex, about whether they left the stove on, about money, about a song stuck somewhere between the ear and the jaw, catchy little pop number from 2340 or a hymn from 1100 or, once, from a historian who specialized in deep Paleolithic culture, a rhythm that was barely music at all, just a pulse like a heartbeat struck against a hollow log, and that one Desh had kept, had held in the cave of his own skull long after the historian had passed through, because it was the closest any mind had ever come to being *beautiful* without knowing it was beautiful, which is the only way beauty works, Desh has decided, after eleven million entries in his involuntary catalog.

He doesn't know why he can do it. He's never known.

It started when he was nineteen, the year they installed the new biometric turnstiles at Kovalam Junction, and his supervisor said *Desh, you're on Gate 7*, and the first person to tap through was a man in a gray suit thinking about killing his business partner, not metaphorically, not colorfully, but with specific and detailed intention involving rat poison and the temporal lag between 1987 and 1987-B where coroners were notoriously overworked, and Desh had vomited into his own lap and told no one, because who do you tell, and what do you say, and would anyone believe you, and—this was the real reason, the one he's spent forty years trying not to examine too closely—*would it matter even if they did*.

He'd thought about quitting. Of course he'd thought about quitting. Every day for the first decade he'd thought about it the way a man standing on a bridge thinks about the water below, not with real intention but with the vertigo of knowing the option exists. But the telepathy wasn't tied to the turnstile. He'd confirmed that on his days off, in grocery stores, on park benches, every brushed shoulder and bumped elbow a small invasion. The turnstile only made it *consistent*—a fixed point, a frame. At least in the booth he knew when it was coming. At least there was a rhythm: tap, open, know, grieve, close, next. Out in the world it was ambush after ambush, minds exploding against him like birds into glass.

So he stayed.

He stayed because the booth at least gave him walls. He stayed because the Transit Authority pension was decent and because the health coverage included temporal-displacement therapy, which he didn't need but which suggested a bureaucracy that at least *gestured* toward caring about its workers' interior lives, even if the gesture was hollow, even if the therapist he'd been assigned after his first decade was a cheerful woman from 2200 who wanted to talk about "boundaries" as if what Desh needed was a better metaphorical fence and not a concrete bunker. He stayed because by year fifteen the calluses were thick enough that he could process a mind in half a second and let it go, most of the time, the way a surgeon can hold a beating heart and set it back down without trembling.

Most of the time.

There are minds that stick. He keeps a list, not written anywhere—he's not a fool—but indexed in the long hallway of his memory, a hallway that after forty years has the quality of a hotel corridor in a dream, doors on both sides, all closed, all humming.

The woman in 2003 who was on her way to a gate that would take her to 1940 and she was thinking, with absolute calm, *I'm going to bring him penicillin and he will live and I don't care what the Continuity Office says, I don't care, I don't care, Alexander will live*. And Desh had wanted to reach through the glass and take her hand and say *Yes, go, run, save him*, but he didn't, because that's not what gate attendants do, and because the Continuity Office was not known for its lenience, and because he could also feel, in the deeper strata of her mind, that she already knew it wouldn't work, had already tried it once or twice or seven times and been caught or thwarted or found that Alexander still died, on schedule, of something else, because the timestream protects its investments with a cruelty that looks, from a sufficient distance, like physics.

The teenage boy in 2087 thinking about nothing. Genuinely nothing. A void so total and serene that Desh had gasped and pressed his forehead to the cool glass of his booth and thought, *Is that what peace feels like? Is that what it's like to not be drowning?*

The old man—Platform 12, outbound to the Mesozoic, paleobotanical survey permit—whose mind was a single sustained note of wonder, just wonder, nothing else, a man about to see living ferns the size of cathedrals and already weeping inside about it.

But those are three. Three in eleven million.

The rest is noise. The rest is what Desh has come to think of as the Human Frequency, which is not a sound but a texture, like burlap, like sandpaper, like rubbing your face against a wall until the skin comes off: the endless, grinding, banal preoccupation with self. Not evil, mostly. He wants to be clear about that with himself. He has argued this point with himself many times, usually around 2 a.m. when the shift has gone quiet and the only passengers are temporal researchers and insomniacs and the occasional lost child whose mind is mercifully open and formless. Most people are not evil. Most people are just *small*. Their thoughts are an arm's length in every direction, a cocoon of want and worry and minor grievance, and they carry this cocoon through the turnstile and onto the platform and across centuries and back again without ever once reaching out, without once thinking *I wonder what this gate attendant's life is like*, *I wonder if that woman crying on the bench needs help*, *I wonder if something is happening that I am too comfortable to notice.*

And that—Desh knows, has known for decades, knows the way you know a chronic pain, continuously and without revelation—that is the thing that has curdled him. Not the cruelty. The cruelty is rare enough to be survivable, a sharp shock every few hundred minds, the kind of thing that makes him grip the edge of his counter and breathe. It's the *indifference*. The great gray ocean of it. Eleven million minds and not one of them—not *one*—has ever tapped through his turnstile thinking about him.

## II. The First Pass

She comes through at 7:14.

Desh registers her the way he registers everyone: a brief flare of data, a tap-card's blue pulse, the turnstile arm rotating. She's young, maybe twenty-five, though with temporal commuters age is a lie; she could be ninety and unwound, or twelve and fast-forwarded for a work permit. Dark hair. A backpack worn on both shoulders, the way children wear them, both straps buckled across her chest.

Her mind—

Desh's hand stops halfway to his coffee.

Her mind is not like other minds. Other minds are rooms—cluttered, stale, predictable in their architecture. Hers is a stairwell, and someone is running down it, and the stairs are *disappearing behind her as she runs*.

*—not again not again please I just came from here I JUST CAME FROM HERE the gate the gate was supposed to take me to April but it folded and I'm back I'm back in the station again and the clock says 7:14 but my clock says 11:30 no 6:00 no YESTERDAY—*

The thoughts don't flow. They *stutter*. Desh has read panicked minds before—missed connections, deportation fears, the woman who realized on the platform that she'd left her child in 1965—and panic has a grammar, chaotic but recognizable, like a language spoken too fast. This is different. This is a mind whose timestamps are *wrong*. He can feel the temporal markers, the little neurological tags that every brain uses to index its experiences—8 a.m. comes after 7 a.m., Tuesday comes after Monday—and in this woman's mind the tags are shuffled, overlapping, contradicting each other. She thinks it is morning and evening and three days ago simultaneously. She thinks she arrived here *for the first time* and that she has been here *always*.

She passes through. She doesn't look at him. Nobody looks at him.

Desh watches her walk toward Platform 3—outbound to the early 21st century, Standard Western calendar—and her gait is wrong, too fast, the walk of a person who is trying not to run.

He should note it. There's a form for aberrant passenger behavior, TA-7b, "Irregularities in Commuter Conduct," a form he has filled out precisely twice in forty years: once for a man who appeared to be vibrating at a frequency slightly outside the visible spectrum (temporal phasing, turned out; he'd used an unlicensed gate in Kuala Lumpur and his molecules hadn't fully re-synced), and once for a woman who was walking backward through the turnstile with her eyes closed, which turned out to be a performance art piece, and which Desh had secretly enjoyed because it was the first time anyone had treated his gate as something worth performing for.

He doesn't fill out the form. He tells himself it's because the form is in the bottom drawer and the bottom drawer sticks and his knees ache when he bends. He tells himself it's because she's probably fine, probably just a bad gate transfer, temporal jet lag, the kind of thing that self-corrects.

He tells himself this because he is, and has always been, a coward.

That's not self-pity. Desh is long past self-pity—it burned off somewhere around year twenty, like morning fog, leaving behind a landscape that was not beautiful but was at least *clear*. He is a coward in the way that a wall is vertical: it's a structural fact, load-bearing, not open to revision. He stood in the booth when the man with the rat poison walked through. He stood in the booth when the woman with the penicillin walked through. He stood in the booth when the boy from 2019 tapped his card and his mind was a howling red canvas of *I am going to step in front of the express gate and let it scatter me across six centuries* and Desh had gripped the counter and breathed and watched the boy walk to Platform 9 and had thought *Please don't, please don't, please don't* as if thinking were the same as acting, as if witness were the same as intervention.

The boy had not stepped in front of the express gate. Not that day. Desh doesn't know about other days. He has trained himself, with the discipline of a monk, not to wonder about other days.

7:14 passes. The morning rush swells. Minds pour through him: tax worries, affair guilt, what to have for dinner, a toddler's incandescent fury at the texture of socks, a tempo-tourist excited about seeing the 1969 moon landing for the third time, a bureaucrat from the Continuity Office thinking about budget cuts with the intensity of a man defusing a bomb.

## III. The Second Pass

She comes through again at 8:02.

*Same woman.* Same backpack. But the backpack is more worn now—a strap is fraying, and there's a stain on the left shoulder that wasn't there before, something dark, could be coffee, could be blood, could be the residue of a gate that didn't close cleanly. Desh has seen gate residue before. It looks like bruising on fabric.

She taps through. Her mind—

*—the 19th century is closed. I tried the 19th century and it FOLDED. The gate opened and I could see London, I could see the fog and the horses and I stepped through and I was back here. I was back in the station. 8:02. The clock said 8:02. But I've been running for—how long have I been—my phone says six hours but the station clock says forty-eight minutes since I—*

*—something is following me—*

*—something is IN THE GATES—*
