# CHART NOTES FROM AN EMPTY WARD

---

## I.

Here is what you need to know before any of it begins: only one of them will make it through.

The narrator offers this not as suspense but as diagnosis. A thing observed in the tissue, confirmed by the second opinion of time. Whether "making it through" means emerging into the parking lot where the rain has spent eleven months turning asphalt back to gravel, or whether it means something else — whether it means carrying, in whatever architecture of the brain still holds, the knowledge that one was a dog who belonged to a person, and that this mattered — is a question the narrator will not resolve. The narrator is not in the business of resolution. The narrator is in the business of the chart, the note, the observed and the recorded, the progression of a thing from what it was to what it becomes, and the narrator has found, across many years of observation, that resolution is a courtesy extended to the living by other living, and that in this building there is very little of either left.

Their names were Sunny and Bravo.

The past tense is not careless. It is clinical. A name requires someone to speak it, and no one has spoken in the Veterans Affairs Medical Center at Ridgeback since the second week of November, thirteen months ago, when the last nurse — a woman named Delia Royce, fifty-one, who had refused three separate evacuation orders because there were still patients on mechanical ventilation in Ward 6 and she would not leave people on mechanical ventilation — stopped breathing on the floor of the dispensary, her lungs colonized so thickly with *Candida auris* that the postmortem, had anyone conducted one, would have found them the texture and color of wet bread. Her name badge is still there, half-hidden under a collapsed shelving unit, and once, early on, the golden retriever found it and held it in her mouth for the better part of an afternoon. Not because she knew Delia Royce. Because the badge lanyard smelled of alcohol sanitizer and the particular human-salt of a throat in a warm room, and these things, in aggregate, smelled like work. Like purpose. Like the weight of a vest and the click of a leash and the low, steady words that meant: *you are going somewhere you are needed.*

She put it down eventually. She has not found it since. The topography of the dispensary has changed three times — water intrusion, ceiling tile collapse, a slow avalanche of pharmaceutical stock that no one will ever inventory — and the badge has migrated somewhere beneath the rubble with Delia Royce's reading glasses and Delia Royce's half-eaten packet of peanut butter crackers and the small, framed photograph of Delia Royce's son in his Army dress uniform that Delia Royce kept in her scrub pocket and that nobody will ever look at again.

This is the kind of information the narrator keeps. It is not sentimental. It is the record.

## II.

The golden retriever was certified through Warrior Canine Connection, trained at the Bethesda campus, and assigned to the Ridgeback VA's Physical Rehabilitation wing on a Tuesday in April, four years before the outbreak. Her handler was a physical therapist named Martin Cade, who had a beard and no particular gift for language but who communicated, through the leash and through his hands, a set of expectations so clear and so consistent that the dog built her entire cognitive world around them. She worked with amputees, mostly. She learned to hold still while a man with one arm practiced gripping. She learned to walk at the exact speed of a woman learning to walk again. She learned that the rehabilitation wing smelled of rubber mats and isopropyl and effort, and that these were good smells, and that the room with the parallel bars was the center of the world.

Progressive retinal atrophy is a group of genetic diseases characterized by the bilateral degeneration of the retina, leading to progressive vision loss and, ultimately, total blindness. In golden retrievers, the most common form — *prcd*-PRA, progressive rod-cone degeneration — typically manifests first as night blindness, then as a gradual narrowing of the visual field, then as a graying, a hazing, as though the world were being viewed through increasingly opaque glass. There is no pain. This is worth noting because the narrator believes in the precise documentation of suffering and its absence, and the retriever does not suffer from the blindness itself. She suffers from its consequences.

Thirteen months ago, when the evacuation orders came and the staff left in waves — first the administrators, then the specialists, then the nurses who had families, then the nurses who had sense — Martin Cade left. He was not a nurse. He was not bound by any oath to patients on ventilators. He had a wife in Charlottesville and a child on the way, and he left in the second wave, on a Thursday, and he left quickly because the *Candida auris* was already in the HVAC system and the CDC team had used the word *aerosolized* in a tone of voice that made everyone who heard it stop pretending this was containable. He loaded his car. He drove away. He did not take the dog.

This was not cruelty. The evacuation protocols were for humans. The dogs — there were four therapy animals on-site — were to be collected by a veterinary disaster response team that was dispatched from Roanoke and that never arrived because the interstate was closed and the team was rerouted to a flooded shelter in Lynchburg and then, in the particular cascading logic of institutional failure, simply forgotten.

Martin Cade, in Charlottesville, filed paperwork. He called numbers. He was told the dogs had been collected. He was told this by a person reading from a spreadsheet that had been filled in preemptively, aspirationally, by someone who believed the Roanoke team would arrive because they had been dispatched and dispatch, in that person's experience, was the same as completion.

The retriever waited in the rehabilitation wing for four days. She waited by the rubber mats. She waited by the parallel bars. She waited with a precision that was, in its way, the highest expression of her training: she had been told to stay, not in words, not on this occasion, but in the aggregate of four years of being told to stay and then being rewarded for staying, and so she stayed, and the staying was not obedience anymore but identity, the way a river does not obey gravity but is gravity, expressed in water.

On the fifth day, she went to find water, because thirst is older than training, and she did not come back to the rehabilitation wing because the doors had swung shut and the electromagnetic locks, which had been on a backup generator, had engaged when the generator finally kicked over, and the rehabilitation wing was sealed.

She has been trying to get back ever since.

## III.

The German shepherd was a different kind of dog.

He had been trained through a Department of Defense program, worked with a handler in the Army's Warrior Transition Unit, and was retired — medically, on paper — after his handler completed the program and moved to Ohio. Bravo was reassigned to the VA as a general therapy animal, which is a polite way of saying he was given to an institution that needed a dog and did not have the budget for a specifically trained one. He adapted. He was good at adapting. He learned the new commands, the new rhythms, the new people. He visited patients in their rooms. He lay beside beds. He did not flinch at the sounds that came from Ward 6 at night, the sounds that were not illness but memory, the sounds of men revisiting in sleep the things they could not revisit awake.

Canine cognitive dysfunction is, in the language of the literature, an age-related neurodegenerative condition analogous to Alzheimer's disease in humans. It is characterized by progressive deterioration of cognitive function, including memory, learning, perception, and awareness. The hallmarks, in the clinical shorthand that veterinarians use, are summarized by the acronym DISHA: Disorientation, altered Interactions with people and other animals, Sleep-wake cycle disturbances, House soiling, and changes in Activity level.

The narrator prefers a different summary: the dog forgets what he knows. Then he forgets that he knew it. Then he forgets what knowing was.

Bravo is eight years old, which is not old for a German shepherd, which means the disease is early-onset, which means it is aggressive, which means the progression that might take three years in another dog is taking months in this one. Thirteen months ago, when the hospital emptied, Bravo could still sit on command, still heel, still distinguish between a door that should be entered and a door that should not. Six months ago, he could still navigate the hospital's main corridors without circling. Three months ago, he could still recognize the retriever as a specific, known individual — as a dog he had worked beside, not merely as a dog.

Now he recognizes her as something. A shape that smells correct, that moves in a way that does not trigger his prey drive or his territorial aggression. He follows her. He does not know why he follows her. The neural pathway that encoded the why — the four years of shared space, the mornings in the therapy room when they lay on opposite sides of the same mat, the specific and irreplaceable knowledge that *this is Sunny and Sunny is safe* — has been replaced by a kind of white noise, a static, and through the static there is only the signal of her movement and the fact that his body moves in response, the way a compass needle moves in response to north: not because the needle understands north, but because the needle is made of a material that has no choice.

The narrator notes, for the record, that this is not a metaphor the shepherd would appreciate. The shepherd does not appreciate metaphors. The shepherd, increasingly, does not appreciate anything, because appreciation requires the comparison of a present state to a remembered state, and his remembered states are being deleted, one by one, like files from a corrupted drive, and what remains is not a dog who has lost his memory but a dog who is becoming something prior to memory — something that runs on the ancient code, the pre-domestication code, the code that says: *eat, avoid pain, move toward warmth, do not die.*

He still shadows the retriever.

The narrator finds this remarkable and does not say so, because the narrator does not find things remarkable. The narrator notes it and moves on.

## IV.

The hospital is dying the way hospitals die when there is no one to stop them: aggressively, and from the inside out.

The Ridgeback VA Medical Center was built in 1971, expanded in 1988, and partially renovated in 2004 with federal funds that covered the main lobby, the administrative wing, and approximately thirty percent of the electrical system before the money was reallocated to a facility in Maryland that had a more politically connected director. The remaining seventy percent of the electrical system is original, which means it is fifty-two years old, which means the insulation on the wiring in Ward 3 has the tensile strength of dried pasta and the junction boxes in the basement have been actively corroding since before the dogs were born.

Rain enters the building through the roof, which lost sixteen percent of its membrane in a windstorm the previous March. Rain enters through the windows, forty-three of which were broken during the evacuation — some by people leaving, some by people trying to get in after the quarantine perimeter was established, some by the particular desperation of a Tuesday night when three families arrived with symptomatic patients and found the doors locked and the CDC tape up and nobody inside who was alive enough to hear them knocking. Rain enters through the loading dock, whose rolling door was left half-open by the last supply truck, which delivered seventy-two cases of saline, forty cases of nitrile gloves, and a crate of body bags that no one unpacked and that the retriever sometimes sleeps beside because the cardboard has retained, through some alchemy of material and memory, the scent of the warehouse in Bethesda where she was trained.

What rain does to a building over thirteen months:

It finds the seams. It enters through the seams. It colonizes the drywall, which is made of gypsum and paper, which is to say it is made ofite food for water and food for mold. The drywall softens, then darkens, then blooms — first in spots, then in continents, then in entire walls that are no longer walls but ecosystems, black and green and furred with hyphae, breathing out spores in quantities that the narrator could enumerate but will not, because the number is large enough to be meaningless and the effect is visible enough to be its own data: the dogs cough. The strays cough. The cougar, in her den in the sub-basement, coughs, and her cubs — there are two, ten weeks old, still nursing — cough, and the sound rises through the floor vents like a rumor.

The floors are worse. The hospital was built with poured concrete over steel decking on the ground level and precast concrete planks on the upper floors, and the precast planks were rated for their loads and their loads did not include thirteen months of unmanaged water infiltration weakening the grout in the joints and corroding the steel reinforcement within the concrete itself, and so the floors flex. They groan. In Ward 4, on the second story, a section of floor the size of a dining table collapsed in October, dropping a filing cabinet and an adjustable bed frame into the room below with a sound that sent every living thing in the building into a terror so absolute and so unanimous that for eleven seconds, nothing moved. Not the dogs. Not the strays. Not the cougar. Not the rats, whose population the narrator estimates at between three and four hundred and whose movements through the walls provide a constant, papery susurrus that the retriever uses, in the absence of sight, as a kind of sonar — the rats move away from danger, and so the absence of rat-sound means danger, and she listens for the silences the way a sailor watches for the places where the water goes flat.

The building is not falling down. The building is lying down. Settling into its own foundation the way a body settles into a bed it will not get up from, and the process is patient and without malice and absolutely certain, and the narrator has calculated, based on the rate of deterioration and the load-bearing capacity of the remaining structural elements, that the second floor will be impassable by spring and the roof will begin its final collapse by the following winter, and none of this matters to the dogs because dogs do not have calendars, dogs have *now,* and now the building is still standing, and now the rain is coming through the ceiling of the main corridor in six distinct streams that the retriever has memorized by sound — the quick, metallic ping of water hitting a gurney, the flat slap of water hitting linoleum, the deeper, almost hollow sound of water hitting the carpet in the administrative wing that has been wet so long it no longer dries — and she navigates between them with a precision that looks, to anyone who might watch, like sight.

No one watches.

This is the fact that structures everything else: no one watches. No one has come. The quarantine perimeter, established by the county health department and reinforced by the National Guard, was officially lifted in February but the road to the facility was washed out in the same storm that took the roof membrane and no one has repaired it because the facility is on no one's list anymore, the facility has been removed from the system, the facility exists in the particular bureaucratic limbo reserved for places where everyone died and the paperwork was filed and the line item was zeroed out and
