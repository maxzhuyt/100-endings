# THE ARNETTE ANOMALY

## Or: A Complete Institutional History of the Six Days Nobody at Arnette State University Could Get Their Shit Together, Compiled from Primary Sources and Presented with the Weary Authority of Someone Who Watched Every Single Second of It

---

## PART ONE: TUESDAY

---

The object came up out of the ground at 7:43 a.m. on a Tuesday in October, which is already the most meaningless day of the week and therefore, in retrospect, the only day it could have happened.

It emerged from the seam of grass between Linden Hall (Sciences) and the Margaret Okoye Humanities Pavilion—a stretch of quad that had, until that moment, been notable only for the fact that a senior named Derek Koh-Varilla had once thrown up an entire Chipotle burrito bowl there during orientation week 2021 and the grass had never quite recovered. The ground didn't crack. There was no sound. Campus security footage—the one camera that happened to be pointed in the right direction, mounted on the loading dock behind the dining hall—shows the following: at 7:42, there is a quad. At 7:43, there is a quad with something in it.

The word "something" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It will continue to do a lot of work for the duration of this account.

What the camera captured—what every subsequent photograph, video, and one deeply inadvisable oil painting would also capture—was a shape. It was approximately nine feet tall at what most people agreed was probably its apex, assuming it had one, which was not a safe assumption. It was not quite silver and not quite the color of the inside of your eyelid when you press on it. It had edges, or it had suggestions of edges, or it had the memory of edges, and the number of those edges changed depending on where you were standing and whether you'd eaten breakfast. A geometry professor named Dr. Alice Yun would later describe it to a local news crew as "a solid object that is somehow also an argument," and then she would ask them to please not air that quote because she'd been awake for thirty-one hours, and they would air it immediately.

It did not move. It did not hum. It did not glow or pulse or do any of the things that objects in movies do to announce their narrative importance. It simply sat there, silent and incomprehensible, in the exact center of the most heavily trafficked pedestrian corridor on campus, and it made the air around it taste faintly of copper and wrong numbers.

The first person to see it in the flesh was Margaret "Midge" DeLuca-Foster, a facilities groundskeeper on her morning route, who stood roughly forty feet away, holding a leaf blower, and said: "Huh."

The second person to see it was Dr. Rangan Mehta, Associate Professor of Inorganic Chemistry, who was cutting through the quad to avoid a colleague he owed a peer review. He stopped. He stared. He took off his glasses, cleaned them, put them back on. He took a photograph that would later circulate on Twitter with 1.3 million impressions and the caption "me when the edible hits at the conference." He said nothing, because Dr. Mehta had not voluntarily spoken before 10 a.m. since 2009.

The third person to see it was a sophomore named Jordan Kasterlee, who walked directly into a bike rack while staring at it and chipped a tooth.

This, as it turned out, was not an accident.

By 9:00 a.m. the group chat had exceeded two hundred members and the conversation had forked into eleven simultaneous arguments, including but not limited to: whether the object was man-made or geological; whether it was an elaborate prank by the engineering school at Calloway Tech across town; whether the government was involved; whether it was a marketing stunt for a energy drink; and whether Chaz Fineman could be trusted as a reliable narrator given that he had once claimed to have seen a mountain lion in the Wendy's parking lot (Chaz maintained then and maintains now that the mountain lion was real).

But the most important thing that happened in that first hour was not in the group chat. It was not in any digital record at all. It happened to a fraternity pledge named Aiden Briggs, who, on a dare from his pledge master, attempted to walk up to the object and take a selfie with it.

Aiden Briggs was nineteen years old, six foot one, and had never once in his life experienced a moment of profound public embarrassment. He'd been handsome since the seventh grade. He'd scored the winning goal in his high school's state semifinal. He wore confidence like a factory setting. He walked toward the object with his phone raised and his jaw set at the angle he'd identified, through extensive practice, as his best angle.

At approximately eight feet from the object's surface—or what his body interpreted as eight feet, though distance seemed to lose its enthusiasm near the thing—Aiden Briggs tripped.

There was nothing to trip on. The ground was flat, recently mown, and free of debris. He tripped on, by all available evidence, the concept of walking. His legs simply forgot their choreography. He went down face-first with enough momentum to slide two feet across the grass, and his phone, which he had been holding at arm's length, somehow—through a chain of fumbles so improbable that the physics department would later request the security footage—bounced off his own forehead, spiraled into the air, and landed screen-down in a puddle that had not, by multiple witness accounts, been there thirty seconds ago.

He stood up to a crowd of three hundred people.

His fly was down. It had not been down when he started walking. His shirt had come untucked in precisely the way that made it look like a bib. There was a grass stain on his face in the shape of—and this is attested to by photographs from nine separate angles—a nearly perfect question mark.

"Dude," said his pledge master, who had gone pale. "How did you—"

"I don't know," said Aiden Briggs, in a voice that had somehow gone up half an octave.

He was, he would realize much later, the first experiment. The object had used him to demonstrate its thesis.

Nobody understood what they had just seen. Not yet. That would take two more incidents, a pattern, and one aggressively pre-med sophomore's doomed attempt at a controlled observation.

But we're getting ahead of ourselves. First: the theater kids.

---

It is necessary, at this point, to provide context that will seem like a digression but is in fact structurally essential, because you cannot understand what happened at Arnette State University during the week of October 14 without understanding that the campus was, even before the object appeared, a ecosystem of deeply entrenched feuds operating at various stages of detonation, and that the object did not create chaos so much as it *provided chaos with a public venue.*

The Arnette State University Department of Theater Arts and the Arnette Improv Collective (AIC) had been engaged in what both sides described, without irony, as a "war" since the spring of 2023, when the AIC had been granted use of the Black Box Theater on alternating Fridays—a space the theater department considered sovereign territory and which the improv club had, in their first week of occupancy, used to perform a set that included a ten-minute bit called "Shakespeare But He's a Guy at Jiffy Lube" that the department chair, Professor Eleanor Voss, described in a formal complaint as "an act of cultural vandalism indistinguishable from a hate crime against language."

The improv club's president, a junior named Bex Thornton-Moore who performed under the stage name "Bex Machina," had responded by doing a fifteen-minute crowd-work set at the campus comedy night in which she improvised an increasingly specific and accurate parody of Professor Voss's speaking style, including Voss's signature habit of pausing mid-sentence to close her eyes as though receiving a transmission from a more aesthetically worthy dimension. The video had gotten 340,000 views on TikTok. Professor Voss had not addressed it publicly. Professor Voss had not needed to. Professor Voss had simply scheduled the theater department's tech rehearsal for *Antigone* to run until 11 p.m. on every alternating Friday for the rest of the semester, meaning the improv club arrived each week to a Black Box full of set pieces, a soundboard running Theban crowd noise, and a stage manager named Petra who would stand in the doorway and say "we're not done" with the quiet authority of someone who had been empowered to define the word "done."

The war had subcampaigns. The theater kids had started a whisper campaign that improv wasn't real performance. The improv kids had started doing bits in the dining hall that were transparently about specific theater students, including a recurring character called "Resting Monologue Face." Three friendships had ended. Two couples had broken up. One double major had been forced to choose.

On the morning of October 14, both factions were in the quad—the theater kids because they had an outdoor rehearsal space behind Okoye Pavilion, the improv club because Bex had called an emergency meeting to workshop material for the upcoming "Laugh Riot" competition against Calloway Tech—and for the first time in eighteen months, they were standing next to each other without a single hostile glance, because both groups were staring at the object with expressions of identical, slack-jawed incomprehension, and it is very difficult to maintain a feud when the universe has just introduced something that makes all human creative endeavor feel like a bit.

The fraternity situation requires less exposition but more patience.

Delta Kappa Sigma was mid-Rush, which meant that their chapter house—a three-story Victorian on Greek Row that had been "renovated" in 2018 in a process that involved removing one load-bearing wall and replacing it with, as far as the county building inspector could determine, optimism—was full of forty-seven sleep-deprived pledges and a pledge master named Connor Ault who had recently discovered the concept of "psychological resilience training" via a podcast and had been applying it with the creativity of someone who had not read the primary sources.

The DKS pledges had been awake for approximately forty hours when the object appeared. They had been doing trust falls, which Connor had escalated to "trust falls but blindfolded," which he had then escalated to "trust falls but blindfolded and no one tells you when," which several pledges had pointed out was just "getting pushed." They were, in short, already operating at the exact intersection of exhaustion and suggestibility that made them maximally vulnerable to something like an incomprehensible geometric anomaly, and when word reached the chapter house that there was a "weird thing" in the quad, all forty-seven of them had walked over in a herd, still wearing their matching Rush Week tank tops, and stood in a cluster staring at it with the glazed reverence of early hominids encountering fire.

"What if we, like, brought it offerings," said a pledge named Skyler.

"What would you offer it," said another pledge named also Skyler.

"I don't know. A beer?"

"Don't offer the anomaly a beer, Skyler."

Connor Ault, to his credit, had enough functioning executive function left to recognize that the object represented a potential liability issue. To his discredit, his response was to announce that approaching the object would be "a new pledge challenge" and that whoever got closest would be "first pick for the philanthropy committee," which was DKS code for "gets out of cleaning the bathroom."

Three pledges approached the object in quick succession.

The first, a freshman named Miles, attempted to walk up confidently and bow to it. Midway through the bow, his shorts split from inseam to waistband with a sound that witnesses described as "emphatic." He was wearing boxers printed with cartoon lobsters.
