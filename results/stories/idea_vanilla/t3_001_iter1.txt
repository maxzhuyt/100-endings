# Pending Further Processing

## A Report Filed by Junior Ranger Lucinda Ochoa, Olympic District, Regarding the Events of September 14–15

---

We were counting owls. I need to start there because if I don't anchor it to something ordinary the rest of it lifts off the ground and I can't get it back.

Northern spotted owl survey. Transect 9, upper Quinault drainage. Old growth, the real stuff—Doug firs so wide you can't see around them, canopy so dense the floor stays dark at noon. Dale had walked that transect eleven times in six years. I'd walked it with him twice. This was supposed to be my third.

My knee was bad. The meniscus I'd torn two summers ago doing trail maintenance on the Enchanted Valley route had swollen up overnight, and when I came out of my tent at the trailhead camp that morning I was limping hard enough that Dale noticed before I'd crossed fifteen feet of gravel.

"Nope," he said. He was sitting on the tailgate of the truck, lacing his boots. He didn't look up. "You're base camp."

"Dale—"

"You're base camp and you're keeping the radio hot and you're going to elevate that knee and drink water and stop being twenty-six about it."

That was Dale. Twelve years my senior in the service, twenty-two years older than me in the world. He spoke like a man tightening lug nuts—quarter turns, firm, nothing extra. He'd been my training officer when I started at the district, and he was the reason I'd stayed. Not because he was warm. He wasn't warm. He was *reliable*, which at that point in my life I'd confused for the same thing and have since decided might actually be better.

He took the handheld, the clipboard, the owl callback speaker. He took two liters of water and a Clif bar and his headlamp because the protocol called for dusk surveys—you play the recorded call, you wait, you listen, you mark. He'd be out until well after dark.

"Radio check every hour," I said.

"Every two."

"The regs say—"

"The regs say you should've told me about that knee yesterday. Every two." He adjusted his pack and walked into the trees, and within about forty feet the forest took him the way deep water takes a stone. Not fast. Just completely.

---

First check-in was 1400. Right on time.

"Base, this is Mattick, transect 9, station one complete. One contact, probable juvenile barred, not spotted. Moving to station two. Over."

"Copy, Dale. How's the trail?"

"Trail's a trail. Elk have been through here. Lots of sign."

"Copy."

"Also somebody nailed something to a tree out here."

I sat up a little. We were strict about that in the old growth—no blazing, no nailing, nothing into the living wood. "What kind of something?"

"Looks like a…" A pause. Faint sound of him moving through brush. "Huh. Looks like a little wooden sign. Like a number plate. Got a '1' on it. Painted white."

"Recent?"

"Can't tell. The nail's rusted but the paint's pretty clean. Weird. I'll flag it for resource management. Mattick out."

I wrote it in the log. *1417: Dale reports nailed sign, numbered "1," on Douglas fir approx. 0.4 mi along transect 9. Possible vandalism.* I elevated my knee. I drank water. I was twenty-six about it anyway—I wanted to be out there. The camp was just a gravel pullout and the truck and a blue tarp I'd strung between two alders, and the river was loud enough to fill the silence, and the silence was loud enough to fill everything else.

---

Second check-in. 1600. Seven minutes late, which for Dale was noteworthy.

"Base, Mattick."

"Go ahead."

"Station three complete. No contacts this round. Canopy's thick—I might be getting into some old survey timber nobody's walked in a while. GPS is getting jumpy."

"How jumpy?"

"Jumpy enough that I'm navigating by compass and pace count. It's fine. Done it before."

"Copy. You find any more signs?"

A longer pause than I expected.

"Yeah. I found number two."

"Same style?"

"Same style. White number on dark wood. This one had a—" He stopped. I heard him exhale, a short almost-laugh. "Okay, this is going to sound strange."

"Go ahead."

"This one had a kind of shelf underneath it. Like a little wooden ledge nailed to the tree below the number. And there was a piece of paper on it. Laminated."

"What kind of paper?"

"Like a form. Like an intake form. It's got fields printed on it. 'Name.' 'Date.' 'Purpose of visit.'" He paused. "There's a golf pencil."

I didn't say anything for a second.

"Some kind of art project?" I said. "Could be someone from the college."

"Could be. Could be someone with too much free time and a laminator. But Lu—"

He almost never called me by my first name on the radio.

"—the second one's placed exactly where my pace count says station two should be. Right where the transect goes. And there's no trail to it except the elk path I've been following. I don't think anyone's been out here who didn't know the survey route."

"Want me to call the district office?"

"On a Saturday? For some hippie art installation? No. I'll document it and keep moving. Just wanted it on record."

"It's on record."

"Also, the 'Purpose of Visit' field—there's a second line under it. Smaller print. Says 'Purpose of visit to the living world.' Like it's being cute."

"That's weird, Dale."

"Yeah." Another almost-laugh. "Forest HR. Maybe I need to file my time sheet with a Douglas fir. Mattick out."

---

I sat with that for a while. I ate half a sandwich. I looked at the map, our transect line drawn in Dale's neat pencil, the stations marked with small red dots, the contour lines tightening where the drainage climbed into the older timber. There was nothing out there. No structures, no trails except game paths. The nearest maintained route was nine miles south. The nearest road was the one I was sitting next to.

I tried to think about who would go to the trouble—hiking into unmarked old growth, finding the exact survey stations, nailing up numbered signs and laminated forms with golf pencils. Why.

The river answered with the only thing a river knows, which is its own name, over and over.

---

Third check-in. 1800. On time. The light was going amber through the trees to the west.

"Base, Mattick."

"Go ahead, Dale."

"Station five complete. One confirmed spotted owl contact, adult female, bearing 270 from station, estimated distance 200 meters. Responded to callback on second attempt."

"That's great. That's a good contact."

"Yeah." He didn't sound like it was great. He sounded careful, the way he sounded when he was writing a report about something that had gone wrong and was choosing his words.

"Everything okay?"

"I found number three. And number four."

"More signs."

"More signs. More forms. Lu, these are—they're getting more detailed. Number three asked for medical history. And I don't mean like 'check if you have allergies.' I mean there were specific fields. Specific conditions."

"What do you mean, specific?"

"I mean one of the fields said 'history of involuntary commitment.' With a yes/no checkbox."

I felt something shift in my stomach.

I knew about Dale's history. Not because he'd told me—Dale would sooner narrate his own autopsy than volunteer personal information. I knew because Cheryl at the district office had told me, my first week, in the way that people in small government offices brief each other on the particular weather systems of their coworkers. *Dale's solid. Best field man we have. He had a rough patch about eight years ago—hospital, the whole thing. He's good now. Just don't surprise him and don't ask about his daughter.*

I'd followed those instructions the way you follow a trail blaze: I looked for the marks, I stayed on the path, I didn't deviate. In two years of working with him I'd learned that his daughter's name was Brie, that she lived in Boise, that she'd stopped calling in 2019, and that the gap where she should have been was the largest thing in his life—larger than the forest, larger than the work, a clearcut in the middle of him where nothing grew back.

"Dale. I think you should come back."

"I've got four more stations."

"You can get them tomorrow. I'll walk them with you."

"On that knee?"

"Dale—"

"There's a rational explanation for this. Someone accessed the survey records. Planned this out. It's harassment or it's a joke or it's someone's MFA thesis. I'm documenting it and I'm finishing the transect."

"At least tell me about number four. What did number four say?"

A pause so long I almost keyed the mic again.

"Number four had a field that said 'Next of kin.' And underneath it someone had already written a name."

"Whose name?"

"Brie's."

The river. The river and the river and the river.

"In my handwriting, Lu."

---

I need to say something about the quality of his voice at this point because it's going to change and I want you to understand what it was changing from. Dale Mattick on the radio sounded like Dale Mattick everywhere else—level, dry, contained. Even telling me that someone had written his estranged daughter's name on a laminated form nailed to a tree in the middle of nowhere, in his handwriting, he sounded like a man reporting a downed tree across a trail. Factual. Slightly inconvenienced. The fear, if it was there, was so deep under the surface that it registered only as a kind of excessive precision, the way a man with a broken rib breathes—very controlled, very deliberate, because the alternative is screaming.

That was 1800. At 1830 the sun dropped below the canopy line. At 1845 I turned on the truck's headlights because the dark was coming in fast and thick and I didn't want to be sitting in it. At 1900 I radioed him.

No answer.

At 1905 I tried again.

At 1912, he called me.

"Lu."

"Dale—I've been trying to reach you—"

"I know. I'm sorry. I was—" A sound that might have been branches moving. Might have been something else. "I was filling out the form."

"What?"

"At number four. I was filling it out. I don't—" For the first time, his voice did something unfamiliar. It bent. "I stood there and I picked up the golf pencil and I filled it out. Name, date, medical history, all of it. I checked the box for involuntary commitment. I wrote Brie's name next to 'Next of kin' even though it was already there. I wrote it again underneath, like I was confirming it. I don't know why I did that."

"Dale, listen to me. I want you to turn around and walk back along the transect—"

"There was a gate."

"A what?"

"After I finished the form. The branches—there's a kind of corridor through the understory here, where the elk have been pushing through, and there was a place where the branches were woven across it like a—like a turnstile, almost. Like a barrier. And after I put the pencil down it opened."

"Branches don't open, Dale."

"I know that." His voice bent again and then straightened. "I know that. I watched it happen. They pulled apart like—like arms uncrossing. And on the other side there was a light."

"What kind of light?"

"Warm. Low. Yellow-green. Like bioluminescence but steadier. It was about two hundred meters ahead and it was right where station six should be, and it was—Lu, it was *inviting*. That's the only word. It looked like the light you remember from a room where you were once happy."

I was standing now. My knee was forgotten. I had the radio in one hand and with the other I was pulling the first aid kit out of the truck, the emergency pack, the second headlamp.

"I'm coming in to get you."

"No. Don't. Listen to me—don't come in here."

"Dale—"

"I need to tell you what was at number five."

"I don't care about number five. I care about getting you out."

"Number five had a desk."

I stopped moving.

"A desk. In the forest. In the old growth, a hundred and fifty feet from the nearest trail. Made of—it was made of material that had come from the forest. Moss and wood and, I think, bone. Antler. Roosevelt elk antler, big ones, forming the legs. And the surface was packed moss, very smooth, very green, like it had been growing for a hundred years into exactly that shape."

"Dale."

"And there was someone sitting behind it."

The river was too loud. I walked away from it, toward the tree line, as if being closer to where he was would help.

"The someone—" His voice was doing something new now, something architectural, like he was building a structure to hold a thing that couldn't be held. "The figure sitting behind the desk was wearing a uniform. Green pants, gray shirt. Our uniform. And it had a name tag. And a face."

"Whose face, Dale."

"Mine."

I sat down on the gravel. I don't remember deciding to. My knee made a sound and I didn't hear it.

"It was my face, Lu. But not—not like a mask. Like a *draft*. Like someone had gotten most of the details right but the expression was— it was the expression of someone who has been doing a very boring job for a very long time and has gotten perfectly comfortable with that. Patient. Almost kind, but the kind of kind that doesn't actually require caring about you. Like a clerk. Like an intake clerk at a hospital."

He knew what intake clerks at hospitals looked like.

"It looked at me and it opened a folder. A manila folder, like from a filing cabinet, except it was made of bark. And it started reading."

"Reading what?"

"My life."

He said it the way you'd say *my blood type*. Flat. Clinical.

"Not events. Not, 'On this date you did this.' More like—an assessment. A performance review. 'Subject demonstrates persistent avoidance of emotional disclosure. Subject has constructed functional identity around professional competence to offset fundamental belief in personal inadequacy. Subject's relationship with daughter Brianne deteriorated following involuntary commitment at Harborview, 2016, and subject has made no substantive effort to repair this relationship due to—'" He stopped. I heard him swallow. "'Due to a conviction, held below the level of conscious articulation, that his daughter's rejection is both deserved and permanent.'"

"Dale, that's not—that's not—"

"It's accurate. You know it's accurate."

I did. I knew it the way I knew the survey routes—by walking them, by seeing the marks, by reading the shape of the terrain.
