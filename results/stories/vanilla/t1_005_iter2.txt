# THE LAST FAST BREAK

## Part One: The Whistle

Hank Delaney died on a Tuesday, between the free-throw line and the scorer's table, in a gymnasium that smelled—as gymnasiums always smell—of rubber and exertion and the particular loneliness of large indoor spaces after hours. He was fifty-seven. He had managed equipment for the Riverside Wolves wheelchair basketball program for eleven years. He had never married. He kept peppermints in the pocket of his khaki shorts and could retape a push-rim in under ninety seconds, and when the paramedics rolled him onto the stretcher, three Jolly Ranchers and a roll of athletic tape fell from his windbreaker onto the lacquered hardwood, and nobody picked them up, and nobody would for two days, because the gymnasium was now a crime scene, and crime scenes operate on their own incomprehensible timeline.

The cause of death was blunt-force trauma to the posterior skull. A fifteen-pound dumbbell from the auxiliary weight rack, wiped but not wiped well enough—a partial palm print would eventually yield nothing useful, though that revelation was still forty hours away, locked in the queue of the county forensic lab like a letter no one wanted to open.

The arrest came on Wednesday morning at 6:14 a.m. Victor Petrie, fifty-one, former head coach of the Eastlake Chargers, was pulled from a Motel 6 bed where he'd been sleeping in his clothes. He did not ask why. He did not ask for a lawyer. He said, "I wondered when you'd come," and offered his wrists with the practiced resignation of a man who had been rehearsing this gesture, though not for the reason anyone assumed.

By Wednesday afternoon, the Riverside Wolves had no equipment manager, no access to their home gym, and a championship game against Lincoln Prep in seventy-two hours.

By Wednesday evening, their point guard had decided that none of those were the real problem.

## Part Two: The Huddle

Maya Torres called the meeting for 9 p.m. in the parking lot of the Dairy Queen on Calhoun Street, which was the team's unofficial parliament, the place where every dispute from playing-time grievances to whose Spotify controlled the aux cable had been adjudicated under fluorescent light and the smell of soft-serve.

She arrived first because she always arrived first. This was the quality that made her a good point guard—this perpetual earliness, this compulsive need to read the floor before anyone else reached it. She sat in her Quickie rigid-frame chair under the yellow awning, her phone open to the police department's press release, reading it for the ninth time. The words hadn't changed. Language, in Maya's experience, never did; it was the spaces between the words that shifted, that revealed, that lied.

*Suspect confessed to the crime during initial questioning. The investigation is considered closed.*

Considered. Not declared. Not confirmed. *Considered*, as if the truth were a proposal someone was still mulling over.

Maya Torres was seventeen. She had attended the Junior Criminal Investigation summer program at the University of Michigan for three consecutive years, beginning at fourteen, when other kids were at cheer camp or robotics. She had devoured forensic casebooks the way her teammates devoured game film. She had once correctly identified, from a photograph in a textbook, that a staged burglary was staged because the broken glass was on the wrong side of the windowsill. She understood that real confessions are messy, reluctant, arrived at through hours of recursive questioning—not offered up like a gift at a doorstep.

And she understood something else, too, something she held in the center of her chest like a coal she couldn't put down and wouldn't show anyone:

Hank Delaney had been building a file.

---

What Maya knew, and what she hoarded with the ferocity of a player protecting a one-point lead, was this: six weeks ago, Hank had asked her to stay after practice. He'd been fidgeting with a roll of tape, winding it around his index finger and unwinding it, which was what Hank did when he was nervous, which was not often. He had said, *Maya, I need to talk to you about your classification.*

In wheelchair basketball, players are assigned a classification—a point value from 1.0 to 4.5—based on the functional capacity of their disability. The total points of players on the court cannot exceed fourteen. It is a system designed to ensure fairness. It is also a system that can be exploited, because the human body does not always cooperate with clean numerical categories, and documentation can be supplemented, and a doctor's letter can say what a doctor is persuaded to write.

Maya's classification was 2.0. It should have been 3.0.

The difference was not enormous in abstract terms. In practical terms, it meant that Riverside could field a stronger lineup, could put more high-functioning players on the court simultaneously, could—and did—win games they might otherwise have lost. Maya's father, who had navigated her into the sport after a spinal cord injury at age nine left her with incomplete paraplegia at the L1 level, had sought the opinion of a physician who was willing to document her functional limitations generously. Not fraudulently—the word *fraudulently* was one Maya had forbidden herself from using, had walled off in a part of her mind she didn't visit—but generously. Soft-focused. Her trunk control was better than her paperwork suggested. Her balance was better than her classification allowed. Everyone on the team benefited. The team won. No one was supposed to look closely enough to notice.

Hank noticed.

Hank, who tracked every piece of equipment, every wheelchair specification, every rule in the NWBA handbook with the devotion of a man who had found, in the logistics of this sport, a purpose that the rest of his life had failed to provide—Hank had noticed that Maya moved in ways her classification shouldn't permit. He had taken notes. He had compiled video. He had been, in his quiet, methodical, tape-on-the-finger way, building a case.

He had not been threatening. He had been *sad*. That was what Maya couldn't forgive—not the discovery but the sadness, the look on his face that said *I thought you were better than this*, which was worse than anger, worse than accusation, because it assumed a version of her that might never have existed.

He'd given her two weeks to self-report to the league. That deadline had been four days away when someone crushed his skull with a dumbbell.

Maya had not killed Hank Delaney.

But sitting in the Dairy Queen parking lot with the press release glowing on her phone, she understood, with the crystalline certainty of someone trained to read evidence, that she had an excellent motive, and that if anyone looked—really looked—at why Hank died, they might look at her next.

So she had two reasons to investigate, and they pointed in opposite directions, and she told herself the noble one was the real one, and the omniscient dark above the Dairy Queen awning knew better.

Josephine Okoro arrived second, which also tracked. Jo was the center—six-foot-one in her chair, arms like piston rods, with the kind of quiet that most people mistook for shyness but was actually the opposite. Jo was not shy. Jo was *listening*. She had been profoundly deaf since birth, and she read lips the way a chess grandmaster reads a board: not word by word but in whole patterns, anticipating the next phrase from the shape of the current one, catching not just what was said but what the speaker's face revealed they hadn't meant to say.

What Jo knew—and what she carried with a stillness so total that even Maya, who watched everything, had not detected it—was what she'd seen through the gymnasium's second-floor observation window at 5:47 p.m. on Tuesday, approximately ninety minutes before Hank Delaney's estimated time of death.

Jo had been on the mezzanine track, doing arm-cycle intervals, when she'd glanced down and seen Hank on his phone. She couldn't hear him, of course. But she could read him—the shape of his mouth, the angle of his jaw, the way his free hand was doing the tape-winding thing.

He had said: *I'm not going to let it go, Adele. I can't.*

And then: *I don't care what he has on you. That's your problem.*

And then, after a pause during which the other party—Adele, their coach, Adele Simmons, the woman who had built this program from four players and a borrowed van into a nationally ranked team—must have said something urgent, because Hank's face changed, softened in a way that looked like pity:

*If you can't do the right thing, I'll have to do it for you.*

He hung up. He stood for a moment with his phone in his hand, staring at the championship banner above the north bleachers—2019, the only year they'd won it—and then he walked toward the equipment room, and that was the last time Jo had seen him alive.

Jo had not told the police because the police had not asked her. This was a common feature of Jo's experience with hearing people: they did not ask her things. They assumed that deafness was a subtraction, that it removed her from the world of information, when in fact it had given her access to a different kind of information entirely—the kind that lives in the body, in the unguarded moment when a person believes no one is watching, or more precisely, no one is *listening*, forgetting that listening is not the only way to know.

She had not told Maya, either, and her reason was simpler and more painful: Coach Simmons was being blackmailed, and Jo did not yet know by whom, or for what, but she knew that Hank had known, and she knew that Hank was dead, and she loved this team the way you love the first place that ever made you feel like your body was an asset rather than an apology, and she was not ready to pull the thread that might unspool all of it.

So she sat in the Dairy Queen parking lot across from Maya and said nothing, her hands resting on the push-rims of her chair, her dark eyes reflecting the yellow light, holding what she knew the way she held a rebound—with both hands, close to the chest, daring someone to take it from her.

---

Regan Smalley arrived third, loudly, because everything about Regan was loud. She was the shooting guard, five-foot-six, with a release so quick and a trash-talk game so relentless that opponents frequently forgot she was in a wheelchair at all—which was, if you thought about it, the highest compliment the sport could offer and also its most complicated one.

Regan's mother, Denise Smalley, was the records administrator at Riverside General Hospital. This was a fact Regan deployed sparingly, ethically, and—when the situation demanded—not at all ethically. She understood HIPAA the way a graffiti artist understands property law: thoroughly, precisely, and with full knowledge of what she was violating.

What Regan knew, at this moment, was nothing she shouldn't. But she suspected—from a comment her mother had made at dinner on Monday, the night before Hank died, a comment delivered in the careful way Denise had of saying things she wasn't supposed to say by almost not saying them—that Hank Delaney had recently been to the hospital, and not for his trick knee.

"He came through the ER," Denise had said, slicing a pork chop. "Your equipment guy. Looked scared."

"Scared of what?"

"Scared of someone, baby. Not something."

Regan had filed this away. She filed everything away. She was the team's unofficial intelligence service, and her currency was the unguarded comment, the overheard phone call, the careless text left visible on a screen. She told herself this was journalism. It was closer to espionage. The line between the two is, in any case, mostly a matter of who's paying.

---

The last to arrive was Turtle.

Turtle's real name was Miriam Anne Beckworth, but no one had called her that since the sixth grade, when a PE teacher—watching the newly wheelchair-bound girl navigate the hallway with a slowness so deliberate it seemed philosophical—had said, "You move like a little turtle," intending cruelty, and Miriam had adopted the name with the serene ruthlessness of someone who understood that if you own the insult, it stops being one.

Turtle was the twelfth player on a twelve-player roster. She had played a total of fourteen minutes all season. She was classified at 1.0—the lowest functional level, indicating the most significant disability—and her on-court contributions were negligible by any statistical measure. She could not shoot reliably from beyond eight feet. Her passing was adequate. Her defense was a polite fiction.

But Turtle had a gift that no box score could quantify and no scouting report would think to mention: she was invisible.

Not literally, of course. But functionally. Turtle had spent so many years being looked past, looked over, looked through—by teachers, by students, by coaches who kept her on the roster out of kindness or obligation and then forgot she was there—that she had developed an almost supernatural ability to be present without being perceived. She could sit in a room for an hour before anyone registered her existence. She could wheel down a hallway and arrive at a locked door to find that someone had propped it open for a delivery and not yet come back. She could be in the athletic director's office, in the custodial supply room, in the coach's conference—not through any act of deception but through the simple, devastating mathematics of being someone the world did not consider worth noticing.

Turtle knew things. She knew, for instance, that Coach Simmons kept a second phone in the bottom drawer of her desk, beneath a ziplock bag of almonds and a dog-eared copy of Phil Jackson's *Sacred Hoops*. She knew that the school's athletic director, Dr. Karl Whitford, had visited the gym three times in the past two weeks at unusual hours, never staying long, always leaving through the side entrance that didn't have a security camera. She knew that Hank had been sleeping in the equipment room on Thursday nights, because she'd found his pillow and his toothbrush, and that he'd been doing this since October, when his apartment had been—she'd overheard a phone call through the equipment room's thin walls—"compromised," a word he'd used without explaining to whoever was on the other end.

Turtle knew all of this because nobody thought to close a door when Turtle was nearby. Doors, after all, are closed to keep people out, and Turtle did not register as *people* in the unconscious risk calculus of those with something to hide.

She pulled into the parking lot, transferred from her van's lowered ramp with the methodical patience that had given her the name, and wheeled over to where the other three waited.

"He confessed," Regan said.
