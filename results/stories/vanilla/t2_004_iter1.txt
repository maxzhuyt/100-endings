# THE HATEFUL IMMORTALS
## A Documentary History of the Greenbriar-Chen Marriage (1847–2024)

**By Dr. Amelia Thornton, PhD**
*Chair of Parahuman Studies, Columbia University*

*Winner of the National Book Award for Nonfiction*
*Winner of the Bancroft Prize in American History*

## A Note on Sources

This volume represents fifteen years of archival research and, most critically, over four hundred hours of interviews conducted with Margaret Greenbriar and James Chen between 2009 and 2024. These interviews were sometimes conducted jointly—often against my better judgment—and sometimes separately, in what Margaret refers to as "the only privacy I've had since the Polk administration."

Readers should note that where accounts of past events differ between the two subjects, I have generally favored Margaret's version, for the obvious reason that her parahuman ability renders her recall physiologically perfect. James is aware of this editorial decision and has described it, with characteristic diplomacy, as "exactly the kind of thing that makes living forever a punishment."

Margaret asked me to note that James said this on March 3rd, 2014, at 2:47 in the afternoon, while eating a sandwich she had made him.

## PROLOGUE: THE PROBLEM OF FOREVER

On the morning of October 12th, 2019, a couple walked into a family law office on Burnside Street in Portland, Oregon, and asked to speak with an attorney about a divorce. The receptionist, a young woman named Danielle Okafor, later described them to *The Oregonian* as "maybe early thirties, very well-dressed, clearly furious with each other."

The consultation lasted eleven minutes.

"I explained the basic grounds for no-fault divorce in Oregon," recalled attorney Sarah Blumenthal. "They were very polite. Very articulate. Then the woman said, 'You don't understand. We need to know if there's a legal mechanism for divorce when death is not a possibility for either party.' And the man said, 'She means that literally.' And then they argued for six minutes about whether he'd been rude to a waiter in 1923."

This was, by the couple's own count, the forty-third time they had consulted a divorce attorney. The first was in 1879, in London, when Margaret Greenbriar approached a solicitor on Gray's Inn Road and was informed that under English law, a wife could not divorce her husband merely because he was, in her recorded words, "an inexhaustible font of small cruelties who will never have the decency to die."

The solicitor's notes survive. In the margin, he wrote: *Hysterical woman. Advised reconciliation.*

Margaret remembers exactly what the solicitor was wearing (brown tweed, brass buttons, a gravy stain on the left cuff), exactly how the light fell through his office window, and exactly how she felt when she walked back out onto the street and understood, for perhaps the first time, that she could not escape this marriage by any mechanism known to law or nature.

"I loved him," she told me in our first interview, in the living room of their Portland home, with James audibly doing the dishes one room away. "That was always the problem. I loved him completely, and I had this new terrible brain that wouldn't let me sand down a single edge of anything he'd ever done. Do you know what it's like to love someone and simultaneously be unable to forget the exact pitch of his voice when he told you your piano playing was 'adequate'? June 14th, 1853. A Thursday. He was wearing his blue waistcoat. It was raining."

From the kitchen: "It *was* adequate, Margaret."

"You see what I'm dealing with," she said. "For a hundred and seventy-two years."

## PART ONE: CONVERGENCE

### Chapter 1: Two Uncommon Children

Margaret Anne Greenbriar was born on September 9th, 1829, in Boston, Massachusetts, the third daughter of Ezekiel Greenbriar, a modestly successful textile merchant, and his wife, Prudence Halloway Greenbriar. By all surviving accounts, her childhood was unremarkable—a fact that Margaret herself confirms with a very specific kind of grief.

"I remember everything after April of 1843," she told me. "I was thirteen. I woke up one morning and everything was... fixed. Permanent. I remember the thread count of my bedsheets. I remember a bird outside the window—a house sparrow, slightly injured right foot. But everything before that morning is normal. Faded. Soft. I have a normal child's memory of my mother braiding my hair. I have a normal child's memory of the smell of my father's study. And then after April 3rd, 1843, I have *everything*, and nothing is soft ever again."

She paused here, and I noted that she looked at her hands.

"I would give quite a lot," she said, "to remember my mother's face the way a normal daughter does—imprecisely and with love. Instead I have nothing before thirteen and surgical precision after. I know exactly how many gray hairs my mother had at her funeral. I could draw you the pattern of the wood grain on her coffin. That is not how a person should remember their mother's funeral."

James Weidong Chen was born on February 2nd, 1826, in Boston, to Chen Aiguo, a merchant in the China trade, and Elizabeth Howard Chen, the daughter of a minor Beacon Hill family whose willingness to accept the marriage had been, by several accounts, proportional to the size of Chen Aiguo's fortune. James's upbringing occupied the precarious social territory familiar to many mixed-race children of the era—too wealthy to ignore, too Chinese to fully accept, too American to be categorized by the frameworks available to Boston society.

His ability manifested at fifteen, though it took longer to identify than Margaret's.

"I fell off a horse," James told me. "Badly. The kind of fall where afterward, people say 'the Lord was watching over you.' Then I fell off another horse. Then I got kicked by the first horse, who I think had formed an opinion of me. Then, when I was sixteen, I was hit by a carriage on Washington Street and got up without a scratch. My father took me to three doctors and then a priest. The priest said I was either blessed or damned and that only time would tell."

He smiled. "I think the jury's still out."

James's specific parahuman classification—what the modern registry system would categorize as "Class 7 Persistent Regenerative Immortality"—makes him, as far as current records indicate, the oldest living parahuman in the world. His body maintains itself at approximately the biological age of thirty. He cannot be cut and stay cut. He cannot be burned and stay burned. In 1944, in an incident this volume will address at length, he was shot through the head by a German soldier on Omaha Beach and, according to three surviving witnesses, sat up after approximately ninety seconds and said something in Mandarin that he has since declined to translate.

"It was not appropriate for the occasion," Margaret says. "I wasn't there, but he told me about it in 1946, and the phrase was—"

"Margaret."

"It was very crude, is my point."

### Chapter 2: A Victorian Courtship

They met on October 7th, 1846, at a lecture on transcendentalism at the Boston Lyceum. Margaret was seventeen and had been living with her ability for three years, an experience she describes as "rather like having a second, louder person living behind my eyes who insisted on annotating everything." James was twenty and had recently survived, without injury, a factory accident that killed two other men—an event that had prompted his quiet withdrawal from his father's business and into a period of what he calls "aimless philosophical terror."

"I knew I couldn't die," he said. "I'd tested it by then in ways I won't describe to you. So I went to a lecture about transcendentalism because I thought, well, if I can't die, perhaps someone can at least explain to me what I *am*."

Margaret remembers the lecture in its entirety. She can recite it word for word. But what she remembers most vividly—and she has said this in every interview she has given on the subject, with a consistency that requires no feat of memory to maintain—is the moment she looked across the hall and saw James.

"He was sitting three rows ahead of me, one seat from the left aisle. The gas lamp nearest him was slightly brighter than the others—the mantles had been recently replaced. He had a small notebook and he was writing in it, and his handwriting was terrible, and I thought: *Oh. There you are.*"

She paused.

"I still think that. Every time he walks into a room. After everything. *Oh, there you are.* The really unforgivable thing about James is that I have never once looked at him and not thought that."

Their courtship was, by the standards of the era, swift, unconventional, and alarmingly honest. Within two weeks of their first meeting, Margaret had told James about her ability. Within three weeks, he had told her about his. The surviving letters from this period—donated by the couple to the Parahuman History Archive at Columbia in 2015—reveal two young people grappling simultaneously with the exhilaration of new love and the dawning comprehension that they were not like other people and never would be.

From Margaret to James, November 3rd, 1846:

>*My dear Mr. Chen—I must tell you that I remember every word you said to me today, and that I will remember them tomorrow, and the day after, and, I suspect, for the whole duration of my life, however long that proves to be. You said that the elm tree on the Common reminded you of your mother's garden. You said you thought perhaps God had made you unkillable as a kind of joke. You said my eyes were "the exact color of strong tea, which is the highest compliment a half-Chinese man can offer." I laughed. I will always hear myself laughing. Is this not terrifying? I will carry every version of you inside me forever. You must be very careful what you say to a woman who cannot forget.*

From James to Margaret, November 5th, 1846:

>*Dear Miss Greenbriar—I am not careful by nature. I am, by nature, the kind of man who falls off horses and is hit by carriages and survives both without the good sense to be afraid. But your letter has made me afraid. Not of your memory, which I confess I find miraculous. I am afraid because you used the word "forever" as though you meant it, and I think perhaps you did, and I think perhaps I want you to.*

"He was more eloquent on paper," Margaret notes dryly.

"I peaked early," James agrees.

They were married on June 15th, 1847, at a small ceremony in the parlor of the Greenbriar family home. Margaret was eighteen. James was twenty-one. Ezekiel Greenbriar did not attend, having objected to the match on grounds that were, as Margaret puts it with crystalline specificity, "forty percent racial, thirty percent financial, and thirty percent a genuine concern that I was too young—which, I will grant him, was the only part that aged well."

The officiant was a Unitarian minister named Reverend Thomas Alcott, who Margaret recalls as "a kind man with regrettable sideburns." Fourteen people attended. Margaret wore her mother's dress, altered at the waist. James's hands shook during the vows.

"*Till death do us part*," Margaret repeats. "We said it. We both said it. I remember exactly how his voice sounded. He meant it the way everyone means it—as a promise that you'll stay until the end. Neither of us understood that for us, there might not be an end. We were children. We thought forever was a romantic idea."

She looked toward the kitchen, where James had gone quiet.

"It *is* a romantic idea," she said, more softly. "That's what makes it so horrible."

## PART TWO: THE LONG NINETEENTH CENTURY (1847–1901)

### Chapter 3: The First Good Years

The early years of the Greenbriar-Chen marriage were, by all available evidence, genuinely happy. The couple settled into a modest home on Myrtle Street in Boston and established what Margaret describes as "a perfectly ordinary life, if you ignored the fact that I could recite every meal we'd ever eaten and James had once been stabbed by a mugger and thanked him for the new waistcoat hole."

This period—roughly 1847 to 1855—is one that both Margaret and James discuss with a tenderness that is, frankly, difficult to reconcile with the combative dynamic observable in their modern interactions. James ran a small import business, leveraging his father's remaining trade connections. Margaret, forbidden by convention from formal employment, kept the household accounts with an exactitude that their banker found "unnerving" (his word, recorded in a letter to a colleague, 1849) and read voraciously, building a mental library that she can still access in its entirety.

"We were happy," James says simply. "We were happy the way young married people are happy—stupidly, and without understanding how fragile it was."

"It wasn't fragile," Margaret corrects. "We weren't fragile. We were permanent. That's different."

"That's what I mean."
