# THE SUTTON COUNTY TRANSCRIPTS

## Being a Complete Compilation of Official Court Records, Personal Audio Recordings, and Annotated Documents Recovered from the Residence of Margaret E. Landry (1949–1989?), Court Stenographer, Sutton County Circuit Court, Commonwealth of Virginia

**Compiled and Arranged by the Sutton County Historical Preservation Society, 2019**

**Digitization funded by a grant from the Haroldson Foundation for Archival Studies**

---

## EDITOR'S FOREWORD

The materials that follow were recovered in March 2019 during the renovation of the former Landry residence at 414 Persimmon Lane, Sutton, Virginia. They were found inside a sealed crawlspace beneath the kitchen floor, stored in three waterproof ammunition crates. The crawlspace appeared to have been deliberately concealed—the original access panel had been drywalled over, mudded, and painted to match the surrounding wall, with a heavy Welsh dresser positioned in front of it. The renovation contractor, Mr. Dale Fentress, discovered the space only because his demolition work on the kitchen's load-bearing wall revealed irregularities in the stud framing.

The crates contained:

1. Twenty-three stenographer's notebooks containing Margaret Landry's original shorthand records from the 1987 trial of *Commonwealth v. Eleanor Vance* (Case No. 87-CR-4401), interspersed with extensive marginalia in both shorthand and longhand.
2. Forty-one standard-format cassette tapes, labeled in Landry's handwriting with dates ranging from September 14, 1987 to March 2, 1989.
3. A three-ring binder containing photocopied county employment records, newspaper clippings, hand-drawn charts and diagrams, and thirty-seven pages of handwritten analysis.
4. A sealed manila envelope containing a single VHS tape (unlabeled, contents corrupted beyond recovery).
5. A King James Bible (1972 printing) with extensive annotations and several pages removed.

Margaret Landry was reported missing by her sister, Constance Landry-Webb, on March 19, 1989. She was declared legally dead in 1996. No body was ever recovered. Her case remains officially open.

The Historical Preservation Society presents these materials with minimal editorial intervention. Transcriptions of handwritten marginalia appear in **[brackets]**. Audio diary entries have been transcribed by volunteer staff. Ellipses within court transcripts indicate passages omitted for length; the complete transcripts are available at the Sutton County Courthouse, Record Room B. Descriptions of Landry's non-textual annotations—drawings, diagrams, stains—appear in {curled brackets}.

We have made no attempt to verify Landry's claims or to editorialize on their plausibility. We note only that the documents are authentic, the handwriting is confirmed as hers, and the court transcripts, where they can be compared against the official record, match exactly—with the exception of certain passages Landry marked as omitted or altered in the official filing, which we have been unable to independently confirm or deny, as the relevant sections of the official transcript were damaged by water intrusion in the courthouse basement in 1994.

A note on the audio transcriptions: several tapes contain background sounds that our volunteers have described variously and inconsistently. We have marked these as [INDISTINCT BACKGROUND SOUND] and do not speculate on their nature.

—Helen Marsh-Calder, Director
Sutton County Historical Preservation Society
August 2019

## PART ONE: BEFORE THE PATTERN

### AUDIO DIARY — TAPE 1, SIDE A
### September 14, 1987

[TAPE BEGINS MID-SENTENCE]

—first day assigned to the Vance trial and I already want to ask Judge Alderman for reassignment. I won't, because Janet took early retirement specifically to avoid this one, and Gloria has her carpel tunnel, and there's nobody left but me. But I want to.

The courtroom was full. Standing room. People from the churches, people from the television stations—Roanoke, Richmond, one crew I didn't recognize. The families of the three children were in the first two rows behind the prosecution, and I will say this only once on these tapes and then I will try not to think about it again: the sound Mrs. Delacroix made when the charges were read, that low sound, that was not crying. I have been a court stenographer for fourteen years and I have heard parents of murdered children before. That sound was not grief. I don't know what it was. It was the sound of something that doesn't have a name.

[PAUSE — 8 SECONDS]

Eleanor Vance was... composed. More composed than her attorney, frankly. Public defender, young man named Harwick, looked about twenty-five, looked like he wanted to be anywhere else. She sat with her hands folded and watched everything. I noticed her eyes. They moved— they moved the way a person's eyes move when they're reading, left to right, left to right, like the air in the courtroom contained text only she could see. I made a note. I don't know why I made a note. I'm a stenographer, not an investigative journalist. But I made a note.

The charges: three counts of first-degree murder. Caroline Oakes, age seven. James Oakes, age nine. DeShawn Ridley, age four. All children in the foster care system, all placed in homes supervised by Mrs. Vance in her capacity as a Sutton County Department of Social Services caseworker. All found dead in the home of foster parents Gerald and Patricia Blaylock between December 3 and December 7, 1986. Cause of death in each case: exsanguination.

The prosecution's theory—and this is ADA Calhoun, Richard Calhoun, who I've worked with maybe fifty times and who I've always found to be a serious and careful man—the prosecution's theory is that Eleanor Vance, during a period of what Calhoun called "occult psychosis," ritually murdered the children as part of satanic worship practices. That she used her position at Social Services to select victims. That the Blaylocks were, as Calhoun put it, "complicit or coerced."

The Blaylocks are not on trial. Gerald Blaylock is in the state psychiatric hospital. Patricia Blaylock hanged herself on February 14, 1987, in the Sutton County Jail.

I am going to try to do my job. My job is to create an accurate and complete record. That is all.

[LONG PAUSE — 15 SECONDS]

I should note for the record—my record, nobody else's—that this is a new practice for me, these tapes. I bought the recorder at the RadioShack on Route 11 this afternoon. I don't entirely know why. Something about this one. Something about the way the air felt in that courtroom. I have never felt the need to keep a personal record before. I am not a person who keeps diaries.

[TAPE ENDS]

### OFFICIAL COURT TRANSCRIPT — EXCERPT
### *Commonwealth of Virginia v. Eleanor May Vance*
### Case No. 87-CR-4401
### Sutton County Circuit Court
### The Honorable Robert H. Alderman, Presiding
### September 15, 1987 — Trial Day 2, Morning Session

**[PROSECUTION OPENING STATEMENT — ADA RICHARD CALHOUN]**

. . .

CALHOUN: Ladies and gentlemen of the jury, what happened in that house on Devlin Road was not random. It was not the product of mental illness, though the defense will try to convince you otherwise. It was deliberate. It was planned. And it was evil.

Eleanor Vance selected Caroline Oakes, James Oakes, and DeShawn Ridley. She selected them using criteria that we will demonstrate through testimony and documentary evidence. She placed them in the Blaylock home knowing—*knowing*—what would happen to them there. And on the nights of December 3rd, December 5th, and December 7th, she returned to that home and she participated in their murders.

The evidence will show that these children were killed according to a pattern. Every other night. In descending order of age. Using methods that correspond— and I recognize how this sounds, I recognize that we are in a court of law and not a church, but the evidence will speak for itself—using methods that correspond to ritual practices described in texts seized from Mrs. Vance's home. Texts she personally annotated. Texts she ordered through the mail from suppliers in New York, San Francisco, and, notably, a post office box in Bern, Switzerland that the postal service has been unable to associate with any registered business or individual.

. . .

**[DEFENSE OPENING STATEMENT — PUBLIC DEFENDER THOMAS HARWICK]**

HARWICK: Members of the jury, my client is innocent. And I want to acknowledge something right at the top, because Mr. Calhoun won't: we are in the middle of a national hysteria. In the last four years, there have been over one hundred cases across this country in which daycare workers, teachers, social workers, and parents have been accused of ritual satanic abuse. In McMartin, California. In Kern County. In Jordan, Minnesota. In case after case, the accusations have followed the same pattern: fantastical claims, coerced testimony, a community in the grip of fear looking for someone to blame.

Eleanor Vance is a scapegoat. She is a woman who has devoted her life to protecting children, and she is being sacrificed— if you'll pardon the word—on the altar of this community's grief and terror. Three children are dead. That is real. That is a tragedy. But the person who killed them is not sitting at this table. The evidence will show—

THE DEFENDANT: They're here.

THE COURT: Mrs. Vance—

THE DEFENDANT: They're in the room right now. They've been in this room since it was built. Can't you feel the floor? The floor is wrong. The floor has been wrong since 1843.

HARWICK: Eleanor—

THE DEFENDANT: [SPEAKING IN RAISED VOICE] I am trying to tell you what happened. I have been trying to tell you. None of you are listening because you think I did it. I didn't do it. I *found* it. I found the system in the records. The placement records. Every eighteen years. Every eighteen years the same addresses come up and children go into those houses and the files close and nobody— nobody— you should look at the files. Pull the files from 1968. Pull the files from 1950. Pull the files from 1932. You'll see the same houses. You'll see—

THE COURT: Mrs. Vance, I must insist—

THE DEFENDANT: [SHOUTING] *Testor quod non auditur. Scriba fert testimonium. Scriba accipit munus.*

THE COURT: Bailiff.

THE DEFENDANT: [CONTINUING WHILE BEING RESTRAINED] *Scriba! Scriba accipit—*

[DEFENDANT REMOVED FROM COURTROOM]

THE COURT: We will take a thirty-minute recess. Mr. Harwick, I expect your client to be under control when we resume, or I will have her bound and gagged, and I have done it before.

HARWICK: Yes, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Court is in recess.

. . .

### AUDIO DIARY — TAPE 2, SIDE A
### September 17, 1987

The prosecution called Dr. Alice Mooring today. Forensic pathologist from the state medical examiner's office. I have transcribed a great deal of medical testimony in my career. I have a strong stomach. I am a professional.

I had to stop the machine twice. Not because of the details of the children's deaths, though those were— I had to stop because of what Dr. Mooring said about the blood.

Not the blood loss. The exsanguination. That's in the official record and it's what you'd expect. The children were cut and they bled to death. That's horrible enough.

It's what she said about the *pattern* of the blood at the scene. She described it as "gravitationally inconsistent." She said the blood on the walls of the basement room had flowed *upward*. Not spatter—she was clear about that, this wasn't a spatter pattern, this was flow, the way blood flows down a surface with gravity, except it went up. Up the walls. Across the ceiling. She said she'd initially assumed the children had been killed upside down, suspended somehow, but the lividity patterns on the bodies were inconsistent with inversion.

Calhoun asked her for her professional explanation.

She said she didn't have one.

Harwick objected to the entire line of testimony as prejudicial and speculative. Alderman overruled. Harwick looked like he might be sick.

I went home and I sat in my kitchen for a very long time. I ate a bowl of cereal at eleven o'clock at night and I listened to the house make the sounds a house makes, and I will admit that I did not enjoy those sounds the way I usually do.

[PAUSE — 6 SECONDS]

Something else. I want to say this out loud so I can hear how it sounds.

During Eleanor Vance's outburst on the fifteenth—the Latin, the shouting before they removed her—I was recording. Obviously. That's my job. But I also had this tape recorder in my bag, running. I'd left it on by accident after recording my notes during the lunch recess. So I have a recording of the outburst. Not high quality—it was muffled, it was in my bag under my cardigan—but I have it.

I played it back tonight. Just to see. Just because— I don't know why. Something about the way the words sounded. When Eleanor shouted, the words had a cadence that was— wrong. Not wrong like a non-Latin speaker trying to pronounce Latin. Wrong like the rhythm was carrying something else inside it. Like there was a sound underneath the sound.

So I played it back. And then—and I'm embarrassed to even say this—I played it backward. I don't know why. I think because of something I half-remembered from church, from Reverend Polk's sermons about rock music and backward messages, which I always thought was nonsense, and I still think is nonsense, and yet.

[LONG PAUSE — 12 SECONDS]

Backward. Reversed. When the full outburst is reversed, most of it is just reversed speech. Garble. Obviously. But there's a section— the section where she's shouting while the bailiff is restraining her, where her voice goes high and strained— in that section,
