# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## A Bureaucratic Odyssey in Conditions of Terminal Redundancy

---

## Part One: Regarding the Matter of Incorrect Filing (see Appendix Never)

In the beginning—and here we must specify *which* beginning, because the Auditors recognized at minimum eleven, each requiring its own dedicated intake form—there was the Universe, and it was full of hydrogen, and it was adequate.

Then the hydrogen did what hydrogen, left unsupervised, will inevitably do. It clumped. It fused. It became stars, planets, oceans, amino acids, opinions, and, in what even the most generous observer would characterize as a regrettable chain of events, middle management. Somewhere in the gap between amino acids and opinions, life had appeared—abundant, varied, and almost universally unaware that it existed at the pleasure of an administrative apparatus so vast, so ancient, and so profoundly committed to procedural compliance that it had, over the course of several billion years, developed the institutional personality of a passport office on the Friday before a long weekend.

The Auditors had always been there. Or rather, they had always been *here*, which was a different place than *there*, and if you confused the two on your filing you'd be looking at a six-to-eight-cosmic-cycle processing delay and a Conditions of Residency review that nobody—and the narrator means *nobody*, not even species with distributed consciousness and eleven-dimensional perception—had ever successfully completed on the first attempt.

They were not cruel. This is important to establish early, because cruelty implies intention, and intention implies a degree of personal investment that the Auditors found professionally distasteful. They were *thorough*. They were *consistent*. When they had processed the Keth-Varandi Hegemony—a civilization spanning nine hundred star systems, whose art could make black holes weep and whose mathematics had unlocked the deep grammar of reality itself—they had done so not out of malice but because the Keth-Varandi had failed to file a Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence within the prescribed window, which had been clearly posted in the public notice dimension, which the Keth-Varandi could not perceive, which was not the Auditors' problem.

The form had been due on what humans would call a Tuesday.

The Keth-Varandi Hegemony had ceased to exist on what humans would call a Wednesday.

The Auditors had then processed the paperwork related to the cessation on what humans would call the heat death of that particular local cluster, which happened to fall on a Thursday, and which the Auditors considered a reasonable turnaround time.

---

Humanity's first contact with the Auditors occurred on March 7th, 2247, at approximately 3:42 PM Greenwich Mean Time, which was a time zone that no longer corresponded to any physical location on Earth but which the International Bureau of Standards had stubbornly continued to maintain because changing it would require updating too many databases.

This fact—humanity's devotion to an arbitrary temporal reference frame anchored to a meridian running through a building that by 2247 served primarily as a café specializing in synthetic scones—would later prove to be either its greatest vulnerability or its single most important cultural asset, depending on which character in this account you asked, and when, and whether they had recently eaten.

The Auditors arrived not in ships but in *notifications*. Every digital display on Earth, every personal device, every heads-up display in every helmet of every asteroid miner in the belt, simultaneously displayed the same message:

---

**ATTENTION: SPECIES DESIGNATION [H. SAPIENS / SOL-3 / REFERENCE CODE: 77281-QX-PROVISIONAL]**

**YOUR CIVILIZATION HAS BEEN FLAGGED FOR ROUTINE PROCESSING UNDER GALACTIC ADMINISTRATIVE CODE SECTION 4,417,882,003, SUBSECTION 12(b): "SPECIES EXHIBITING INDICATORS OF IMMINENT INTERSTELLAR CAPABILITY."**

**PROCESSING WILL COMMENCE IN [30] STANDARD ROTATIONAL PERIODS OF YOUR PRIMARY BODY.**

**TO FILE AN OBJECTION, PLEASE SUBMIT FORM 27-B: APPLICATION FOR CONTINUED EXISTENCE, IN TRIPLICATE, TO YOUR NEAREST REGIONAL PROCESSING NEXUS.**

**NOTE: REGIONAL PROCESSING NEXI ARE LOCATED IN DIMENSIONS NOT ACCESSIBLE TO SPECIES BELOW INTERSTELLAR CAPABILITY THRESHOLD. WE APOLOGIZE FOR ANY INCONVENIENCE.**

**THIS IS AN AUTOMATED NOTIFICATION. PLEASE DO NOT REPLY.**

---

Humanity did what humanity does when confronted with an incomprehensible threat delivered in the language of bureaucracy. First, it panicked. Then it formed committees. Then the committees formed subcommittees, and the subcommittees issued preliminary findings, and the preliminary findings were leaked to media outlets who reported them inaccurately, and someone started a petition, and someone else started a different petition opposing the first petition, and a not-insignificant number of people simply refused to believe any of it was real because the font on the notification looked "cheap."

Thirty days later, the Auditors processed Earth.

"Processed" is doing a great deal of work in that sentence, so let us be precise: the Auditors did not destroy Earth. Destruction was a separate department (Forms 40 through 40-ZZZ, depending on the method requested and whether the applicant wanted to preserve mineral rights). What the Auditors did was more administrative. They *archived* it. Earth was still there, technically, in the same way that a document placed in a filing cabinet in a sub-basement of a condemned building in a city that no one visits in a country that has been redistricted out of existence is still, technically, *there*. The planet continued to orbit its star. Its tectonic plates continued their ancient, grinding waltz. Its oceans rose and fell with the tides.

Nothing lived on it.

Nothing *could* live on it. The Auditors had revoked its Habitability Certification. The chemistry still worked—atoms still bonded, water still flowed—but the paperwork authorizing biological processes to occur on that specific body in that specific orbit had been rescinded, and biology, it turned out, was a stickler for paperwork.

Of Earth's eleven billion inhabitants, three thousand survived.

They survived because of a ship—the *UNSS Perseverance*, a generation vessel that had been under construction in lunar orbit for forty years, humanity's great gamble on the stars, a cathedral of ambition and engineering that was, at the moment of Earth's archival, approximately 73% complete. It had engines but no adequate radiation shielding on decks nine through fourteen. It had a hydroponic bay but no viable soil cultures. It had a bridge with a captain's chair that still had the manufacturer's plastic wrap on the armrests because the interior design subcontractor had gone bankrupt in 2245 and the replacement firm was still in the bidding process.

The *Perseverance* survived because it was in space, and space, bureaucratically speaking, was a commons—administered not by the regional office that handled Sol-3 but by the Interstitial Transit Authority, which was technically a different department, and which had not yet received the memo regarding Sol-3's change in status because inter-departmental communications were handled by a courier system that had been underfunded since the previous cosmic fiscal cycle.

Three thousand people lived because of a memo that was stuck in someone's inbox.

The narrator would like you to sit with that for a moment.

## Part Two: In Which We Meet the Relevant Parties (See Attached Dossiers, Filed Under "Unlikely")

The three thousand souls aboard the *Perseverance* were not, as one might romantically imagine, the best of humanity. They were not its brightest scientists, its bravest warriors, its wisest philosophers. They were the people who happened to be on or near the moon on March 7th—construction workers, engineers, a Luna City tourism delegation from Osaka, forty-seven members of a competitive zero-gravity volleyball league who had been mid-tournament, a visiting school group of nine-year-olds from the Reykjavik Academy for Gifted and Determined Children, eleven confused wedding guests whose celebration on the Lunar Regolith Pavilion had been interrupted mid-toast, a woman who had come to the moon to break up with her boyfriend in a setting dramatic enough to match her feelings about it, and her boyfriend, who had not yet been broken up with and remained in an agonizing state of romantic superposition for approximately six hours longer than he otherwise would have, because his girlfriend decided the literal end of the world constituted extenuating circumstances and she could do it next week.

She did not, in fact, do it next week. She did not do it for another eleven years. But that is not yet our concern.

Among the three thousand, the following individuals will matter:

---

**LINDA HARGROVE**, age 54 at the time of Earth's archival, formerly of the Department of Motor Vehicles, Sacramento Office, Window 7.

Linda had spent twenty-six years at the DMV. She had renewed licenses. She had processed registrations. She had stared into the faces of people who had waited three hours to be told they were in the wrong line and had said, with practiced and genuine compassion, "I understand your frustration, but I'm going to need you to take a number from the machine by the door and wait for your number to be called, and I promise we'll get this sorted out." She said this an average of forty-one times per day. She said it with kindness every single time, and she meant it every single time, and this combination of patience and sincerity had carved grooves in her soul that would turn out to be exactly the right shape for the task the universe was about to assign her.

Linda had been on the moon visiting her sister, who worked in lunar sanitation. Linda had not wanted to visit the moon. She did not like low gravity—it made the fluid distribution in her body feel "wrong," and she was someone who trusted her body's opinions about things. But her sister had asked, and Linda was someone who showed up when asked.

She was also someone who, upon learning that the Earth had been archived by an incomprehensible cosmic bureaucracy, had taken one long breath, closed her eyes, and said: "Okay. Who do I need to talk to?"

Not *why*. Not *how*. Not *this can't be happening*. Who do I need to talk to. Because Linda Hargrove had learned, across twenty-six years at Window 7, that every system, no matter how cruel or arbitrary or broken, had a person in it somewhere who could help you, and the only thing you had to do was find them and be patient and have your documents in order.

She would become humanity's advocate. Not because anyone elected her or appointed her. Because she started filling out forms, and nobody else knew how.

---

**CAPTAIN DEVIKA SHAH**, age 48, commander of the *UNSS Perseverance*, a title that had been largely ceremonial before Earth's death and had become considerably less so after.

Captain Shah was a Navy woman. Her Navy no longer existed. Her chain of command terminated in a void that began approximately 384,400 kilometers below her feet. She had been trained to respond to crises with protocols, and the protocols she had been given made certain assumptions about the nature of reality that were no longer operative—assumptions like "Earth exists" and "reinforcements are possible" and "the laws of physics are not subject to administrative review."

She was a woman who believed in cause and effect, in action and consequence, in the fundamental dignity of rational response to material conditions. She believed that the universe was, at its base, a comprehensible place, governed by laws that could be discovered, described, and relied upon.

The notion that the universe ran on triplicate offended her on a level so deep it was almost spiritual.

"I reject the premise," she said, seventeen years later, when Linda placed the first successful interdimensional form submission on her desk and tried to explain that the Auditors had responded with a request for additional documentation. "I reject it, Linda. I categorically reject the premise that the continued existence of the human species is a *permitting issue*."

"I understand your frustration," Linda said, because she always said that, and she always meant it. "But I'm going to need you to sign on page four, initial on pages seven through twelve, and provide a DNA sample for the species verification appendix."

"I won't."

"The DNA sample can be saliva."

"It's not about the *saliva*, Linda."

"I know. But we need the saliva."

Captain Shah gave the saliva. She always gave the saliva. She objected, she rejected, she categorically refused, and then she signed on page four, because Linda asked, and because somewhere beneath her objections, Captain Shah understood that Linda was the only person on the ship who seemed to have any idea what she was doing, and in the absence of a functioning chain of command, competence was its own authority.

---

**OSEI MENSAH-CARTER**, age 9 at the time of Earth's archival, age 26 at the time this account primarily concerns itself with, member of the Reykjavik Academy school group, currently the ship's most prolific and least comprehensible artist.

Osei had been drawing since before Earth died. His mother, who had not been on the moon and who therefore no longer existed in any way that the Auditors recognized as administratively valid, had given him a box of colored pencils for his seventh birthday—real wood, real pigment, the kind that cost more than they should have because humanity had, by 2247, largely transitioned to digital media and the few remaining pencil manufacturers had pivoted to luxury goods. The pencils had survived. The box had not. The mother had not. Osei carried the pencils in a leather pouch his adoptive father—one of the wedding guests, a cellist named Marco—had made from the cover of a book that nobody could remember the title of anymore.

Osei drew constantly. He drew on paper when paper was available, on walls when it wasn't, on the inside of maintenance panels in the engine compartment where he was not supposed to be and where he regularly was. He drew things he had never seen: landscapes of planets he had never visited, organisms that did not correspond to any known taxonomy, architectural structures that seemed to fold in on themselves in ways that made the ship's engineers first uncomfortable and then, upon prolonged examination, dizzy.

He also drew forms.

Not intentionally. Not consciously. But among his thousands of drawings—filed, catalogued, and archived by Marco, who treated his son's art with the reverent diligence of a museum curator—there appeared, with increasing frequency, pages covered in symbols that were not any human language, arranged in grids and tables and nested hierarchies that bore an unsettling resemblance to paperwork.

When Linda, in Year Fourteen, finally established communication with the Auditors and received her first batch of actual forms—forms that existed in eleven dimensions and had to be collapsed into two-dimensional representations before they could be printed, a process that made the ship's printer emit a sound Linda would later describe as "screaming, but politely"—she recognized something.

She went to Osei's quarters. She laid his drawings on the table next to the Auditors' forms.

They were not identical. But they rhymed.

"Osei," she said, carefully, because she was a woman who had spent her career being careful with people, "do you know what these are?"

Osei, then twenty-three, looked at his own drawings as if seeing them for the first time. He looked at the Auditors' forms. He picked up a pencil—not one of his mother's; those were down to stubs now, and he only used them for things that mattered—and made a small correction to one of the Auditor forms. A single line. A curve where there had been an angle.

Three days later, the Auditors sent their first acknowledgment of humanity's existence since Earth's archival.

It was a receipt.

It said: **SUBMISSION RECEIVED. PROCESSING.**

Linda framed it.
