# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## A Novel (Excerpt: Chapters 1–6)

---

## CHAPTER ONE: In Which a Clerical Error Destroys Only Most of a Planet

The universe, it turned out, had a filing system.

This was not the revelation that humanity had hoped for. Across the long centuries of pointing radio telescopes at the sky and composing earnest golden records for the benefit of hypothetical alien pen pals, human beings had imagined many possible shapes for the cosmos. A vast and indifferent emptiness, mostly. Perhaps a teeming jungle of competing biologies. The more optimistic among them had pictured a kind of galactic neighborhood, full of wise elders ready to share clean energy solutions and the cure for cancer. The more pessimistic had imagined predators lurking in the dark. Almost nobody had imagined middle management.

Yet here it was.

The Auditors of the Galactic Administrative Authority—or, more precisely, the Provisional Continuity Bureau of the Sub-Spiral Compliance Division (Orion Arm), Operating Under Emergency Protocols Established by Former Authority [SEE FILE: REDACTED/ARCHIVED/ERROR 6.02×10²³]—had first contacted humanity on a Tuesday. This felt cosmically appropriate, as Tuesdays have always been the day when the truly miserable bureaucratic things happen. Nobody ever gets audited on a Friday.

The message had arrived simultaneously in every language spoken on Earth, though "message" was generous. It was a form. Specifically, it was Form 1-A: Notice of Pending Civilizational Review, and it was four hundred and seventeen pages long in its English rendering, which was actually the shortest version because English had a convenient tolerance for sentences that sounded precise but committed to nothing. The Mandarin version was six hundred pages. The German version was nine hundred and twelve, because the Auditors' translation matrix had correctly identified that German compound nouns were the closest any human language came to their own classificatory system, and had gotten enthusiastic.

The form had materialized on every digital screen, every sheet of paper near a printer, every chalkboard in every classroom, and, in one particularly memorable case, in the soap foam of a bathtub in Reykjavik, where a retired fisherman named Björn had been trying to have a quiet soak. Björn had read the first three pages, concluded it was probably a tax thing, and drained the tub.

He was, in a sense, the most correct person on Earth that day.

Governments panicked. Scientists analyzed. The United Nations convened an emergency session that devolved into a twelve-hour argument about which committee had jurisdiction over extraterrestrial compliance notices, which was—though nobody appreciated it at the time—the most productive possible response, because it was exactly the kind of behavior the Auditors understood.

The form requested, in language that managed to be simultaneously crystal clear and utterly opaque, that the human species submit within one standard galactic cycle (approximately fourteen Earth months) a complete accounting of its civilizational outputs, resource utilization patterns, territorial claims (limited to one stellar system, pending review), and a Statement of Purpose explaining, quote, "the ongoing justification for the allocation of thermodynamic resources to the applying species' continued organized existence."

In other words: Why should you keep existing? Please answer in triplicate.

Humanity did not submit the form.

This was understandable. Fourteen months is not a long time to compose a species-wide existential justification, particularly when most of those months were consumed by geopolitical arguments about who should write it, in what language, and whether it should mention God. Several nations submitted their own versions unilaterally. The United States sent something that read like a combination of a college admissions essay and a defense budget proposal. France sent a philosophical treatise. Brazil sent a poem. Russia did not acknowledge receipt of the form, on the official position that it was a NATO psyop, and then quietly submitted a very thorough response through back channels. North Korea submitted a form claiming to have already completed all requirements and requesting the immediate audit of South Korea.

None of it mattered, because all of it was, technically, a response to Form 1-A, and what should have been submitted was Form 1-A(R): Acknowledgment of Receipt of Notice of Pending Civilizational Review, which was a different document entirely, and which nobody filed.

The deadline passed.

Sixty-three hours later, the Auditors arrived.

---

"Arrived" was the wrong word, but human languages didn't have a right one.

The Auditors did not travel through space. They did not descend from the sky in ships. They simply *were*, suddenly, in every location that mattered—which is to say, in every location where a significant administrative decision had ever been made. They appeared in capitals and courthouses, in central banks and census offices. They manifested as presences that the human eye refused to fully register, like trying to stare at a word you've just misspelled until the wrongness resolves itself, except it never did. Those who saw them—or experienced them, or suffered them—described the sensation differently. Some said it was like standing in a room where all the furniture had been moved two inches to the left. Others said it felt like receiving a letter you'd been dreading but had never been sent. A surprisingly large number of people said it was exactly like being at the DMV.

The Auditors did not communicate. They processed.

It happened in waves, over a period of eleven days. Cities went first—not destroyed, not burned, not conquered. *Filed*. The buildings remained. The infrastructure remained. The pigeons, for reasons that would not be understood for another seventeen years, remained. But the people were simply... archived. Gone from the physical plane, stored in some format that was neither alive nor dead, a bureaucratic superposition that the Auditors considered perfectly humane, because in the Auditors' framework, "humane" was not an adjective that applied to species under review. It applied to the neatness of the filing.

Eleven days. Seven billion lives, give or take, processed and stored.

All except three thousand and forty-one, who were, at the time, aboard the *ISS Guangming*, a joint Chinese-European deep space research vessel on a shakedown cruise near Jupiter. The *Guangming* had been too far from any recognized administrative center to be included in the initial processing wave, and by the time the Auditors' system flagged the discrepancy, the ship had already received a final, frantic burst transmission from the surface—an automated SOS from the European Space Agency's ground station in Darmstadt, where someone, in the last moments before being archived, had done three things: screamed, wept, and attached a copy of Form 1-A(R), filled out correctly, to the transmission.

It had been stamped RECEIVED too late to save anyone on the ground. But the file was valid. And because it was valid, and because it was associated with the signal footprint of the *Guangming*, the Auditors' system automatically generated a new file: Form 27-B, Application for Continued Existence (Remnant Population, Sub-Planetary), which granted the surviving humans a provisional continuity period of one hundred standard galactic cycles while their application was reviewed.

One hundred standard galactic cycles.

Approximately seventy-two Earth years.

Sincerely, the Sub-Spiral Compliance Division.

Have a productive cycle.

---

That had been seventeen years ago.

The *Guangming*—extensively modified, expanded by cannibalizing two unmanned fuel depots near Saturn, and now roughly the size and shape of a small town that had been rolled into a cylinder and taught to feel guilty about its fuel consumption—limped through interstellar space at a fraction of the speed of light, aimed at a set of coordinates that had been extracted from the only other piece of useful information in the Auditors' files: a reference to a "Designated Sanctuary Zone (Provisional)" in a star system approximately forty-two light-years from Sol.

Forty-two light-years at their current speed was a journey measured in generations.

Three thousand and forty-one human beings. Then, as births slowly outpaced the deaths of the elderly (cardiac events, cancers, and one memorable case of a man who choked on a recycled soy nugget while laughing at a joke about the apocalypse), the number crept upward. Three thousand one hundred. Three thousand two hundred. The number was now three thousand four hundred and nine, not counting the seventy-eight fertilized embryos in cryogenic storage who were the subject of an ongoing and increasingly theological debate about whether they counted as applicants on Form 27-B or required separate documentation.

The ship had a captain. The ship had a council. The ship had a school, a hydroponic farm, three therapists (woefully insufficient), a brewery (which had started as a morale initiative and was now the de facto social nucleus of the vessel), and a small, one-room office with a laminated sign on the door that read:

**DEPARTMENT OF AUDITOR COMPLIANCE**
**Gloria R. Huang, Director**
**Please Take a Number**

There was a number dispenser beside the door. It had been 3D-printed at Gloria's request in the second year of the voyage, and it was, in her professional opinion, the single most important piece of technology on the ship.

Nobody else shared this opinion.

Nobody else had worked for seventeen years at the Pasadena branch of the California Department of Motor Vehicles.

## CHAPTER TWO: Gloria

Gloria Rosario Huang was fifty-four years old, five feet two inches tall, the daughter of a Taiwanese-American electrical engineer and a Filipina nurse, and she had been, at the time of Earth's administrative dissolution, the most patient person on the planet.

This was not a figure of speech. It was practically a clinical diagnosis. Her therapist on Earth—she had seen a therapist on Earth, because working at the DMV for seventeen years did things to a person, even a patient one—had once told her that her tolerance for frustration was "either a gift or a pathology, and I genuinely can't tell which." Gloria had taken this as a compliment, because she had learned long ago that any system, no matter how broken, no matter how kafkaesque, no matter how seemingly designed by a vengeful god to crush the human spirit, could be navigated if you simply refused to take it personally.

This was the secret. The only secret. The DMV was not your enemy. The tax code was not your enemy. The hold music was not your enemy. These were systems, and systems had rules, and rules had edges, and edges could be found if you stayed calm, read carefully, and brought the right documentation.

She had not been an important person aboard the *Guangming*. For the first three years, she had been a logistics assistant, helping catalog and distribute supplies. She was good at it. She was organized. She kept spreadsheets the way some people kept gardens, pruning them, tending them, finding quiet satisfaction in their orderly rows.

Then, in Year Four, the Auditors had made contact again.

It came in the form of a status update—a brief, automated notice that materialized on the ship's main communications display, in the same eerily precise font as the original Form 1-A:

> **RE: File #SOL-3/H.SAPIENS/27-B/PROVISIONAL**
>
> Your application for Continued Existence (Remnant Population, Sub-Planetary) is currently in queue for review.
>
> Estimated processing time: 34–97 standard galactic cycles.
>
> To expedite your application, please ensure all supplementary documentation has been filed, including but not limited to:
>
> - Form 27-B(S): Supplementary Species Profile
> - Form 27-B(G): Genetic Viability Assessment
> - Form 27-B(C): Cultural Output Justification
> - Form 27-B(E): Environmental Impact Statement (Historical)
> - Form 27-B(T): Technological Capacity Inventory
> - Form 27-B(P): Statement of Philosophical Coherence
>
> Failure to submit supplementary documentation may result in application processing delays and/or default archival.
>
> For questions, please visit your nearest Compliance Interface Point.
>
> Thank you for your cooperation.
>
> — Automated Systems, Sub-Spiral Compliance Division

Captain Vasquez had stared at this message for a long time. Then she had turned to her senior staff and said, "Does anyone here know what the hell a Compliance Interface Point is?"

Nobody did.

"Does anyone here know how to fill out a Supplementary Species Profile?"

Nobody did.

"Does anyone here have any experience at all with any kind of bureaucratic system so obtuse and self-referential that it makes you want to set things on fire?"

Gloria, who had been in the back of the room restocking a supply cabinet, had raised her hand.

"The DMV," she said mildly. "Seventeen years."

Every head turned.

"Can you read this?" Vasquez asked, gesturing at the screen with the particular brand of exhausted fury that, Gloria would learn, was the captain's default state.

Gloria walked to the screen. She read the notice. She read it again. She adjusted her glasses—she no longer needed them; the ship's medical bay had corrected her vision in Year One, but she had kept them because they helped her think, the way a carpenter might keep a lucky pencil behind their ear even when using a laser level.

"Yes," she said.

"You understand it?"

"No. I can read it. Understanding comes later. Understanding comes from the next fourteen forms." She paused. "Do we have a printer?"

---

They did have a printer. They had several, in fact, repurposed manufacturing units that could produce anything from hull patch adhesive to dinner plates. Gloria requisitioned one for her exclusive use, along with a dedicated storage room, a filing cabinet (fabricated to her specifications, four drawers, chrome-finished, because some things mattered), and a rubber stamp that read RECEIVED, which she carved by hand from a block of recycled polymer because the fabrication unit could not understand why anyone would want such a thing.

The Department of Auditor Compliance was born.

At first, it was a joke. The ship had real problems—life support calibrations, hydroponics failures, the slow psychological erosion of living in a metal tube hurtling through the void. Nobody had time for Gloria's forms. Captain Vasquez tolerated the department the way one tolerates a harmless eccentricity in wartime, which is to say, she ignored it unless it got in the way.
