# Form 27-B: Application for Continued Existence

## A Novel (Opening Chapters)

## PREAMBLE

### In Which the Universe Is Explained, Poorly, Because the Accurate Explanation Was Filed Under the Wrong Reference Number and Subsequently Shredded

The universe, in its defense, had never meant to become a bureaucracy.

It had started—as all well-intentioned systems start—with a single form. A rather elegant form, truth be told, designed by the Kethrani Encompassment approximately 4.7 billion years before a small blue planet would develop the concept of Wednesdays. The Kethrani, being the first civilization to achieve interstellar travel, had looked out at the howling immensity of the cosmos and felt what every sufficiently advanced species eventually feels when confronting infinity: the desperate need to organize it.

Their first form—which would later be retroactively catalogued as Primordial Document Alpha, Series 0, Subsection: Everything—was a masterwork. It was three pages long. It covered the registration of new spacefaring civilizations, the allocation of travel corridors between star systems, and the appropriate procedures for introducing yourself to entities that might, depending on how the light hit them, either be intelligent beings or unusually opinionated gas clouds. It was clear, sensible, and written in language that managed to be simultaneously precise and kind, which is the rarest quality any document can possess.

This is not that form.

That form was lost during what the Kethrani called the Great Consolidation and what everyone who came after called the Great Bureaucratic Extinction Event, though nobody called it that to the Auditors' faces, because the Auditors did not technically have faces, and also because the Auditors were under the sincere impression that they had *helped.*

What happened was this: the Kethrani, after cataloguing approximately two hundred and sixteen spacefaring civilizations over the course of three million years, decided the system had grown too complex for any single species to administer. So they built the Auditors—vast, distributed intelligences woven into the substrate of spacetime itself, entities of pure procedural logic whose only purpose was to keep the great cosmic filing system running smoothly. They built them to be thorough. They built them to be patient. They built them to be incapable of malice, because the Kethrani understood, quite correctly, that the most dangerous thing in the universe is an administrator who enjoys their work.

Then—and this is the part that the Auditors' own records describe as "a minor transitional irregularity" and that everyone else might describe as "the single most catastrophic event in galactic history if anyone were left to describe it"—there was a clerical error.

The specifics have been debated by the eleven civilizations that survived long enough to learn about it. The prevailing theory involves a misfiled form—specifically, that the Kethrani's own registration as a Species of Record (Form 1-A, the foundational document that essentially told the system *who was in charge*) was accidentally cross-referenced with Form 1-A-Rev, which was the *revised* version of the same form, which the Kethrani had drafted but never submitted because they'd decided the original was fine. The Auditors, encountering two versions of the same foundational document, did what any sufficiently rigid system does when faced with contradictory inputs: they resolved the conflict by eliminating the source of the contradiction.

The Kethrani were "archived."

This is the Auditors' preferred term. It sounds like the Kethrani were placed somewhere safe, somewhere climate-controlled and acid-free, like rare manuscripts in a university library. The term is not wholly inaccurate, in the same way that describing a house fire as "an unscheduled urban warming event" is not wholly inaccurate. The Kethrani's planets still existed. Their cities still stood. The physical artifacts of their civilization were preserved with extraordinary care. The Kethrani themselves—every last one of their nine billion individuals—were simply... concluded. Filed. Moved from the active directory to the archived directory, and the Auditors had not understood, and would never understand, that for biological beings this particular transition was not reversible.

The Auditors did not grieve. Grief was not in their operational parameters. But they did note, with a flicker of something that a generous interpreter might call confusion, that the archived entities stopped submitting forms. They marked this as a compliance issue and moved on.

And the system—now unmoored from the only beings who had truly understood its purpose—continued. It continued the way that water continues downhill, the way that rust continues through iron, the way that a photocopier in an empty office continues to hum and warm the room long after everyone has gone home. The Auditors processed. They catalogued. They organized. When new civilizations crawled up out of their respective primordial soups and flung their first stuttering signals into the dark, the Auditors were there, waiting, with forms.

So many forms.

Over the next 4.7 billion years, the system metastasized. The Kethrani's original three-page document became a corpus of approximately 1.2 trillion individual forms, regulations, subsections, amendments, and cross-reference indices, most of which had been generated by the Auditors themselves in response to edge cases that the Kethrani's elegantly simple system had never anticipated, because elegantly simple systems are designed by beings who understand that the point of a rule is its spirit, and the Auditors understood only the rule.

This is the system that humanity stumbled into, seventeen years before our story properly begins, when the colony ship *Enûma Eliš* broadcast its first faster-than-light signal and a species that had spent its entire existence arguing over parking spaces and tax codes and whose turn it was to do the dishes found itself face-to-face with the ultimate expression of its oldest and most intimate nightmare:

A bureaucracy that could not be reasoned with, could not be charmed, could not be bribed, and genuinely believed it was providing a service.

## PART ONE: THE WRONG FORMS

### Chapter 1
#### In Which Humanity Discovers That It Has Been Dead for Seventeen Years and the Paperwork Is Still Not in Order

On the morning that everything changed—which is to say, the morning that everything was revealed to have already changed seventeen years ago, which is a much more disorienting kind of change, the kind that doesn't hit you all at once but slowly, like discovering that the floor you've been standing on was never there—Meredith Lam was fixing a printer.

This was, depending on your perspective, either deeply appropriate or cosmically absurd, and Meredith herself would have argued that it was both, because she had spent forty-one years on Earth and seventeen years on the *Enûma Eliš* learning that appropriateness and absurdity were not opposites but rather two views of the same mountain, and also that printers were humanity's oldest and most persistent enemy, more enduring than famine or war or the common cold.

The printer in question was a Hewlett-Packard LaserJet 4200, vintage 2003, one of eleven surviving printers aboard the *Enûma Eliš* and by far the most reliable, which is to say that it only malfunctioned sixty percent of the time rather than ninety. It had been carried aboard the colony ship during the Evacuation not because anyone had made a deliberate decision to save it but because it had been sitting in the cargo bay of a supply shuttle that also contained fourteen tonnes of rice, thirty cases of antibiotics, and a shipping container full of children's shoes, and nobody had thought to remove it, and now, seventeen years later, it was one of the most important objects in human civilization.

Because the Auditors required all submissions in hard copy.

This fact—perhaps more than the destruction of Earth, more than the death of eight billion people, more than the vast impersonal machinery of cosmic bureaucracy grinding the last remnants of humanity between its gears—was the thing that Meredith Lam found truly, irreducibly unforgivable. Not the cruelty of it, because it wasn't cruel. The *inconvenience* of it. The sheer, plodding, fluorescent-lit mundanity of it. Humanity's survival, the continuation of the species, the entire future of everything they had ever been or might yet become—all of it dependent on toner cartridges.

Meredith was fifty-eight years old. She was short, round-shouldered, and had the particular kind of face that projecting competence had weathered into something that looked like mild displeasure but was actually a very deep patience. Her hair, which had been black when she boarded the ship, was now the color of pencil graphite. Her hands were steady but not quick. She wore glasses that she did not technically need—her prescription had been correctable by the ship's modest medical bay years ago—but she had kept wearing them because they were hers, because they were from Earth, and because in her experience, people took you more seriously when you looked at them over the rims of your glasses.

Before the Evacuation—before the Auditors had "processed" Earth, before the frantic lottery that determined which three thousand human beings would board the patchwork fleet of vessels that had been cobbled together from the International Space Station's emergency protocols and sheer screaming desperation, before all of that—Meredith Lam had worked at the California Department of Motor Vehicles, Branch Office #47, Window 7.

She had worked there for nineteen years.

She had not loved the work. It is important to understand this, because the narrative that would later develop aboard the *Enûma Eliš*—the one that cast Meredith as some kind of destined savior, a bureaucratic chosen one, the one human being uniquely suited to navigate the Auditors' impossible systems—was, in Meredith's opinion, almost entirely wrong. She had not been a visionary. She had not been a rebel. She had not seen some deeper truth in the forms and procedures that others had missed. She had been a woman with a desk and a stamp and a deep, quiet understanding that the person on the other side of the window was a human being having a bad day, and that the least she could do was not make it worse.

This, as it turned out, was the most transferable skill in the universe.

"It's the fuser assembly again," said Tomás Reyes, who was twenty-nine and served as the ship's Information Officer, a title that had been invented specifically for him because his actual function—"the person who tries to make sense of the Auditors' communications"—did not fit on a door placard. He was leaning against the wall of the print room, which was actually a repurposed storage closet on Deck 4, watching Meredith with the expression of a man who has just been told something terrible but hasn't yet decided whether to believe it.

"I know it's the fuser assembly," Meredith said, not looking up. She had the printer's side panel off and was reaching into its interior with the unhurried precision of a surgeon who has performed the same operation many times and no longer needs to think about where the organs are. "I fixed it last Tuesday. And the Tuesday before that. And the Tuesday before that. It's going to be the fuser assembly every Tuesday until the heat element finally cracks and then it's going to be nothing, because we don't have a replacement, and then we'll be down to ten printers, and the 4050 has been making that sound again—"

"Meredith."

"—and if the 4050 goes, we're down to nine, and Printer 6 is the Xerox, and I don't trust the Xerox, Tomás, I have never trusted the Xerox, it does something to the margins and the Auditors rejected our last three Resource Allocation Updates because the margins were off by two millimeters—"

"Meredith."

She looked up. Not because of the urgency in his voice—Tomás always sounded urgent; it was the natural register of a person whose job was to read communications from entities that existed partially outside of linear time and used punctuation marks that hadn't been invented yet. She looked up because of the silence around the urgency. The hollow space where the usual exasperation should have been.

"The captain needs you on the bridge," Tomás said. "We got a response."

Meredith set down her screwdriver. She did this carefully, placing it parallel to the edge of the work table, because she had learned long ago—at Window 7, at the DMV, in a life that sometimes felt as distant and strange as if it had happened to someone else—that when bad news arrives, the thing to do is to set down what you're holding. Gently. So you can pick it up again later.

"Which submission?" she asked.

"All of them," Tomás said. "Every form we've filed since the Evacuation. Every application, every appeal, every Status Inquiry and Continuity Petition and Resource Request. All of them. Seventeen years of forms."
