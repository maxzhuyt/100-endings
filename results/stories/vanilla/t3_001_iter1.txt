# The Infinite Playoff

---

The first contradiction appeared in the seventh inning of a game played on August 14th, 2019, between the Tri-City Dustdevils and the Stockton Ports, California League, Single-A. In Scorebook A—the official record maintained by the home team's scorekeeper—Dustdevils third baseman Ricky Fontaine grounded into a 6-4-3 double play on a 2-1 count, ending the inning with runners stranded at first and third. In Scorebook B—the league copy, submitted electronically that same evening—Fontaine singled through the right side on an 0-2 count, driving in two runs and extending the inning by four more batters, three of whom reached base, one of whom scored. The final score in Scorebook A was Stockton 4, Tri-City 2. The final score in Scorebook B was Stockton 4, Tri-City 5.

I found this on a Tuesday in March while sitting in the storage closet that the Tri-City front office called my "consulting suite." The closet smelled like mouse droppings and the industrial solvent they used on the clubhouse floors. Through the wall I could hear the groundskeeper arguing with someone about infield clay. I had a folding table, a laptop with a cracked hinge, and eleven boxes of records that Tri-City's general manager, a nervous man named Dale Perrault, had assembled for me with the reverent disorganization of someone who believed that volume equaled thoroughness.

"We need this airtight," Perrault had told me when he'd called in January. "The league's taking the Stockton thing seriously. Point shaving, maybe worse. We need someone who can go through the numbers and find the pattern."

He'd called me because I was cheap. He'd called me because I had fifteen years of minor league statistical work on my résumé and no current employment. He'd called me because someone at the league office had given him my name, probably as a joke, probably because my reputation had degraded so gradually over the past decade that it now existed in a quantum state: people remembered that I'd once been meticulous without remembering when I'd stopped being meticulous, which meant they could recommend me with a clear conscience while harboring a vague unease they couldn't source.

My name is Howard Luntz. I am fifty-three years old. I have worked in professional baseball in various statistical and record-keeping capacities since 1994. I am telling you this so that you understand there are certain things I should be trusted to know, and I am telling you this also because I have recently discovered that the category of things I can be trusted to know may be empty.

---

The Fontaine discrepancy was, by itself, unremarkable. Scorekeeping errors happen. The California League in particular had been, during the period in question, a swamp of administrative neglect—underpaid part-timers keeping book, overworked league officials rubber-stamping submissions, nobody cross-checking anything because nobody cared enough about Single-A ball to cross-check anything. A grounded double play becomes a single through the right side. Two runs score that didn't score. A game flips. These things happen.

What doesn't happen—what shouldn't happen—is for both records to contain internally consistent box scores, play-by-play logs, and pitch counts that support their respective versions of events with zero artifacts of alteration. Scorebook A wasn't a correction of Scorebook B, or vice versa. They were parallel accounts that agreed on everything except the at-bat in question and everything that followed from it, which means someone had constructed one of them whole cloth, inventing not just the changed outcome but the entire downstream consequences: every subsequent pitch, every fielding play, every substitution, every line in the box score that would need to be different if those two runs had or hadn't scored.

This was not an error. This was architecture. Someone had built a second game inside the seams of the first one.

I noted the discrepancy in my working file. I pulled the next box.

---

By the end of the first week I had found eleven more.

They were all Stockton games. They all involved late-inning at-bats where the outcome determined the final score. They all featured the same structural elegance—two complete, internally consistent records, each one a perfect and irreconcilable account of a game that could not have been played both ways. In six of the eleven, the discrepancy flipped the result. Stockton won in one version, lost in the other. In the remaining five, the margin of victory changed by two or more runs, which would matter if someone was betting not just outcomes but run lines.

I built a spreadsheet. I mapped the discrepancies by date, opponent, and the identity of the player whose at-bat diverged. Three names came up repeatedly: Fontaine, a Dustdevils utility player named Marcus Heap, and a Stockton reliever named Josué Villanueva whose ERA, depending on which set of records you believed, was either 3.41 or 5.87.

I called Perrault.

"Eleven games," I said. "Minimum. Possibly more. Someone was maintaining dual records—one set for the league, one set for the home team. The question is which set is real."

"Jesus," Perrault said. "Which one?"

"That's the question," I said again, because he had not understood that it was literally the question, the question that sat at the center of everything, the question I was already beginning to suspect might not have an answer.

"Well, find out," he said. "That's what we're paying you for."

What they were paying me was four thousand dollars a month plus a per diem for meals that I'd been spending on scratch tickets from the Chevron on Clearwater Avenue. I mention this not because it's relevant but because I've decided to mention everything, since I no longer trust my ability to determine what's relevant.

---

The second week, I requested game footage. This was optimistic. Minor league clubs in the California League during this period were not the Dodgers. Many games were filmed on a single camera mounted behind home plate, if they were filmed at all. Stockton had archived some footage on an external hard drive that the front office shipped to me after three weeks of follow-up emails and a threatening call from the league's compliance office. Tri-City's footage was theoretically available through the league's central media server, but the server had been migrated in 2020 and approximately forty percent of the California League's video archive from 2017–2019 had been corrupted or lost.

I got what I could. For seven of my eleven discrepant games, I had nothing. For three, I had partial footage—innings one through five, or a single camera angle that cut out during the seventh-inning stretch, or a file that played for thirty seconds and then dissolved into macroblocked green static. For the August 14th game—the Fontaine game, the original—I had full footage from the Stockton home camera.

I watched the seventh inning. Fontaine came up with runners on first and third. The count went to 2-1. He swung at a fastball on the inner half and grounded it to short. The shortstop flipped to second, the second baseman pivoted and threw to first. Double play. Inning over. Stockton 4, Tri-City 2.

Scorebook A was correct.

Scorebook B—the league copy, the one submitted electronically to the central database, the one that would have been used to calculate official statistics, the one that showed Fontaine singling and two runs scoring and Tri-City winning 5-4—was fabricated.

I noted this in my file. I felt the particular satisfaction of a thing confirmed, the small pleasure that comes from watching a discrepancy resolve into a fact. Scorebook A was real. Scorebook B was fiction. Someone had falsified the league copy. The investigation had a direction.

Then I looked at the box score for Scorebook A—the real one, the one the video confirmed—and I noticed that it listed the Tri-City starting pitcher as a left-hander named Devon Oakley, who had thrown six innings and given up three earned runs.

Oakley, I knew, had been on the injured list since August 7th with a strained UCL. I knew this because I had processed his IL placement myself when I'd worked for the league office in 2019. I had entered it into the system. I had seen the paperwork.

Devon Oakley could not have pitched on August 14th.

I rewound the video. I watched the first inning. The pitcher on the mound was right-handed. His name, visible briefly on the back of his jersey when he walked off the mound after a strikeout, was not Oakley. It was a name I didn't recognize, or rather a name I recognized from a different team in a different year, a name that should not have been on a Tri-City roster at any point in 2019.

I paused the video. I looked at the jersey number. I cross-referenced it against the 2019 Tri-City roster. The number belonged to a catcher named Paul Wittig who had been released in June.

I closed my laptop. I sat in the closet for a long time. Through the wall, the groundskeeper had stopped arguing. I could hear the sprinklers running on the outfield grass, a rhythmic chk-chk-chk that sounded like a clock that couldn't decide what it was counting.

---

I should tell you something about my career.

I started in baseball in 1994, the year of the strike, which is appropriate because I have spent my entire professional life in a sport that periodically stops being itself. I was twenty-four. I had a mathematics degree from a state school in Oregon and an obsessive love of baseball statistics that I'd cultivated since childhood the way other people cultivated stamp collections or religious faith—as a system for organizing the world into comprehensible patterns, a grammar of events that transformed the chaos of athletic competition into rows and columns that could be read, interpreted, trusted.

I worked for two major league clubs in their front offices doing data entry and basic analysis. I was not good enough or ambitious enough for the analytics revolution that was coming. When the smart people arrived with their pitch-tracking systems and their Bayesian models, I became obsolete in the way that hand-looms became obsolete: not all at once, but with a finality that made the gradual pace of it feel like cruelty.

I migrated to the minor leagues, where the infrastructure was twenty years behind and my skills were still useful. I kept score. I maintained databases. I reconciled statistical records between teams and leagues. For a while, I did this for the California League directly, as a part-time contractor responsible for data integrity—a title that now strikes me as containing either irony or prophecy, though I can no longer tell the difference.

I was good at this work. Let me say that clearly. For at least ten years—from roughly 1998 to 2008—I was meticulous, reliable, and honest. I found errors that other people missed. I caught a scoring mistake in a 2003 Midwest League game that would have incorrectly credited a pitcher with a perfect game. I was known, to the small number of people who knew me at all, as someone who cared about getting the numbers right.

What changed was not sudden. It was not dramatic. There was no inciting incident, no moment where I chose to become unreliable. What happened was that I began to gamble, and the gambling was fine at first—small bets, recreational, the kind of thing that a person who loves baseball and understands probabilities might naturally drift into—and then it was not fine, and then it was very much not fine, and then I owed money to people who did not have the patience for payment plans, and then I did something.

I'm going to tell you what I did, but I want you to understand that I'm not entirely sure what I did. This is the problem. This is, in fact, the entire problem.

---

Here is what I believe happened:

In 2016, or possibly 2015, I began altering statistical records for California League games in which I had placed bets. The alterations were small and targeted. A hit became an error. A run scored in one inning migrated to another. A pitcher's line changed by a fraction. I did this not to change the outcomes of the games—the outcomes were already determined; the games had been played—but to change the official record of those outcomes in ways that aligned with the betting lines I had taken. If I had bet the under on a run total, and the over had hit, I would find a way to shave a run from the official record. If I had bet a player's strikeout total, I might adjust a gameday log to add or subtract a K.

This sounds insane. I know it sounds insane. The bets I was placing were not through legitimate sportsbooks that cross-referenced official statistics in real time. They were through a person in Bakersfield whose operation ran on trust and delayed settlement and an Excel spreadsheet that was only marginally more sophisticated than the ones I maintained for the league. When this person—I will call him Ray—settled a bet, he used the league's official statistical database, which I controlled. I was both the bettor and the auditor. I was writing the exam and grading it.

For a while this worked, in the narrow sense that Ray paid me and I paid off a fraction of what I owed to the other people I owed, and the debts did not grow. They did not shrink either. They achieved a kind of equilibrium, a steady state of financial ruin that I maintained the way a pilot maintains altitude: by constant, exhausting adjustment.

But here is where it gets complicated. Here is where I need you to understand that I am not entirely sure that any of the preceding paragraph is true.

Because when I look at the records now—the records I was hired to investigate, the records that Dale Perrault shipped to me in eleven boxes and called evidence of Stockton's malfeasance—I recognize my own handwriting. Not literally. The alterations are digital. But I recognize the logic of them. I recognize the architecture. The dual scorebooks, the parallel game states, the internally consistent fictions that branch from a single changed at-bat and propagate forward through every subsequent play with the precision of a mathematical proof—this is my work. I am almost sure this is my work.

The problem is that I also remember investigating exactly this kind of alteration in 2018, at the request of the league office, as part of a data-integrity audit. I remember finding discrepancies and reporting them. I remember writing a memo that described the pattern in clinical terms and recommended enhanced cross-checking procedures. I remember the league ignoring my recommendations.

And the problem after that problem is that I cannot find the memo. I cannot find any record of the audit. The person at the league office who I believe requested it does not remember requesting it. When I called her—her name is Sandra Chu, she runs compliance—she said, "Howard, we haven't used you for audits since 2014."

So either I fabricated the records and later forgot I'd fabricated them and then even later fabricated a memory of discovering and reporting the fabrication, or I discovered someone else's fabrication and correctly reported it and the report was lost or suppressed and I am now being set up to take the fall for someone else's crime, or—and this is the possibility that keeps me awake, the one that runs underneath the others like a bass note—the fabrications and the discovery and the investigation and my memory of all of these things are part of a single recursive structure, a loop I built and entered and cannot find the exit to because the exit is also part of the loop.

---

In the third week, I found a game that did not exist.

It appeared in my spreadsheet—a Stockton home game against the Rancho Cucamonga Quakes, dated September 3rd, 2019. I had a complete box score for it. I had play-by-play. I had it listed in both Scorebook A and Scorebook B, with the usual divergence: a ninth-inning at-bat that went two different ways, producing two different outcomes. In one version, Stockton won on a walk-off double by a designated hitter named Tomás Reyes. In the other, Reyes struck out looking on a checked swing, and Stockton lost in extra innings.

I went to the league schedule. September 3rd, 2019, was an off day for both teams. There was no game.

I checked the rosters. Tomás Reyes had never played for Stockton. He had never played for any California League team. As far as I could determine through every database available to me, Tomás Reyes had never played professional baseball.
