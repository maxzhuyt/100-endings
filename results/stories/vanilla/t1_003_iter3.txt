## OPENING SEQUENCE — "FOUR LIVES, FOUR CAGES"

### MARGOT CHEN-WALKER — Portland, Oregon

We open on perfection.

A $2.3 million Pearl District loft, immaculately staged. Margot Chen-Walker, 32, moves through the space like a surgeon, adjusting a throw pillow by two degrees, repositioning a single orchid stem. She is Portland's most sought-after residential stager—the woman who makes empty houses feel like someone's aspirational life. Her website tagline: *Less is the only more.*

She's showing the property to a realtor couple. Every gesture is controlled. Every surface is bare. She speaks in the clipped, authoritative cadence of someone who has built an entire identity around the elimination of excess.

The couple leaves. Margot locks up. She drives her spotless Volvo across the river to an industrial district near the airport. She parks in front of a row of storage units. She owns eleven of them.

The first one she opens is floor-to-ceiling newspapers, sorted by date, going back to 2006. She doesn't read them. She runs her fingers along the spines of the stacks like a pianist warming up, and something in her shoulders releases for the first time since we've met her.

The second unit: broken electronics. Monitors with shattered screens. Keyboards missing keys. VCRs. She has a system—everything is catalogued in a series of identical notebooks, cross-referenced by acquisition date and "emotional weight," which she rates on a scale of one to ten. She writes an entry for a toaster oven she found on the curb this morning. Emotional weight: seven. She doesn't know why. She just knows it can't leave.

In the third unit, smaller, she sits in a narrow canyon between two walls of children's clothing—none of it hers, none of it belonging to any child she's ever met. She bought it all at estate sales. She sits and breathes and we see, for just a moment, a woman experiencing something adjacent to peace.

Her phone buzzes. Her husband, PETER, a kind-faced man we'll meet shortly: *Dinner's at 7. Jake's asking for you.*

Jake is her son. He's four. She glances at the children's clothes around her—they're all approximately the size a four-year-old would wear.

She locks the unit, triple-checks it, and drives home to the life she's built on the other side of the river.

At home, her house is half-magazine-spread, half-war-zone. Every room that Peter and Jake primarily occupy is cluttered, normal, alive. Every room that belongs to Margot is bare. The garage is locked. Peter has never been inside. When he asks about it, she changes the subject with the smooth deflection of long practice.

Over dinner, Jake is showing her a drawing—a family portrait, stick figures. He's drawn her holding something in every hand. "That's you carrying your work stuff," he explains. She stares at it like it's an X-ray she wasn't prepared for.

After Jake's in bed, she and Peter have a conversation we can feel they've had a hundred times. He's not angry, which almost makes it worse. He's *exhausted.* He knows about the storage units—he found the credit card statements two years ago. What he doesn't know is how many there are now, or that she's taken out a second mortgage on their home to fund them.

"I just need to understand what you're keeping," he says.

"I'm not keeping anything," she says. "I'm just not ready to lose it."

He doesn't know what to do with that. Neither does she.

After he's asleep, she drives back to the units. It's 11 PM. She sits in the children's-clothing unit and opens her laptop and bids on a lot of vintage camp memorabilia being sold by an estate in upstate New York. Canteens, compass lanyards, a counselor's whistle. She wins the auction at three times what anyone else was willing to pay. Her hands are shaking and she doesn't know why.

That's a lie. She knows exactly why.

### DEXTER "DEX" OKONKWO — Chicago, Illinois

Dex, 33, stands in front of a conference room full of corporate executives at a major retail chain's headquarters. He's the founder and CEO of *Perimeter Security Consulting.* He's wearing a $4,000 suit. He's the guy Fortune 500 companies hire to identify weaknesses in their loss prevention systems.

He's giving a presentation. On the screen behind him: *$112.1 Billion: Annual U.S. Retail Shrinkage.* He walks them through common shoplifting methodologies with the casual authority of a man who has studied the subject to a doctoral level. Blind spots. Distraction techniques. The psychology of the grab-and-go versus the slow-play.

"Your associates are looking for nervous people," he tells them. "The good ones don't look nervous. The good ones look like they belong there more than your employees do."

After the presentation, a VP shakes his hand, tells him they'll be signing the contract. Dex smiles, shakes firmly, and walks out through the retail floor below the corporate offices—it's one of the chain's flagship stores.

We watch him from a high angle, the way a security camera would. He walks through casual menswear. His hand passes over a rack and a silk pocket square vanishes into his sleeve. He passes a display table and a pair of cufflinks migrate from the felt display board into his jacket pocket. He never looks at what he's taking. His eyes are forward, pleasant, empty. A sales associate asks if he needs help. He chats warmly for thirty seconds. During the conversation, a tie clip disappears.

He walks out the front door without breaking stride. No alarms. No tags. He's better than any system he's ever designed.

In his car—a leased German sedan he can comfortably afford—he opens the center console. It's full of pocket squares, cufflinks, tie clips. Dozens of them. He doesn't wear pocket squares, cufflinks, or tie clips. He takes the new ones and adds them to the collection. He stares at them. The excitement drains from his face like water from a tub, and what's left underneath is something we recognize: shame.

He drives to his apartment—a beautiful two-bedroom in Lincoln Park, tasteful, a little sterile. His girlfriend, YUMIKO, is on the couch reading. She's a public defender. She's brilliant, patient, and increasingly suspicious.

They have a good evening. They cook together. They're clearly people who love each other. But there's a moment—she opens a drawer to get a corkscrew and finds three watches that aren't Dex's. She holds them up. He has a story ready. "Client gifts." She nods. She doesn't believe him but doesn't have the energy to excavate the lie tonight.

Later, while Yumiko sleeps, Dex stands in his closet. Behind a false panel he installed himself: a space roughly the size of a phone booth, packed to its edges with stolen goods. Not valuable goods—not primarily. There are priceless items mixed with absolute junk. A Montblanc pen next to a gas station lighter. A first-edition book next to a packet of chewing gum. The value is irrelevant. It was never about the objects.

He picks up a small item from the very back of the closet—a compass, old, the kind they used to give out at summer camps. It's the one thing in here he didn't steal. He turns it over in his hands and puts it back.

### RUTH "RUTHIE" VASQUEZ-GRANT — Austin, Texas

Ruthie, 32, hosts a moderately popular YouTube cooking show called *Ruthie's Table.* She's warm, funny, self-deprecating in a way that reads as confidence. Her brand is "approachable comfort food with a twist." She's filming an episode—chicken-fried steak with a mole-inspired gravy. She tastes as she goes, holds things up to the camera, reacts with performative pleasure.

"Cut." She checks the footage with her producer/camera operator, LEON, a friend who clearly worries about her more than he lets on. The food looks incredible. Ruthie hasn't actually swallowed anything.

She's been doing this for three years. Three years of cooking on camera, tasting on camera, and quietly spitting into a cup just off-frame. Leon has noticed. He's tried to bring it up. She deflects with humor so sharp it draws blood.

After filming, Ruthie goes home to a rented bungalow in East Austin. Her kitchen is two kitchens, really. The filming kitchen—bright, organized, designed for the camera—and the private kitchen behind it, where the evidence lives. Her recycling bin on pickup days is suspicious if you know what to look for: too many containers for one person, the wrong ratios, the telltale archaeology of a binge.

She was a competitive gymnast until seventeen. The eating disorder predates the gymnastics by a year, but nobody noticed because thinness was the point. What people don't understand about her—what the YouTube comments certainly don't understand when they call her "body positive" and "refreshingly real"—is that the show is itself a symptom. She has built a career around the preparation and presentation of food she does not allow herself to keep.

Tonight, alone, the cycle runs its course. We don't need to see all of it. We see her at the supermarket, filling a cart with a specificity that indicates ritual. We see her at home, at the counter, hands moving with the same expertise she shows on camera but faster, mechanical, without the performance of joy. We see the aftermath—her on the bathroom floor, exhausted, running the shower to cover the sound from no one because she lives alone.

She calls her mother. Her mother talks about a cousin's wedding in San Antonio. Ruthie keeps her voice bright. Her mother asks if she's eating well. "You know me, Ma. I literally eat for a living." Her mother laughs. Ruthie laughs. The bathroom floor is cold against her legs.

Later, she opens her laptop to find a DM from a fan: *"Your show literally saved me from my eating disorder. Watching you enjoy food gave me permission to enjoy it again."* Ruthie types: *"That's beautiful. I'm so happy for you."* She closes the laptop and sits in the precise center of her couch and stares at the black screen.

On her refrigerator, held up by a magnet: a photo from summer camp. Four kids, sunburned, arms around each other. You can't quite make out their faces. She hasn't looked at it closely in years. She doesn't need to.

### OWEN PARK — Reno, Nevada

Owen, 33, is an actuary at a mid-tier insurance company. His job is to quantify risk. He's exceptional at it. His cubicle is a grid of spreadsheets, probability tables, and a desktop background that's just a plain blue screen because anything else would be a variable.

He's the most outwardly ordinary of the four. Medium build. Medium wardrobe. A face that was handsome at twenty and will be handsome again at fifty but at thirty-three is just sort of *there,* as if he's designed himself to be statistically unremarkable.

He lives in Reno. He does not gamble. That's the story he tells himself, and it's technically true in the sense that he has not placed a legal wager in a casino in fourteen months. What he does is harder to classify from the outside and impossible to stop from the inside.

We watch his day. Every decision is a bet. Not metaphorically—literally. He counts things. Red cars versus blue cars on his commute. If red wins, he takes the highway. If blue wins, he takes surface streets. He assigns probabilities to the outcomes of conversations before they happen and keeps a running mental tally of his accuracy. In the break room, he watches two people reach for the same coffee pot and his pupils dilate like a gambler watching a roulette wheel. *Who will defer?* He projects odds based on body language, social hierarchy, how much sleep each person appears to have gotten. He's right 73% of the time. He's tracked this.

He has an app on his phone. It looks like a fitness tracker. It's actually a custom-built probability engine he coded himself.
