# THE SUTTON COUNTY TRANSCRIPTS

## Being a Partial Collection of Official Court Documents, Personal Recordings, and Annotated Materials Related to the Matter of *The State v. Eleanor Ruth Vance* (Sutton County Circuit Court, Docket No. 87-CR-0441), Compiled from the Effects of Margaret Alice Conn (1949–?)

---

**COMPILER'S NOTE**

The following materials were recovered from the home of Margaret A. Conn, 14 Birch Hollow Road, Sutton County, on June 3, 1989, during a welfare check initiated by her sister, Judith Conn-Hargrove of Morgantown. Mrs. Conn had not reported for work at the Sutton County Courthouse since May 11, 1989. Her home showed no signs of forced entry or struggle. A half-eaten bowl of cereal was found on the kitchen counter. The television was on. Her car remained in the driveway. Her purse, wallet, and keys were on the hook by the door. Her shoes were lined up in the hall closet. All of them.

The materials below were found in a locked filing cabinet in Mrs. Conn's basement, for which the key was located in an envelope taped to the underside of her bathroom sink. The envelope was labeled, in Mrs. Conn's handwriting: *FOR WHOEVER COMES LOOKING, THOUGH IT WON'T MATTER.*

Certain documents have been withheld at the request of the Sutton County Prosecutor's Office. What remains has been arranged in approximate chronological order. Transcript marginalia, originally handwritten, has been rendered in bracketed italics. Audio diary transcriptions were prepared by this office from standard cassette recordings and are presented verbatim, including pauses, ambient sounds, and apparent distress.

—**Office of the Sutton County Clerk of Courts, Records Division, August 1991**

## PART ONE: THE OPENING

### DOCUMENT 1
**Official Transcript Excerpt — *The State v. Eleanor Ruth Vance***
**Sutton County Circuit Court, Docket No. 87-CR-0441**
**Day 1 of Trial — September 14, 1987**
**Hon. Walter R. Comstock, Presiding**
**Court Reporter: Margaret A. Conn, RPR, CCR**

---

THE COURT: This court is now in session. We are here on the matter of the State of West Virginia versus Eleanor Ruth Vance. The defendant is charged with three counts of murder in the first degree, three counts of felony child abuse resulting in death, and one count of desecration of human remains. Mr. Garrick, is the State prepared to make its opening statement?

MR. GARRICK: We are, Your Honor.

THE COURT: You may proceed.

MR. GARRICK: Thank you, Your Honor. Ladies and gentlemen of the jury, over the course of this trial you will hear evidence that on or about the nights of March 7th, March 19th, and April 2nd of this year, the defendant, Eleanor Vance, a licensed social worker employed by the Sutton County Department of Health and Human Resources, did willfully and with premeditation cause the deaths of three children placed under her supervisory care: Bethany Ann Coles, age seven; Marcus DeWitt Coles, age nine; and Lily Marguerite Pruitt, age six.

*[Marginal note: Standard opening. Garrick seems well-prepared. Note defendant is very still — unusual composure for someone facing three capital murder charges.]*

MR. GARRICK: You will hear that these children were found in conditions that defy any innocent explanation. That their bodies bore marks consistent not with accidental harm, not with neglect, but with deliberate, ritualistic violence. You will hear from the medical examiner that the cause of death for each child was exsanguination — that they were, to put it plainly, drained of blood — and that the incisions responsible were made with surgical precision by someone with anatomical knowledge. You will hear from investigators that symbols were found carved into the floor beneath each child's bed, symbols that experts will identify as belonging to a pre-Christian European tradition of blood offering. And you will hear that Eleanor Vance, who had unrestricted access to these children, who was responsible for monitoring the foster homes in which they were placed, and who was the last known adult to see each of them alive, had in her possession at the time of her arrest no fewer than fourteen texts dealing with the occult, with ritual sacrifice, and with what can only be described as demonology.

*[Marginal note: "Demonology." Lord. Here we go.]*

MR. GARRICK: Now, I understand that some of you may find this difficult to believe. I understand that some of you may be skeptical. I ask only that you keep an open mind and follow the evidence. Because the evidence will show that Eleanor Vance believed — genuinely, fervently believed — that what she was doing served a higher purpose. And it is precisely that belief that makes what she did murder in the first degree.

THE COURT: Thank you, Mr. Garrick. Ms. Ashford, does the defense wish to make an opening statement at this time?

MS. ASHFORD: We do, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Proceed.

MS. ASHFORD: Thank you. Ladies and gentlemen, my name is Catherine Ashford, and I represent Eleanor Vance. And I want to begin by saying something that Mr. Garrick will not say, because it does not serve his case: everything he just told you is built on hysteria.

*[Marginal note: Good — she's going right at it.]*

MS. ASHFORD: We are living through a period in this country where accusations of satanic ritual abuse have become almost commonplace. You have seen it on the news. You have read about it in the papers. Daycare workers in California. Preschool teachers in North Carolina. And now a social worker in Sutton County, West Virginia. In every one of these cases, the same pattern emerges: vulnerable children, frightened communities, and prosecutors who discover that the word "satanic" transforms a difficult, ambiguous, heartbreaking case into a simple one. Three children are dead. That is a fact, and it is a tragedy. But Eleanor Vance did not kill them. Eleanor Vance tried to save them.

MR. GARRICK: Objection, Your Honor. Counsel is testifying.

THE COURT: Sustained. Ms. Ashford, please confine your remarks to what the evidence will show.

MS. ASHFORD: The evidence will show, Your Honor.

THE COURT: Proceed.

MS. ASHFORD: The evidence will show that Eleanor Vance discovered anomalies in the foster placement records for these three children. The evidence will show that she reported her concerns to her supervisors and was told to drop the matter. The evidence will show that the symbols found at the scenes were not in any of the fourteen books seized from her home — because she had acquired those books *after* the first child's death, in a desperate attempt to understand what she was seeing in the field. And the evidence will show that Eleanor Vance was not the last adult to see these children alive. Someone else was. And the State has not bothered to find out who.

*[Marginal note: Defendant watching Ashford. No — watching me. Looked away when I looked up. Probably nothing.]*

### DOCUMENT 2
**Personal Audio Diary of Margaret A. Conn**
**Tape 1, Side A — Recorded September 14, 1987 (evening)**

[Sound of tape recorder clicking on. Ambient: television in background, lowered. Ice in a glass.]

OK. First day of the Vance trial. I'm recording this because Glen — Glen at the courthouse, the bailiff — he said something funny to me this morning before we started. He said, "Maggie, you sure you want this one?" And I laughed, because what a thing to say to someone who's been doing this for eleven years. I've done homicides. I've done the Patterson dismemberment. I sat through Roy Childress describing what he did to those women on Kanawha Road without missing a keystroke. So when Glen says "you sure you want this one," I laugh.

But then he said — and this is why I'm recording this, because the way he said it was odd, and I want to remember it exactly — he said, "I just mean, the ones with the — the occult stuff. They go a little different in this courthouse."

[Pause.]

And I said what do you mean, Glen. And he just shrugged and said he'd been here a long time and that the courthouse has its own memory about certain kinds of cases. And then the judge came in and that was that.

[Sound of ice clinking. A sip.]

The defendant. Eleanor Vance. She's — I don't know. She's not what I expected. She's forty-three, small, glasses, looks like a librarian. Looks like someone you'd see at the church bake sale. Very composed. Almost *too* composed. Most defendants, even the ones who've been coached, they fidget. They look at their families. They react to the charges. Vance sat there like she was attending a lecture. Taking mental notes.

And there was one thing. During Ashford's opening statement — the defense attorney, she's good, came down from Charleston — during her opening, she said, "Eleanor Vance tried to save them." And when she said that, Vance closed her eyes. Not like she was pained. More like — confirmation. Like someone had finally said the right words.

[Pause.]

I don't know why I'm recording this. Professional curiosity. I've never recorded thoughts about a trial before. Eleven years and I've never felt the need.

[Longer pause.]

She was looking at me. During the opening. I wrote it off, but she was. Not at the jury, not at the judge, not at her own attorney. At me. At the stenographer. Who looks at the stenographer?

[Click. Tape ends.]
