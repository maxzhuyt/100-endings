# The Reckoning at Perdition Gulch

---

## I.

**Now — October 1887**

The land remembers what people will not.

Perdition Gulch sits in a crease of the earth where the Brazos dried to nothing and the mesquite moved in like mourners to stand over the river's bones. The town was never meant to last. No town out here was. They sprouted from the dirt with all the bravado of wildflowers in drought country, blazing for a season, then gone — taken by heat or violence or the slow starvation that comes when the railroad decides to lay its tracks somewhere else and the future simply routes around you.

But Perdition Gulch held.

For sixteen years it held, long past the point when geography and economics should have ground it to dust. The well never quite went dry. The outlaws who raided the Comstock freight lines gave the town a wide berth they could not explain even to themselves. The railroad surveyors, who trampled everything in their path from Kansas to the Pacific with the indifference of a biblical plague, filed their reports and moved on, leaving Perdition Gulch as a flyspeck on their maps, unworthy of the trouble.

Nobody asked why. Or rather, nobody asked twice.

There were five people in that town who knew the answer, and they had long since stopped speaking it aloud. They convened on the first morning of each month in the back room of Bellweather's dry goods store, where the air smelled of lye soap and cedar and the quiet authority of people who have made themselves indispensable. They called themselves nothing. They had no charter, no elected offices, no written rules. They were simply *the ones who handled things*, and if the citizens of Perdition Gulch understood — in the unspoken, sidelong way that frontier people understood all things too dangerous to name — that the handling involved methods beyond the natural order, well. Faith was faith, whether you directed it at the Almighty or the woman who could tell you the precise contents of a letter she'd glimpsed through a window three years gone.

The woman's name was Constance Bellweather, and on the morning the world began its long unraveling, she forgot the color of her mother's eyes.

---

It happened between one breath and the next. She was standing behind the counter of her store, measuring out a yard of cotton drill for Harlan McPhee's wife, and the bolt's pattern — blue and white windowpane check — sent her mind back, as everything did, to a morning in the kitchen of her parents' house in Nacogdoches, her mother lifting a blue-checked cloth from a basket of bread, her face turning toward the window light, her eyes—

Nothing.

Constance's hands went still on the fabric. The shears hung open over the cloth like a jaw caught mid-word.

"Mrs. Bellweather? You alright?"

She could see the kitchen. She could see the bread, the basket, the weave of the cloth, the flour dust suspended in the angled light like a constellation of very small and purposeless stars. She could see her mother's hands — every line, every knuckle, the thin scar along the left thumb where a paring knife had slipped on Christmas Eve, 1849. She could see her mother's face in perfect, annihilating detail: the pores along her nose, the stray hair at her temple, the lips slightly parted in the half-smile she wore when she thought no one was watching.

But the eyes were gone. Not closed, not averted. *Gone.* Smooth skin where they should have been, like a portrait some artist had left unfinished.

"Mrs. Bellweather?"

"I'm fine," Constance said. Her voice was steady because she had spent forty-seven years on this earth and had learned that the voice must always be steady, especially when the ground has just opened beneath you. "I'm perfectly fine, Mrs. McPhee. Three yards, wasn't it?"

"One yard. For the baby's—"

"One yard. Of course."

She cut the fabric. She folded it. She wrapped it in brown paper and tied it with string and made the correct change from the till and all the while her mind was racing backward through the vast, crystalline archive that had been her life's constant companion, her burden, her gift — every image she had ever seen stored with the fidelity of a daguerreotype, accessible in an instant — and she was finding, here and there, like moth holes in a garment she'd thought impervious, *gaps*.

The face of the minister who'd married her parents. Gone.

The pattern of frost on the windowpane of the schoolhouse in Waco, February 1856. Gone.

The words on the third page of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, which she had read once at a clerk's desk in San Antonio while waiting for a land deed, and which she had carried in her mind ever since, complete in every article, every subclause. She reached for the third page and found only the faintest impression, like a watermark fading in strong light.

Small things. Distant things. Things no one would think to ask about, things that served no practical purpose.

But she had never lost anything. Not once. Not ever, in all the years since the gift had announced itself in childhood — the sudden, terrifying understanding that she could not forget, that every instant was being etched into her by some process she did not understand and could not stop, that the inside of her head was filling up like a room with no door and the images would only ever accumulate, never fade, never soften, never grant her the mercy of blurring.

Until now.

Constance closed the till. She locked the register. She turned the sign on the door to CLOSED and stood in the empty store for a long time, her hands flat on the counter, breathing.

Then she walked to the back room, where the ledger was, the one she kept not for the business but for herself — the one with dates and names and the meticulous, block-letter notations she had made over sixteen years, logging every application of every gift, every quiet intervention, every time one of the five had used what they were to bend the trajectory of the town away from ruin.

She opened it to the most recent page. She read the last entry:

*September 14, 1887. E.L. reported hostile intent from Braddock surveyors — three men thinking in terms of forced easement, possible arson. S.D. rode out. Altercation resolved. S.D. sustained knife wound to left forearm, healed by morning. R. & M.C. confirmed via observation from both north and south approaches that no further party en route.*

She closed the ledger.

She thought about the color of her mother's eyes, and it would not come.

## II.

**Then — June 1871**

They found each other the way all unlikely things converge: by accident, by necessity, and by the mute recognition of one impossible creature encountering another.

Constance was the first to arrive in the place that was not yet Perdition Gulch but merely a handful of structures — a saloon, a livery, a general store with ambitions, a church with none — clinging to the banks of a river that was already dying. She came west because Nacogdoches had become intolerable. She remembered too much. She remembered the face of every man who had marched away in gray and not come back, and she remembered the face of every widow who had come to her because word had gotten around, quietly, the way dangerous words do, that Constance Bellweather could look at a dead soldier's personal effects once and tell you everything — the handwriting, the sketches, the half-finished letters — as if the objects were still in front of her. She had become an archive of grief. The widows came and she opened the vaults of her mind and gave them back their husbands' last words, and each time, a piece of her capacity for ordinary feeling was displaced by the sheer *mass* of accumulated sorrow.
