# The Last Watch of the *Mara Celeste*

---

The tea went cold. I want to say that first because people will ask about the storm, about the wind, about were we afraid, and I will not know how to answer any of that. But I know the tea went cold. I brought it up at six bells in a thermos I'd rubber-banded to my wrist so I wouldn't lose it on the ladder, and I poured it into her cup—the white one, chipped on the lip where she'd knocked it against the binnacle two summers back—and she said *thank you, Ji-yeon* the way she always said it, like I'd done something miraculous instead of just boiled water. And then she didn't drink it. She wrapped both hands around it the way you hold a living thing you're trying to keep warm, and she watched the glass, and the tea went cold, and I stood there behind her in the wheelhouse and I didn't say anything because there was nothing in my whole life I knew how to say.

That was Tuesday. The second day.

---

I should tell you about the boat.

The *Mara Celeste* was a sixty-two-foot wooden ketch, built in 1971 in a boatyard in Sokcho by a man named Wen Dae-jung, who everyone called the Carpenter, though he was also a fisherman, a drinker, a father, a terrible singer, and—according to his granddaughter—the only person in the family who understood that a boat is not a thing you own but a thing that owns you back. He built her from cedar and teak. He steamed her ribs himself. The story goes that he bent each plank while singing to it, and that this was why she rode so well in heavy weather—she'd been sung into her shape, and shape remembers.

Captain Wen told me this the day she hired me. I was twenty-three. I had a culinary certificate from a community college in Incheon and exactly one season on a charter yacht where the owner's wife had fired me for making bibimbap that was, quote, *too authentic*. Captain Wen tasted my doenjang-jjigae from a thermos on the dock in Hamilton and she said, *This is good. Can you make it in a Force 8?* And I said I didn't know what a Force 8 was. And she said, *You'll learn.*

That was four years ago.

---

The letters started in March.

I know because I sort the mail when we're in port. That's not a cook's job, but nothing on a three-person boat is exactly anyone's job—you just do what's in front of you. Danny took the mail sack up from the harbor office, and I sat on the engine cover in the companionway and sorted it into piles. Danny's pile was always the biggest: postcards from his mother, gear catalogs, the odd love letter from one or another of the women he kept carefully apportioned across four Caribbean ports. My pile was small. Captain Wen's pile was brown envelopes.

I didn't open them. But I could see the return addresses. The bank. The second bank. The maritime lender whose name I can't remember but whose logo was an anchor crossed with a key, which even then struck me as ominous—as if they were telling you, right there on the letterhead, that they could lock you to something and throw you in the sea.

Wen read them in her cabin with the door shut. She came out the same way she went in: level, quiet, her face arranged in that particular expression she wore like a bulkhead—weight-bearing, load-distributing, showing nothing of the structure behind it.

But she stopped smiling. I want to be precise about this. She hadn't been a woman who smiled often—she wasn't grim, just economical, the way some people are with their faces the way others are with money. She'd smile when the jib set perfectly and the *Celeste* heeled into her groove. She'd smile at the first hand of cards each morning. She'd smile sometimes at nothing I could see, looking out at the water at dusk, and I always thought she was smiling at her grandfather, or at the ghost of him that lived in the grain of the wood.

After March, she stopped. That's all. It simply ceased, like a instrument quietly removed from an orchestra. You couldn't identify the silence exactly. You just knew the music had thinned.

---

We played rummy.

Every morning at 0700, in the galley, before I started breakfast. This was law. This was more inviolable than watch schedules or right-of-way or the law of the sea itself. She drank coffee, black. I drank whatever tea I'd brought from the last port. Danny, if he wasn't on watch, slept through it, because Danny understood in his bones that some rituals are not for him.

She kept a running tally in a waterproof notebook. Four years of tallies. She was ahead by 847 points, which she attributed to skill. I was certain it was because she could read my face like a tide chart, but I never said so, because the only thing Captain Wen loved more than winning was believing she'd won fairly.

On Monday—the first day of the storm—we played at 0700 as always. The barometer had been dropping all night. The sea had that greasy, sullen quality it gets before something truly terrible, like an animal pretending to sleep. The NAVTEX had been printing warnings since midnight: TROPICAL CYCLONE SEVERE. ALL VESSELS SEEK HARBOR.

Wen laid down three queens and said, *Gin.*

I said, *That's not how rummy works.*

She said, *It is when you're losing.*

I think about this constantly now. How normal Tuesday morning was and how we just sat there in the galley with the cards and the coffee and the little notebook, while somewhere out beyond the horizon the ocean was gathering itself into a fist. How she shuffled the deck with that precise, mechanical motion—bridge, riffle, cut—and the hull creaked the way it always creaked, and the coffee was the same coffee, and neither of us said the word *storm* because the morning card game existed outside of weather, outside of debt, outside of everything. It was seven o'clock. We were playing rummy. The world was the size of a galley table.

---

Danny left on Monday afternoon.

I need to say this carefully because Danny was not a coward. Danny was a deckhand from Montserrat who'd crewed on the *Celeste* for two years, who could reef a sail in the dark by feel, who once dove under the hull in Martinique to free a fouled propeller and came up laughing with a piece of fishing net wrapped around his head like a crown. Danny was brave the way people are brave who don't think about it, who simply do the next thing in front of them without first consulting their fear.

But Danny also had a three-year-old daughter in Plymouth, and when the Coast Guard cutter came alongside Monday afternoon and said they were conducting evacuations, and Captain Wen said she was staying, Danny looked at her and then at me and then at the sea, and I could see him doing the math that no one should ever have to do. The weight of loyalty on one side. The weight of a three-year-old on the other.

Wen saw it before he spoke. She said, *Go, Danny.* She said it the way she said everything: like a navigational fact. Not north or south, not right or wrong. Just: the direction you need to travel.

He hugged her. I'd never seen him do that before. She stood very still inside it, the way she stood inside weather—absorbing it, enduring it, her hands at her sides.

He hugged me too, and whispered, *Get her off this boat, Ji-yeon.*

I said I would. That was a lie, and we both knew it, and he left anyway, because what else was there to do.

---

The Coast Guard lieutenant was a young woman with a jaw like a carpenter's square and the particular exasperated professionalism of someone who has been trained to save people who do not wish to be saved. She stood in the wheelhouse dripping on the chart table and said, *Captain, I am strongly advising you to evacuate.*

Wen said, *Noted.*

*Ma'am, this is a Category 3 system and it is not done intensifying.*

*Noted.*

*Your vessel is—* the lieutenant glanced around the wheelhouse, at the brass fittings green with patina, the compass that Wen had to tap each morning to unstick, the VHF radio held together with electrical tape and faith—*your vessel is not rated for these conditions.*

*She's rated for whatever I rate her for,* Wen said. *She's my boat.*

The lieutenant looked at me. I was standing in the companionway holding a pot of soup, because that is what I do when I don't know what to do. I make soup. I have made soup through engine failures, through a grounding in the Bahamas, through the afternoon Danny accidentally set the jib on fire. Soup is my answer to the unanswerable.

*And you, ma'am?* the lieutenant said. *Are you also electing to remain?*

I looked at Wen. Wen did not look at me. She was watching the barometer, her fingers resting on the glass the way you rest your hand on someone's chest when you're checking if they're still breathing.

*The captain hasn't had lunch yet,* I said.

The lieutenant stared at me for a long moment. Then she said, *I'm logging this as a voluntary refusal of assistance,* and she left, and the cutter pulled away, and we were alone with sixty-two feet of cedar and teak and a dead man's singing.

---

Why did I stay?

People will ask this. I've been asking it myself in the way you press a bruise—compulsively, knowing it will hurt, unable to stop.

I could say loyalty. I could say duty. I could say I was young and stupid and didn't understand what a Category 3 typhoon would do to a wooden ketch in open water. All of these are true and none of them are the truth.

The truth is that I had been making Captain Wen's meals for four years. I knew that she ate her rice plain, that she disliked cilantro, that she took her coffee black except in December when she allowed herself cream, like a gift. I knew the way she held her chopsticks—too high up, the way her grandfather had taught her, which is technically wrong but which she performed with such precision that it became its own kind of correct. I knew all of the things you learn about a person by feeding them, which are not the things they tell you with their mouth but the things their mouth tells you without them knowing.

And I knew that if she was going to sit in that wheelhouse and watch the sea come for her grandfather's boat, someone should bring her tea.

That's not heroism. I want to be clear. Heroism is what Danny did—he left, he went back to his daughter, he chose the living over the lost. What I did was something smaller and stupider and more stubborn. I stayed because the tea would go cold, and someone should be there to notice.

---

Tuesday. The second day.

The wind was a living thing by then. I don't mean that metaphorically. I mean that it had moods, phases, a character that shifted from hour to hour. In the morning it was merely violent—a steady, muscular force that heeled us over and held us there, pressed flat against the sea like a hand pinning a moth. By afternoon it had become capricious, gusting and dropping, gusting and dropping, so that the *Celeste* lurched and staggered like a drunk. By evening it was screaming.

I had never heard wind scream before. I'd heard it howl, whistle, moan. But screaming is different. Screaming is personal. It means the wind has found something to be angry about, some particular edge or surface that turns force into voice, and in our case it was the rigging—the stays and shrouds that ran from mast to deck, each one a different thickness, each one tuned to a different pitch by its tension, so that the whole boat became an instrument, a harp strung in the throat of the storm, playing a chord that had no name in any music I knew.

Wen stood at the wheel.

She'd been standing at the wheel since dawn. I brought her food she didn't eat. I brought her tea she didn't drink. I brought her a rain jacket, which she put on without looking at me, shrugging into it with one hand while the other held the wheel, and I zipped it for her because her fingers were too numb, and neither of us acknowledged this, because to acknowledge it would be to acknowledge that we were in the kind of weather where your fingers stop working, and we were not yet ready to acknowledge that.

The pumps were running. The *Celeste* was taking water—not catastrophically, not yet, but steadily, the way a body bleeds from a wound that isn't fatal on its own but will be if you can't stop it. The electric bilge pump ran in cycles: thirty seconds on, two minutes off. I counted. I counted the way you count heartbeats, superstitiously, as if the counting itself keeps the rhythm going.

At 1400 the cycles shortened. Twenty seconds on, ninety seconds off.

At 1600, sixty seconds on, sixty seconds off.

At 1800 the pump ran continuously, and Wen said, without turning from the wheel, *Ji-yeon. Go below and check the seams.*

I went below. The water was ankle-deep in the forward cabin. It was cold, much colder than I expected, and it smelled of cedar—the *Celeste* bleeding her own scent into the sea that was killing her. I put my hand against the hull and I could feel it: a give, a flex, a rhythm that was not the rhythm of the waves but something slower, something structural. The boat was breathing. The boat was tired.

I went back up. I said, *She's taking it at the garboard seam, forward. It's steady but it's not stopping.*

Wen nodded. She didn't ask if it could be fixed, because she knew the answer, and because asking would have been a form of hope, and hope was not what she was doing at that wheel. What she was doing was something else entirely. I didn't have a word for it then. I think the word might be *custody*. She was in custody of her grandfather's boat, and she would remain in custody until there was nothing left to be custodian of.

---

I should tell you about the repo men.

I only learned about them later, from Danny, who learned about them from the harbor master in Hamilton, who told him over rum in a bar called *The Swizzle Inn*, which is a real place with a real sign that says *Swizzle Inn, Swagger Out*, and which seems like the kind of detail I should not include in a story about drowning but which I am including anyway because the world is full of things that don't match.

There were two of them. They worked for the maritime lender—the one with the anchor and the key. They had flown in from New York on the Thursday before the storm with a court order and a diesel mechanic and a plan to change the locks on the *Celeste* and motor her to a broker's dock in St. George's. They had a number, a final number, six months past due, and the number was not large—not by the standards of the people who sent them, who repossessed oil tankers and mega-yachts, who thought in units of millions the way Wen thought in units of nautical miles. For them, the *Celeste* was a line item. An annoyance. A sixty-two-foot wooden problem that was not worth the fuel it would take to motor her to auction.

But they had come anyway, because that is what numbers do when they get large enough: they send men.

Wen left Hamilton on Wednesday. The storm was a tropical depression east of Barbuda—five days out, maybe six, the models still uncertain. The repo men were due Friday.

She sailed east. Into the Atlantic. Into the Sargasso, where the currents die and the weed gathers and the water is so clear
