# THE SUTTON COUNTY TRANSCRIPTS

---

**EDITOR'S NOTE:**

*The following materials were recovered from a storage unit in Sutton County, Virginia, in October 2019, when the unit's lease payments ceased. The unit was registered to a Margaret Elaine Holloway, born 1951, a certified court reporter employed by the Sutton County Circuit Court from 1979 until her disappearance in January 1989. The materials consist of official court transcripts bearing extensive handwritten marginalia, personal audio recordings on standard-format cassette tapes, and miscellaneous documents including personnel records obtained through unclear means. They have been arranged here in what appears to be their intended chronological sequence. All materials pertaining to* Commonwealth of Virginia v. Eleanor Ruth Vance *(Case No. 87-CR-00441) remain technically sealed by judicial order. Several attempts to have this order revisited have been denied without comment.*

*Margaret Holloway has never been found.*

## PART ONE: THE RECORD

---

**AUDIO DIARY — TAPE 1, SIDE A**

**Recorded June 3, 1987**

**[Tape hiss. Sound of a glass being set down. A woman clears her throat. Her voice is measured, clear — the voice of someone accustomed to precision.]**

This is Margaret Holloway, and I'm starting these recordings because Dr. Perrin suggested a verbal journal might help with what he calls my "processing difficulties." His words. What he means is that I've been bringing the transcripts home in my head and he'd like me to leave them at the courthouse.

Fine. So I'll leave them here instead. On tape.

Today was jury selection for the Vance case. I should note for my own reference that I didn't ask for this assignment. Carol Meecham was scheduled — she does all of Judge Alderman's felony cases — but she called in Monday with what she described as a family emergency and I was reassigned. I mention this because I want it noted, somewhere, that I did not seek this out.

Eleanor Vance. Forty-one years old. Licensed clinical social worker, employed by Sutton County Department of Child Protective Services for sixteen years. Charged with three counts of first-degree murder in the deaths of:

Michael Lassiter, age seven.
Danielle Pruitt, age four.
Thomas Kinney, age nine.

All three were foster children in homes where Vance served as the assigned caseworker. All three died between March 1985 and October 1986. The prosecution's theory is that Vance — influenced by what they call "satanic ritual abuse hysteria" — became convinced these children were being abused in occult ceremonies by their foster families, and that when the system failed to act on her reports, she killed them herself. To save them, is the implication. The Commonwealth's Attorney used the phrase "mercy killings rooted in delusion" in his opening statement during voir dire.

The defense says she didn't do it. That the children's deaths are attributable to the foster families themselves, that Vance's reports of ritualistic abuse were accurate, and that she is being prosecuted because the county failed to protect three children and needs someone to absorb the blame who isn't the county.

The gallery was full today. There are people with signs outside. One camp says FREE ELEANOR. The other camp says DEVIL WORSHIPPER. I don't think either group has read the actual case file.

I have. All nine hundred and twelve pages.

I don't have an opinion. That's not my job. My job is the record.

**[Pause. Ice clinking in a glass.]**

Jury selection will take through the week. Alderman is being thorough. He wants people who haven't watched the news, which in Sutton County in 1987 means he wants people who've been living under a rock. The Vance case has been on television for eight months.

I'll note one thing, though. Just for the tape. When they brought Eleanor Vance in today, she was — she's a small woman, if you haven't seen the news footage. Short, thin, early gray in her hair, glasses. She looks like a librarian. She looked at the gallery, and she looked at the prosecution table, and then she looked at me. Directly at my station. Most defendants don't notice the stenographer. We are by design the least noticeable people in the room.

She noticed me.

Dr. Perrin would tell me that's not significant. I'm noting it anyway.

**[Click.]**

The defendant, as Michael's caseworker, had unlimited access to the Lassiter home. In the weeks before Michael's death, she filed three reports alleging that the Lassiters were involved in — and I'm quoting from her own reports — "organized ritualistic abuse of a satanic nature." These reports were investigated and deemed unfounded. The defendant was instructed to cease making unfounded allegations. One week later, Michael was dead.

THE COURT: Mr. Argento, your opening statement.

MR. ARGENTO: Thank you, Your Honor. Members of the jury, my colleague Mr. Haines has just told you a story. It's a compelling story. It's neat and it's tidy and it has a clear villain and three small victims and it will make you feel, at the end, like justice has a shape you can recognize. That's what good stories do.

But I'm not here to tell you a story. I'm here to show you a mess. Because the truth in this case is a mess, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

Eleanor Vance did not kill these children. She tried to save them. Not in the abstract, deluded sense Mr. Haines wants you to believe — not through some misguided act of "release." She tried to save them through the system. She filed reports. She followed protocol. She escalated. And the system — this county's own child protective system — failed. Not once. Not twice. Three times.

**[STENOGRAPHER'S MARGINAL NOTE, blue ink: Argento is also competent. He's leaning into the mess. Smart in a different way — juries distrust neatness.]**

MR. ARGENTO: Now — I know what you're thinking. The satanic abuse allegations. The ritual claims. You're thinking: that's where she lost credibility. That's where she went off the rails. And I won't insult your intelligence by pretending those reports don't sound — unusual. They do. 

But I will ask you to consider two things. First: Eleanor Vance was not the only person making these claims. In 1985 and 1986, similar allegations surfaced in cases across the country — the McMartin preschool case in California, the Fells Acres case in Massachusetts, dozens of others. There was a national conversation happening, and Eleanor Vance was part of it. She may have been wrong about the specific rituals. She was not wrong that these children were being hurt.

Second: the physical evidence. The forensic evidence. Mr. Haines says it links Eleanor to the scenes. What it actually shows — and we will go through it piece by piece — is that Eleanor visited these homes as part of her job. Her fingerprints are there because she was there. Legally. Professionally. Repeatedly. The prosecution's forensic case cannot distinguish between a caseworker's routine visit and a crime.

**[STENOGRAPHER'S MARGINAL NOTE, blue ink: The defendant has stopped watching me. She is watching the jury. Her expression is — I don't know how to describe it. Patient. Like someone waiting for a bus she knows is coming.]**

MR. ARGENTO: What the prosecution does not have — and listen for this, because it matters — is a single eyewitness. A single confession. A single piece of physical evidence that places Eleanor Vance at any of these locations at the time of death. What they have is proximity and a theory. The theory is that she was crazy, and crazy people do crazy things. That's not evidence. That's a story.
